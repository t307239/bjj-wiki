The Clock Choke is a powerful submission initiated from closed guard, aiming to constrict blood flow to the brain. It's a fundamental choke that can end a match quickly when applied correctly.

Many white belts struggle due to an overreliance on brute strength rather than precise body mechanics. This leads to inefficient technique and potential injury, as they don't understand the leverage points.

The key to the Clock Choke lies in isolating one arm and using your legs and hips to create a precise angle, effectively turning their neck into a vice. It's about leverage, not power.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, establish a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand on their left lapel, thumb inside. Your left hand grips their left sleeve near the wrist.
    2. Immediately begin to shift your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's torso. Your weight should be on your left hip.
    3. Bring your left knee across their chest, pinning their left arm to their body. Keep your left shin perpendicular to their spine.
    4. With your right hand, reach across your opponent's body and secure a deep grip on their right lapel, thumb inside, aiming for the fabric near their sternum.
    5. Drive your left hip into their left shoulder, creating pressure and preventing them from posturing up. Your right leg slides down their back, hooking their hip.
    6. Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, pulling your opponent's head and trapped arm towards your chest. Your right leg acts as a lever, pushing their body away slightly.
    7. Tighten the choke by squeezing your knees together and driving your hips forward. Your right forearm should press into their carotid artery, while your left forearm supports their head.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Attempting to force the choke by pulling your opponent's head down with your arms can strain your shoulder or elbow ligaments. The correct action is to use your hip and leg drive to create the angle, not arm strength.
    • Over-extending your neck or twisting your spine unnaturally when applying the choke can lead to cervical disc injury. Maintain a neutral spine and focus on rotating your hips and torso.
    • Gripping too high or too low on the lapel can prevent the choke from locking. Ensure your right hand grip is deep on the lapel, near their sternum, for maximum pressure on the carotid artery.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip movement, leg placement, and grip transitions without a partner for 5 minutes (0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Execute the entire Clock Choke sequence with a compliant partner. Focus on each step's precision. 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and hip movement. 5 reps per side (25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance): Your partner attempts to defend by creating space or posturing up. You work on maintaining control and finishing the choke. 3 reps per side (50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Clock Choke during a controlled roll where your partner is allowed to defend but not actively resist to the point of injury. Focus on timing and transition. 5 minutes (75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (sparring): Apply the Clock Choke during live sparring, aiming for the submission. Be aware of your partner's defenses and be ready to transition. 5 minutes (90-100% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is turtled or attempting to escape from your closed guard.
    • When your opponent tries to stack pass by posturing up while you maintain closed guard.
    • When your opponent's arm is extended or caught between your legs during a guard pass attempt.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Defense 1 (Arm Isolation): If they trap your arm, keep your elbow tucked to your body and use your free hand to block their hip from creating the angle.
    • Defense 2 (Hip Escape): If they start to rotate, immediately hip escape away from the direction of their choke to relieve pressure and create space.
    • Defense 3 (Posture Up): If they begin to finish, quickly try to posture up and break their grip on your lapel while simultaneously bringing your trapped arm back to your side.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Clock Choke from closed guard?

    Neck pain usually indicates you are using excessive force to pull your opponent's head down with your arms. The Clock Choke's effectiveness comes from the mechanical advantage created by your hips and legs. Instead of pulling, focus on driving your hips into their shoulder and using your legs to create the rotational pressure, which will naturally bring their head into the choke.

    Q: How can I apply the Clock Choke against a much bigger opponent with closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, precision is even more critical. Focus on isolating one of their arms and controlling their posture with your legs to prevent them from using their weight effectively. Your hip movement to create the 45-degree angle and the subsequent leg drive become paramount. Ensure your grips are deep and secure on the lapels, and don't try to muscle the submission; let the leverage do the work.

    Q: When should I specifically switch from a standard cross-collar choke to a Clock Choke in closed guard?

    You should consider switching to the Clock Choke when your opponent consistently defends the standard cross-collar choke by posturing up or pulling their head away to create space. The Clock Choke excels when you can isolate an arm and use that as an anchor to create the necessary angle and pressure, which is often easier to achieve when they are trying to regain guard or posture up.
