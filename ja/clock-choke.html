Clock Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanically Accurate Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Clock Choke is applied from the closed guard, aiming to submit the opponent by compressing their carotid arteries.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise body control and an instinct to simply pull harder.

Success hinges on creating leverage with your hips and achieving a specific shoulder angle, not brute force.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, establish your dominant arm's grip on the opponent's collar, inserting your knuckles deep into the fabric on the opposite side of their neck (e.g., right hand grips opponent's left collar).
    2. Your secondary arm grips the opponent's same-side sleeve (e.g., left hand grips opponent's left sleeve) at the wrist or forearm.
    3. Sit up slightly, tilting your hips to your dominant side (e.g., right hip drops slightly). This creates a "clock" angle around the opponent's head.
    4. Drive your chest towards the opponent's shoulder on the side of your collar grip, creating forward pressure.
    5. Simultaneously, pull your secondary arm (sleeve grip) across the opponent's body, drawing their arm towards your chest.
    6. Rotate your hips and torso, turning your back slightly towards the opponent's head, while maintaining the collar grip and sleeve pull.
    7. Extend your legs slightly, maintaining hip pressure, and use your dominant arm's grip to "slice" the opponent's neck against their own shoulder.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly gripping the collar too shallowly and pulling straight down can strain the rotator cuff in your shoulder, leading to impingement injuries.

    The correct grip involves deep knuckle insertion, and the primary motion is a slicing action across the neck, not just pulling.

    • Attempting to force the choke by arching your back excessively instead of using hip rotation can cause lumbar spine strain and injury.

    Focus on rotating your hips and using your chest pressure to create the choke, keeping your spine neutral.

    • Gripping the sleeve too loosely and allowing the opponent to easily escape their arm can lead to a sudden loss of control and potential wrist hyperextension if you're still pulling.

    Maintain a firm sleeve grip and use it to control the opponent's posture and arm movement to facilitate the choke.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip sequence and hip rotation motion without a partner (10 reps per side).
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice establishing grips and performing the hip tilt and body rotation, focusing on the correct angles (10 reps per side).
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner attempts to resist slightly by not giving you the collar easily, but still allows you to work the mechanics of the choke setup (10 reps per side).
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner actively defends by posturing up or trying to create space, forcing you to adjust your hip angle and body positioning to maintain the setup (10 reps per side).
    5. Partner drilling (75% resistance): Partner attempts to escape the guard and defend the choke more actively, requiring you to execute the steps with precision and timing (5 reps per side).
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the Clock Choke during rolling, focusing on applying the mechanics learned, but without the pressure of a full submission attempt from the opponent (focus on setting up the choke, 3 attempts per round).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent's posture is broken and they are leaning forward within your closed guard.
    • When the opponent attempts to stack you, creating an opportunity to trap their head and arm.
    • When you have established a strong collar grip and can control the opponent's opposite arm.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posture Up and Grip Break: The opponent can immediately posture up, widening the space between your hips and their head, and simultaneously break your collar grip with their free hand.
    • Elbow Escape: The opponent can drive their elbow into your hip on the side of your secondary arm (sleeve grip) and attempt to create space to escape the guard.
    • Shoulder Pressure and Hip Escape: The opponent can apply heavy shoulder pressure into your chest and attempt to walk their hips away, creating an angle to relieve pressure on their neck.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Clock Choke from closed guard?

    Neck pain often occurs if you are not achieving the correct angle and are instead applying direct downward pressure on the trachea. Ensure your dominant arm's knuckles are deep in the collar on the opposite side of the neck, and your secondary arm is controlling the opponent's same-side sleeve. The choke is applied by slicing the opponent's neck against their own shoulder through hip rotation and chest pressure, not by crushing.

    Q: How can I perform the Clock Choke against a significantly larger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paramount. Focus on establishing a very deep collar grip on their far side lapel. Use your hips to create a strong angle, almost perpendicular to their spine, and drive your chest into their shoulder. Your secondary arm's sleeve grip is crucial for preventing them from posturing up and for pulling their arm across to assist in the choke.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Clock Choke, and how do I know if I have the right grips?

    The ideal time is when the opponent's posture is broken and they are leaning into your guard. You want to have their head and one arm relatively controlled. For grips, your dominant hand should feel like it's reaching across their neck to their far shoulder, with your knuckles digging into the thickest part of the collar. Your other hand should have a firm grip on their sleeve, preventing them from using that arm to defend.
