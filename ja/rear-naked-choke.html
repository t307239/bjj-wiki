This technique is applied from the back-mount position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via carotid artery compression. It is a highly effective submission.

White belts often struggle due to incorrect grip placement or insufficient body control, leading to failed attempts or injury risks. Focus on precision over brute force.

The key mechanical insight is creating a stable "frame" with your arms that compresses the opponent's arteries against their own neck structures.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Establish back-mount: Ensure both hooks (your feet) are inside the opponent's thighs, with your hips connected to their hips. Maintain chest-to-back contact.
    2. Secure the choking arm: Insert your right arm (for a right-side choke) under the opponent's chin, placing the bicep against their right carotid artery. Your forearm should be vertical.
    3. Grip the bicep: With your left hand, grip your own right bicep. This stabilizes the choking arm and creates a powerful "frame" against the neck.
    4. Secure the head: Bring your left hand to the back of the opponent's head, cupping it. This prevents them from turning their head away.
    5. "S" the grip: With your left hand gripping your right bicep, begin to "S" your left hand, tucking your fingers deep into your right bicep. This tightens the grip.
    6. Apply pressure: Simultaneously, drive your chest into the opponent's upper back, while slightly tucking your chin. Your hips should remain connected.
    7. Compress the arteries: Slowly and steadily, squeeze your biceps together and drive your chest forward. This compresses both carotid arteries, causing a rapid loss of consciousness.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Applying the choke by "grabbing the shoulder" instead of the bicep: This fails to create a stable frame, allowing the opponent to escape and potentially straining your own shoulder capsule by hyperextending it.
    • Rushing the choke and "pulling hard" with your arms: This can lead to hyperextension of the elbow joint in your choking arm if the opponent resists strongly, risking ligament damage.
    • Allowing the opponent to turn their head: This prevents proper carotid compression and can lead to a "windpipe choke" if you apply pressure to the trachea, causing pain and potential airway injury.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the grip sequence and body positioning on a dummy or the floor. Focus on the "S" grip and arm frame. 20 reps.
    2. Partner drilling - static (25% resistance): With a compliant partner in back-mount, practice the entire sequence without resistance. Focus on smooth transitions and grip security. 10 reps each side.
    3. Partner drilling - slow motion (50% resistance): Execute the choke with a partner who offers slight, predictable resistance. Focus on maintaining your frame and body connection. 5 reps each side.
    4. Partner drilling - controlled resistance (75% resistance): The partner attempts to defend minimally. You practice applying the choke with controlled pressure, aiming for a tap. 5 reps each side.
    5. Live rolling - focus on position (90% resistance): During rolling, if you achieve back-mount, prioritize securing the choke. Your partner will defend actively.
    6. Live rolling - full application (100% resistance): Apply the Rear Naked Choke whenever the opportunity arises during live rolling. Focus on proper technique and recognizing submission opportunities.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have successfully secured back-mount with both hooks in and chest-to-back contact.
    • After a successful takedown where you immediately transition to the back.
    • When your opponent is turtled and you can secure hooks from behind.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Turning the corner": The opponent turns their head into the choking arm, breaking the direct carotid compression. The correct response is to adjust your grip and "S" your hand deeper, or switch to a body triangle.
    • "Defending the neck with the arm": The opponent tucks their chin and places their arm across their throat. You must "S" your hand deeper, bringing your forearm higher on the carotid, or use your secondary hand to push their defending arm away.
    • "Escaping the hooks": The opponent "humps" or rolls to relieve pressure and escape your hooks. You must maintain hip connection and re-secure your hooks, or transition to a body triangle.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Rear Naked Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you grip your bicep too passively or allow your forearm to bend excessively. Instead, focus on creating a rigid frame by keeping your choking forearm vertical and actively "S"ing your gripping hand deep into your bicep. This transfers pressure to your forearm and bicep, not just your wrist.

    Q: How can I do the Rear Naked Choke against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body mechanics are crucial. Ensure your hooks are deep and your chest is firmly against their back to prevent them from creating space. When applying the choke, "S" your grip as deeply as possible and use your entire body weight by driving your chest forward, not just pulling with your arms. Focus on compressing the carotid artery against their neck.

    Q: Is the Rear Naked Choke considered a dangerous submission for white belts to practice?

    The Rear Naked Choke itself is not inherently dangerous if practiced with proper technique and control. The danger for white belts arises from incorrect application, such as applying pressure to the windpipe or hyperextending joints. Always practice with a compliant partner, focus on the mechanics of carotid compression, and tap early if you feel discomfort.
