Mastering the Half Guard in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

Half Guard is a foundational BJJ position where one opponent has one leg between their opponent's legs, while their own legs are not fully entangled. It is typically entered from a bottom position when the opponent attempts a guard pass.

White belts often struggle by presenting a flat back or allowing their hips to be driven forward, losing the crucial leverage needed. They may also over-rely on gripping without understanding the underlying body mechanics.

The core principle of Half Guard is to create a strong 'hook' with the trapped leg and use your hips to create frames and angles, preventing the opponent from establishing dominant control and enabling sweeps or transitions.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a bottom position, as the opponent initiates a guard pass (e.g., knee slice), immediately bring your right knee towards your chest.
    2. Simultaneously, extend your left leg to block the opponent's right thigh, creating a barrier and preventing them from advancing their knee.
    3. Secure a deep grip on the opponent's right ankle with your right hand, palm facing inward, thumb wrapped around the ankle bone.
    4. With your left hand, establish a grip on the opponent's right hip or belt, approximately 45 degrees from their center line.
    5. Drive your hips back and to your left, creating an angle. Your left knee should be pointing towards your opponent's left shoulder.
    6. Maintain tension by pulling the ankle grip towards your chest while pushing your hips away. This creates a 'frame' with your legs.
    7. Use your left hand on the hip to control their base and prevent them from straightening their posture. Keep your head off the mat, looking towards your opponent.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Attempting to 'pull' yourself back into guard by arching your lower back excessively: This can lead to lumbar spine strain or disc injury. The correct movement is to drive hips back while maintaining a stable base and leg frames.
    • Allowing the opponent to flatten your guard by allowing their weight to drive directly forward onto your chest: This can put undue pressure on the sternum and ribs, potentially causing fractures. Instead, create an angle and use your knee as a barrier.
    • Gripping the opponent's foot or shin too high up the leg without securing the ankle: This often leads to the opponent easily stepping over your leg, risking a knee hyperextension or ligament damage. Always aim for the ankle bone with a deep, secure grip.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the leg positioning and hip movement of entering Half Guard from a lying position, focusing on creating the 'hook' and hip angle. 10 repetitions each side.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice securing the Half Guard position from a seated position. Focus on grip placement and hip angle. 5 repetitions each side.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner attempts to flatten your guard. You focus on maintaining the leg frames and hip angle, preventing them from advancing. 5 repetitions each side, 25% resistance.
    4. Transition drill: From Half Guard, practice transitioning to a sweep (e.g., scissor sweep) or a pass prevention. Partner offers minimal resistance. 3 repetitions each side, 50% resistance.
    5. Positional sparring: Start from Half Guard and attempt to establish control or execute a sweep. Partner defends passively. 5 minutes, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Integrate Half Guard into your rolling rounds, focusing on maintaining the position and looking for offensive opportunities. 5 minutes, 90-100% resistance.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent attempts to pass your guard by driving their knee across your body (e.g., knee slice pass).
    • When you are on your back and your opponent is in your guard, but you cannot maintain a closed guard or open guard structure.
    • When you are in the process of transitioning from another guard and the opponent establishes a dominant passing posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Creating an Angle: Immediately drive your hips back and to the side, creating a 45-degree angle. This prevents the opponent from flattening you and allows you to use your legs as frames.
    • The 'Hook': Secure a deep grip on the opponent's ankle with your 'inside' leg (the leg that is trapped). This hook prevents them from easily stepping over your leg and provides a lever for sweeps.
    • Hip Escape and Frame: Use your 'outside' leg to push against the opponent's hip or thigh, creating space and preventing them from driving their weight forward. Your free hand can also frame on their hip or shoulder.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my hip hurt when I try to get into Half Guard?

    Your hip may be hurting due to excessive internal rotation or a lack of proper framing. When entering Half Guard, avoid forcing your trapped leg's knee inwards towards your chest; instead, focus on driving your hips back and to the side to create an angle. Ensure your 'outside' leg is actively pushing against your opponent's hip to create a frame, preventing them from driving their weight directly onto your hip joint.

    Q: How do I maintain Half Guard against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary focus must be on maintaining your frames and angles, not on trying to muscle them. Use your trapped leg's knee as a barrier to prevent their chest from flattening you. Actively push their hip away with your outside leg to create space and prevent them from establishing side control. Secure a deep grip on their ankle to control their base and limit their mobility.

    Q: For white belts, it is generally safer and more biomechanically sound to keep your 'inside' foot (the one trapping their leg) firmly on the mat, creating a stable base. Your 'outside' foot should be actively used to frame against their hip or thigh. Tucking your feet too tightly can lead to knee or ankle injuries if not executed with precise control, and can also make it harder to create necessary angles.
