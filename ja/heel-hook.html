Heel Hook BJJ: A White Belt's Precise Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 茶帯昇格要件

The Heel Hook is a leg lock that attacks the knee joint's rotational stability, typically applied from positions like the 50-50 guard or inside position.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of control and a tendency to apply excessive, uncontrolled force, leading to injury.

Mastering the Heel Hook requires isolating the opponent's heel and precisely rotating their leg to create torque on the knee's ligaments.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Entry from 50-50 Guard: Secure both of your opponent's legs within your own legs, ensuring your hips are aligned with their hips. Your right leg should be over their right leg, and your left leg under.
    2. Grip Acquisition: Place your right arm under their right thigh, securing a "S-grip" (palm facing you, thumb up) on the back of their thigh, just above the knee.
    3. Secondary Grip: Reach your left hand and secure a "Gogoplata grip" on your right shin, interlacing your fingers. This locks your legs together and prevents them from escaping.
    4. Hip Extension: Drive your hips forward and slightly to your left, creating a strong fulcrum point with your own body.
    5. Heel Isolation: Use your left leg (the one under their leg) to "cup" their heel, preventing it from rotating away from you.
    6. Torque Application: Maintain a stable base with your left foot flat on the mat. Begin to extend your hips and slightly pull your chest towards your knees, while simultaneously "cupping" their heel with your left leg and pushing their knee towards the mat with your hips.
    7. Finishing Pressure: The finish occurs when you create a rotational force on their knee by extending your hips and driving their heel away from their body, while their knee is anchored by your body and left leg.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Hip Alignment: Attempting to "crank" the heel hook by simply pulling up with your arms without proper hip extension and alignment. This can cause hyperextension or twisting injuries to the opponent's knee ligaments (ACL, MCL).
    • Slipping Grip on the Heel: Allowing the opponent's heel to slip from your "cupping" grip with your left leg, leading to uncontrolled rotation. This can result in a "knee-on-belly" style knee injury as the leg twists awkwardly.
    • Over-Rotation: Applying excessive rotational force without controlling the opponent's body, causing their knee to buckle inwards or outwards beyond its natural range of motion, leading to severe ligament tears.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Extension Drill: Practice the hip extension and driving motion without a partner. Focus on feeling the correct pelvic tilt and leg tension. 20 reps.
    2. Partnered Grip and Isolation (0% Resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice securing the 50-50 guard and achieving the correct grips. Partner remains completely passive. 5 sets of 3 reps each side.
    3. Controlled Leg Placement (25% Resistance): Partner actively resists leg entry but does not pull away. Focus on maintaining control of the heel and hip alignment. 5 sets of 3 reps each side.
    4. Controlled Leg Extension (50% Resistance): Partner maintains a passive 50-50 position. You practice the controlled hip extension and pulling motion, stopping just before submission pressure. Partner provides verbal "tap" if uncomfortable. 5 sets of 3 reps each side.
    5. Simulated Submission (75% Resistance): Partner actively tries to defend the heel hook but does not attempt to escape to a dangerous position. You apply controlled, smooth pressure until the tap. 5 sets of 3 reps each side.
    6. Live Rolling (90% Resistance): Apply the Heel Hook during live rolling with a focus on proper mechanics, not just finishing. Tap early if you feel an uncomfortable position or if your partner is applying their own submission safely. 5 minutes rolling, focusing on the entry.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have successfully secured the 50-50 guard and have control of both of your opponent's legs.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and you can isolate one of their legs.
    • When you are in a scramble and can isolate an opponent's heel as they try to establish position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Stand Up and Spin" Defense: If your opponent attempts to secure your heel, immediately drive your weight forward and to the side of the attacking leg, attempting to "run" your heel out. This requires a quick weight shift and hip rotation away from the attacker.
    • The "Knee Shield" Defense: If your opponent has achieved the 50-50, use your free leg to create a "knee shield" by placing your knee between their hips and chest, preventing them from driving their hips forward. This also allows you to potentially "kick" their hips away.
    • The "Stack" Defense: If your opponent is applying the heel hook, attempt to "stack" them by driving your weight directly onto their hips and chest, collapsing their base and forcing them to release the grip. This is best attempted if their hip extension is not yet fully committed.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Heel Hook?

    Your knee might hurt because you are likely not isolating the heel correctly or using your hips to create the rotational torque. Instead, you might be relying on brute force to twist their leg. Ensure your "cupping" grip on their heel is firm with your free leg and your hips are driving forward and to the side to apply the pressure correctly on the knee's rotational axis.

    Q: How can I apply a Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paramount. Focus on securing the 50-50 guard with impeccable hip alignment. Do not try to overpower them. Instead, use your hips to drive forward and create the rotational torque on their heel, isolating their leg with your own. A strong "S-grip" on their thigh is crucial for control.

    Q: As a white belt, the safest and most effective time to attempt a Heel Hook is when you have secured a strong 50-50 guard. This position offers the most control and the least risk of injury to yourself and your opponent compared to attempting it from less stable positions. Practice the entry and control until it becomes a natural transition.
