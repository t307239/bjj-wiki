The heel hook is a submission targeting the knee joint, typically applied from a 50-50 guard or controlling positions. It aims to hyperextend or twist the knee, forcing a tap.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of rotational forces and joint mechanics, leading to uncontrolled movements and potential injury.

The key insight is creating a stable base and using your hips to drive rotation into the opponent's heel, isolating and stressing the knee ligaments.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Entry: From a controlling position (e.g., inside control of the opponent's leg), secure a "C-grip" on the opponent's ankle with your non-attacking hand, thumb pointing upwards. Your attacking leg will wrap around the opponent's thigh.
    2. Hip Angle: Angle your hips so your torso is perpendicular to the opponent's leg line. This creates leverage.
    3. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your hips, pressing them towards the opponent's hip to prevent them from turning out.
    4. Hook Grip: With your attacking leg, "hook" your shin and foot behind the opponent's heel, ensuring your ankle is flush against their heel.
    5. Arm Position: Your attacking arm secures a "figure-four" grip around the opponent's ankle, with your bicep against their Achilles tendon and your forearm across the top of their foot. Your non-attacking hand cups the opponent's heel, thumb up.
    6. Knee Drive: Keep your attacking knee pointed slightly upwards, away from the opponent's body, to avoid striking it.
    7. Submission Drive: Drive your hips forward and slightly to the side, creating a rotational force that twists the opponent's knee. Your shoulders should be perpendicular to the opponent's leg.
    8. Finishing: Maintain constant pressure and continue driving your hips forward until the tap. Do not "pull" hard; the power comes from the hip drive and rotational torque.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Hip Drive: Applying pressure by lifting hips vertically instead of driving them forward and rotating. This can lead to hyperextension of the attacker's own knee or ineffective pressure on the opponent's knee.
    • Loose Grip: Not securing a "figure-four" grip on the opponent's ankle, allowing their foot to slip. This results in a loss of control and can cause the attacker's wrist or elbow to be twisted if the opponent counters.
    • Knee Collision: Allowing your attacking knee to buckle or point directly at the opponent's knee. This can cause a direct collision, potentially injuring both practitioners' knees.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Visualization (0%): Mentally walk through the steps of the heel hook entry and finish, focusing on hip angles and grip placement. (5 minutes)
    2. Static Grip Drills (0%): Partner lies on their back. You enter the heel hook position, securing the "figure-four" grip and "C-grip" without any resistance. Focus on precise hand and foot placement. (5 reps each side, 10 total)
    3. Controlled Entry Drill (25%): Partner maintains a neutral leg position. You practice entering the heel hook, securing the grips and hip angle. Partner offers minimal resistance to hip escape. (5 reps each side, 10 total)
    4. Hip Drive Isolation (50%): With grips secured, practice the hip drive motion, focusing on driving your hips forward and rotating. Partner resists slightly by keeping their hips heavy. (5 reps each side, 10 total)
    5. Controlled Submission Drill (75%): Partner allows you to apply the heel hook, tapping when they feel significant pressure. You focus on smooth transitions and controlling the pressure. (5 reps each side, 10 total)
    6. Live Rolling (90%): During rolling, actively look for opportunities to enter the heel hook from the appropriate positions. Your partner will actively defend and counter, but the goal is practice, not immediate submission.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's leg is extended and accessible from your guard or a scrambling situation.
    • When you have established control of the opponent's leg in a 50-50 guard or similar leg entanglement.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard by stepping over your legs, creating an opportunity to attack their heel.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape the Angle: If the opponent is attacking your heel, immediately try to turn your hips away from their hip drive, making it harder for them to generate torque. This involves a slight "bridging" motion away from the pressure.
    • Straighten the Leg: If possible, try to extend your leg outwards, away from the attacker's body. This reduces the flexion at the knee joint and makes the heel hook less effective.
    • Switch to a Guard Pass: If the opponent commits to the heel hook, sometimes the best defense is to transition to a guard pass. For example, if they have your leg, you might try to step over their body to establish a passing position.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Heel Hook?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are trying to force the submission with upper body strength or by pulling. A heel hook relies on precise hip mechanics to drive rotation into the opponent's knee. Ensure your hips are angled correctly, driving forward, and that you have a secure "figure-four" grip. Avoid any motion that twists your own knee.

    Q: How can I use the Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are even more critical. Focus on controlling their leg's ankle and heel with a tight "figure-four" grip. Use your hips to create a stable base and drive rotational force. Your weight needs to be anchored, preventing them from using their mass to power out of the submission.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Heel Hook in BJJ?

    The most opportune moments to attempt a heel hook are when you have established control of an opponent's leg, such as in the 50-50 guard or from a deep half guard where you can isolate a leg. It's also effective during scrambles or when an opponent is trying to pass your guard, as their weight distribution can create openings.
