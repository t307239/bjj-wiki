Heel Hook BJJ: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 茶帯昇格要件

The Heel Hook is a leg lock that attacks the knee joint by rotating the tibia and fibula internally relative to the femur. White belts often fail due to a lack of control and a tendency to 'muscle' the submission instead of using leverage.

The key mechanical insight is to isolate the opponent's heel and create a fulcrum with your hip to apply rotational torque, rather than a straight pull.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a 50-50 guard position, secure your opponent's outside leg with your legs, creating a figure-four lock around their ankle and calf. Your left leg's shin should be across their upper calf, and your right leg's shin should be behind their ankle.
    2. Grip the opponent's heel with your hands, fingers pointing towards their toes. Your right hand grips the heel from the outside (pinky finger side), and your left hand grips it from the inside (thumb side).
    3. Ensure your hips are elevated off the mat, creating space. Your pelvis should be angled slightly towards your opponent's hips.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly to the side, towards your opponent's hips. This creates a stable base and prevents them from easily escaping.
    5. With your hips driving forward, pull your opponent's heel towards your chest while simultaneously pushing your own chest away from their knee. This action creates the rotational force.
    6. Maintain a strong grip on the heel throughout the movement. Your elbows should be tight to your body to prevent them from being used for leverage.
    7. As you apply the pressure, your opponent's knee will be forced into internal rotation, stressing the ligaments and leading to a tap.
    8. The finishing pressure comes from the internal rotation of the tibia, not from a straight pull or knee pressure on their thigh.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly gripping the foot: Instead of isolating the heel, gripping the entire foot or ankle allows the opponent to twist their leg out, leading to a potential sprain or tear of the ankle ligaments.
    • Pushing with the knee into the opponent's thigh: This can hyperextend the opponent's knee in an uncontrolled manner, risking ACL or MCL tears.
    • Attempting to 'muscle' the lock with arm strength: This ineffective method can lead to shoulder or elbow injuries for the attacker and fails to apply the correct rotational force to the opponent's knee.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and leg configuration with no opponent. Focus on achieving the figure-four lock and heel grip. 50 repetitions.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from the 50-50 guard, focusing on proper grip and hip drive. 25% resistance.
    3. Controlled submission drilling: Apply the Heel Hook with light pressure, allowing your partner to tap early. Focus on the feeling of the lock and the controlled hip movement. 50% resistance, 5 reps each side.
    4. Scenario drilling (specific positions): Start from the 50-50 guard and practice executing the Heel Hook against a partner who is actively defending but not resisting fully. 75% resistance, 3 reps each side.
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Implement the Heel Hook in sparring, but with a strong emphasis on tapping early and respecting your partner's safety. Focus on recognizing opportunities. 90% resistance, 5 minutes.
    6. Full live rolling: Execute the Heel Hook in sparring with full resistance, but maintain a high level of control and awareness of your partner's well-being. 100% resistance, 5 minutes.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard, isolating one of your opponent's legs.
    • When your opponent is trying to pass your guard and their weight is distributed in a way that allows you to control their leg.
    • When your opponent attempts a leg lock on you, and you can successfully transition to a 50-50 position first.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Straightening the leg and creating base: The opponent extends their leg fully and plants their foot firmly on the mat, creating a strong base that resists rotation.
    • Turning the heel inward: The opponent rotates their heel towards their own body, relieving pressure on the knee ligaments and making the lock ineffective.
    • Escaping the 50-50: The opponent uses their free leg to push off your hips or body, creating space to turn their hips and relieve the pressure.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my heel hook feel weak and why can't I get a tap?

    Your heel hook likely feels weak because you are not isolating the heel correctly or generating sufficient rotational torque. Ensure your grip is firmly on the heel itself, not the foot or ankle. The primary force comes from driving your hips forward and creating a fulcrum with your pelvis, then rotating the opponent's foot internally. Avoid simply pulling with your arms.

    Q: How can I effectively apply a heel hook against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paramount. Focus on precise hip control and creating a tight figure-four lock around their leg to prevent them from using their size advantage to power out. Drive your hips forward to create the necessary angle and torque, and ensure your grip on the heel is secure. The biomechanics of rotational force are more effective than brute strength.

    Q: When is the right time to go for a heel hook, and how do I avoid getting swept?

    The ideal time to attempt a heel hook is when you have established a stable 50-50 guard, meaning you have secured control of one of your opponent's legs. To avoid being swept, maintain a strong connection with your hips to their hips and ensure your base is wide and stable. Keep your free leg active to prevent them from creating frames or bridging effectively.
