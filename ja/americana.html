Americana: A White Belt's Biomechanically Accurate Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Americana is a shoulder lock typically applied from side control or mount, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's arm at the shoulder joint.

Many white belts struggle by muscling the submission, ignoring crucial body positioning and leverage.

Success hinges on isolating the opponent's arm and using your hips and shoulder to create a controlled lever against their elbow.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From side control, secure your opponent's far arm with your near arm, creating a "figure-four" grip around their elbow. Your forearm is the "crossbar", your bicep the "upright".
    2. Place your chest firmly onto your opponent's chest, ensuring your hips are low and your weight is driving forward.
    3. Your free hand (the one not gripping the opponent's arm) reaches across your opponent's chest and grips your own bicep, creating a "gable grip" or "S-grip" on your own arm.
    4. Your head should be positioned on the same side as the arm you are attacking, creating a strong base and preventing them from turning into you.
    5. To initiate the lock, subtly shift your weight forward, pressing your chest into their chest and hips towards their head.
    6. Keeping your own elbow pinned to your side, slowly lift your elbow upwards, creating a rotational force at the opponent's shoulder.
    7. The opponent's elbow remains bent at approximately 90 degrees. The pressure is applied by rotating your own forearm upwards, which forces their wrist and hand away from their head.
    8. Maintain a constant downward pressure with your chest and hips to control their body as you apply the lock.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Applying pressure before the arm is properly isolated: This can lead to hyperextension of the opponent's elbow, risking ligament tears (e.g., UCL).
    • Muscling the submission with shoulder shrugs: This ineffective motion puts excessive strain on your own shoulder joint, risking rotator cuff impingement or tears.
    • Releasing the figure-four grip too early: This allows the opponent to escape the control and potentially counter, while also risking a painful wrench if not done carefully.
    • Not controlling the opponent's hips: This allows them to bridge or shrimp out, creating uncontrolled movements that can injure both parties.
    • Applying the lock with a straight arm: This transfers force directly to the opponent's elbow and wrist, not the shoulder, increasing the risk of joint damage to both you and your opponent.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip transition and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the "figure-four" and "gable grip" formation. (10 reps per side)
    2. Static drilling with light resistance: Your partner lies on their back. You secure the Americana position and have them offer very slight resistance as you move through the steps. Focus on smooth transitions. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Controlled drilling with partner: Your partner actively resists the initial control but does not attempt escapes. You focus on maintaining position and applying the lock with controlled pressure. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    4. Partnered drilling with active defense: Your partner begins to defend, but you maintain control and work to finish the submission, focusing on counter-defense. (10 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    5. Flow drilling: Practice transitioning into the Americana from side control and attempt the submission. Your partner can defend and escape, but the goal is to maintain control and practice the sequence. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Americana during live rolling, focusing on proper application and timing, but be prepared to disengage if the submission is not clean. (1 minute attempts, 100% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • During side control when the opponent is turtled or trying to bridge away.
    • When the opponent is reaching for your leg to attempt a guard pass or sweep.
    • When you have established a strong cross-face and can control their head movement.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Elbow Tuck: If the opponent begins to apply pressure, tuck your attacking elbow tightly to your ribs, creating a "frame" with your forearm against their grip.
    • Hip Escape and Turn: If they are applying pressure, shrimp your hips away and attempt to turn your body towards your attacking arm, creating space to break their grip.
    • Bridging and Rotating: While difficult, a strong bridge combined with a rotation of your body away from the pressure can sometimes force them to release or adjust, creating an escape opportunity.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Americana submission in BJJ?

    Your wrist may hurt because you are attempting to "bend" or "pull" their arm into submission instead of using your body's leverage. The Americana targets the shoulder joint. Ensure your "figure-four" grip is around their elbow, not their wrist, and that your own elbow is pinned to your side. The submission comes from rotating your own forearm upwards, using your shoulder and hip to create the lever, not by bending their wrist.

    Q: How can I effectively perform the Americana BJJ submission against a much larger opponent from side control?

    Against a larger opponent, focus intensely on controlling their hips and head to prevent them from using their weight effectively. Your "figure-four" grip must be tight, and your free hand must grip your bicep firmly to create a strong, stable frame. Instead of trying to lift their arm, use your body weight and a subtle hip rotation to drive their elbow into your chest and then slowly lift your elbow. Pressure is key, not brute strength.

    Q: Is it okay to use my legs to help finish the Americana submission from mount in BJJ?

    While you can use your legs to help control your opponent's hips and body from mount, they should not be used to directly "squeeze" or "pull" the arm into submission. The primary mechanics of the Americana should still come from your upper body and hips. Using your legs to "squeeze" the arm can lead to an ineffective submission and potentially injure your own knees or hips, while also not optimally targeting the shoulder joint.
