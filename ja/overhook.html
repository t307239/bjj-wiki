The overhook is a dominant positional control where one practitioner secures an arm that has passed over the opponent's body, wrapping their own arm around it.

White belts often fail by muscling the position, creating openings and risking injury due to a lack of understanding of leverage.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and hip to create a tight frame, minimizing the opponent's movement and maximizing your control.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a side-control or mount position, identify the opponent's arm closest to your head.
    2. Place your hand on the opponent's bicep, near their elbow, with your palm facing their body.
    3. Wrap your arm around the opponent's arm, ensuring your elbow is pointing towards the ceiling.
    4. Secure a grip on your own bicep with your opposite hand, creating a "figure-four" or "gable" grip.
    5. Drive your shoulder into the opponent's chest, angling your hips slightly towards their head.
    6. Shift your weight onto your leading foot, creating a base wider than shoulder-width.
    7. Simultaneously, slightly retract your hips while pressing your chest into their upper torso, preventing them from turning into you.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Elbow Position: Trying to "pull" the opponent's arm across with a bent elbow, forcing your own elbow into a high-tension, vulnerable position. This can lead to rotator cuff tears or elbow joint hyperextension.
    • Correct: Maintain a straight or slightly bent elbow throughout, using your shoulder and torso for leverage, not just arm strength.
    • Over-extending the Shoulder: Reaching too far to secure the grip, causing your shoulder to rotate externally beyond its safe range of motion. This risks a labral tear or dislocation.
    • Correct: Keep your shoulder packed and stable, securing the grip by bringing your bicep across your own chest.
    • Jerking the Arm: Rapidly and forcefully pulling the opponent's arm, ignoring their body's resistance. This can cause sudden strain on the opponent's shoulder or your own elbow and wrist joints.
    • Correct: Apply steady, controlled pressure, focusing on maintaining the frame and gradually tightening the control.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Isolation (0%): Practice securing the overhook grip on a heavy bag or a willing partner's stationary arm. Focus on the grip and bicep-to-bicep connection. (20 reps per side)
    2. Partnered Static Control (25%): From side control, have your partner resist minimally as you establish and hold the overhook position. Focus on maintaining your frame and hip angle. (10 reps per side, 30 seconds hold)
    3. Controlled Movement (50%): From side control, slowly move your hips and chest while maintaining the overhook. Your partner offers slight resistance to prevent easy movement. (10 reps per side, 1 minute)
    4. Guard Pass Entry (75%): Practice entering the overhook from a guard pass attempt (e.g., knee-slice). Your partner actively tries to defend. Focus on the transition to the overhook. (15 reps per side)
    5. Submission Setup (90%): Work on transitioning from the overhook to a submission (e.g., arm triangle). Partner offers significant resistance to the pass but not the submission. (10 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling (100%): Focus on securing and maintaining the overhook position during sparring. Do not force submissions, prioritize positional control. (3 rounds)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When transitioning from mount to side control and the opponent is trying to shrimp away.
    • During a knee-slice pass attempt where the opponent is turning into you.
    • When the opponent is attempting to escape from your side control by bridging.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Creating Space: The opponent can attempt to create space by driving their elbow into your chest, widening their base, and pushing their hips away to break your frame.
    • The "Shrimp" Escape: The opponent can shrimp their hips away while keeping their arm tight, creating an angle to bring their knee inside and escape the position.
    • Turning In: The opponent can attempt to turn their body into the overhooking arm, bringing their own arm to defend and potentially establishing their own control.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an overhook in BJJ?

    Shoulder pain often arises from over-reaching or forcing the grip. If you are extending your arm too far to secure the bicep grip, your shoulder joint is rotating past its stable range. Instead, keep your elbow close to your body and use your torso to bring your bicep across your own chest for a secure, pain-free grip.

    Q: How can a smaller white belt use an overhook effectively against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body mechanics are crucial. Focus on driving your shoulder into their chest and angling your hips to their head, creating a strong frame that prevents them from using their size advantage to simply push through you. Your weight distribution and hip angle are more important than brute strength.

    Q: When is the best time to switch from an overhook to a submission in BJJ?

    The optimal time to transition from an overhook to a submission is when you have fully secured the position and the opponent is unable to create space or escape. This means their hips are pinned, their arm is trapped, and you have established a dominant angle. Forcing a submission prematurely will often lead to losing the position.
