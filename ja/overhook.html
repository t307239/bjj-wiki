BJJ Overhook Position: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The overhook is a dominant upper-body control position, most commonly achieved from side control or when establishing guard. It allows you to limit your opponent's movement and set up attacks or sweeps.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength rather than leverage, leading to ineffective control and potential injury. They fail to understand the subtle body positioning required.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and scapula to create a powerful wedge, deactivating your opponent's ability to frame or shrimp away. This is achieved by driving your shoulder blade into their chest or back.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From side control, as you turn your hips towards your opponent's head, extend your arm that is on the same side as their head.
    2. Your goal is to place your bicep and shoulder against the side of their neck or upper chest, creating a "half-guard" like space.
    3. Grip your own bicep with your free hand, forming a "figure-four" grip around their neck/upper chest area.
    4. Drive your shoulder blade into their body, ensuring it is posterior to their scapula and anterior to their spine.
    5. Keep your hips close to their body, creating a tight connection and preventing them from creating space to shrimp.
    6. Rotate your torso so your chest is facing towards their head, creating a strong fulcrum.
    7. Apply downward pressure through your shoulder and upward pressure with your hips, pinning their upper body.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect shoulder rotation: Forcing the arm across the neck with a straight elbow can hyperextend the opponent's shoulder or neck, causing ligament tears or nerve impingement. The correct movement involves rotating the shoulder joint and driving the scapula, not just pushing the arm.
    • Excessive neck cranking: Gripping too tightly around the opponent's neck with the figure-four without proper shoulder engagement can lead to dangerous pressure on the cervical spine, risking nerve damage or even vertebral artery dissection. The overhook's primary function is positional control, not a direct choke; maintain a loose grip on your own bicep and focus on the shoulder pin.
    • Hip disconnection: Allowing the hips to drift away from the opponent's body while attempting the overhook creates space for them to shrimp out or recompose guard. This disconnects your base and negates the leverage. Maintain constant hip contact and drive your hips forward into them.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill (0%): Practice the arm and grip placement for the overhook from a side control position on a dummy or mat. Focus on the shoulder-to-body connection. (20 reps)
    2. Partner drill (25%): With a compliant partner, practice establishing the overhook from side control. Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to focus on the body mechanics and grip. (10 reps per side)
    3. Positional sparring (50%): Start in side control. The goal is to establish the overhook. The opponent can lightly defend to prevent easy establishment. (5 minutes)
    4. Transition drill (75%): From a neutral position, work to achieve the overhook as a transitional control. Opponent actively tries to prevent it. (5 minutes)
    5. Positional sparring with escape focus (90%): Start with the overhook established. The opponent's primary goal is to escape. You must maintain the overhook and prevent escapes. (5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Incorporate the overhook naturally into your rolling. Focus on maintaining the position and transitioning to attacks or sweeps. (3 rounds)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established side control and your opponent is trying to create space by framing with their arms.
    • When your opponent is attempting to recompose guard from side control and you can isolate their upper body.
    • When you are in a scramble and can secure an overhook to temporarily halt their movement and regain control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimping and Framing: Immediately after the overhook is applied, drive your hips away while simultaneously framing with your free arm against their shoulder or bicep, creating space to disengage and recompose guard.
    • Head and Arm Escape: If the overhook is tight, turn your head away from the overhook arm and drive your shoulder into their chest while simultaneously trying to bring your other arm under their chin to create space and escape.
    • Switching Angles: If the overhook is established and you are stuck, attempt to switch your hips to the opposite side, creating a brief moment of disorientation for your opponent and allowing you to attempt a guard recovery.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do the Overhook in BJJ?

    Shoulder pain during the overhook often stems from incorrect joint mechanics. You might be trying to force your arm across your opponent's neck with a straight elbow, leading to hyperextension or impingement. Instead, focus on rotating your shoulder joint and driving your scapula (shoulder blade) into their body. This uses your back and core muscles for leverage, protecting your shoulder joint and creating a more stable, biomechanically sound overhook.

    Q: How can I get an Overhook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, brute strength is not your ally for the overhook. Instead, prioritize timing and precise body positioning. Use your hips and lower body to initiate the movement, creating an angle to get your shoulder behind their upper body. Once you have the angle, drive your scapula into their rib cage or back, not just their arm. Grip your own bicep and use your core to maintain that tight connection, leveraging their size against them.

    Q: When should I use the Overhook, and when should I avoid it in BJJ?

    The overhook is best used when you have established a dominant control position like side control, or when you want to shut down your opponent's movement and prevent them from creating space or escaping. You should be cautious about using it if your opponent has a strong, established guard that you are struggling to pass, or if they are actively working to create significant space to initiate a sweep. Overextending to get an overhook without proper body control can leave you vulnerable.
