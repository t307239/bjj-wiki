Overhook Position: A White Belt's Essential Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The overhook is a dominant control position, typically achieved from guard or side control, where one grappler secures an arm around the opponent's body. It grants superior leverage and isolates the opponent's posture and mobility.

White belts often struggle by over-relying on brute strength rather than precise body mechanics, leading to wasted energy and loss of control. They also tend to create insufficient frames, allowing the opponent to escape easily.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and hip to create a "wall" that prevents the opponent from turning into you, while your hooked arm acts as a lever to control their upper body rotation.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's right arm with your right arm's bicep and forearm, ensuring your hand grips your own right shoulder. Your left arm frames against their left hip.
    2. Rotate your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's body. Your weight should be distributed primarily onto your left hip.
    3. Drive your left shoulder into their right shoulder blade, preventing them from turning into you. This creates a "pinning" effect.
    4. Your right arm acts as a lever; keep your elbow tucked and close to your body, maintaining tension in your bicep.
    5. Simultaneously, extend your left leg slightly, pushing your knee towards their left armpit to further disrupt their base.
    6. If in side control, achieve the overhook by reaching your right arm *under* their right armpit and hooking your hand onto your own left shoulder blade. Keep your chest tight to their chest.
    7. Shift your weight to your right side, angling your hips to match their body angle, creating a "crushing" pressure that limits their movement.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Elbow Extension: Extending your overhooking elbow fully while their arm is trapped can hyperextend your own elbow joint, potentially tearing the ulnar collateral ligament.
    • Correct Alternative: Maintain a slight bend in your overhooking elbow, keeping it close to your body, and use your shoulder and hip for control.
    • Neck Strain from Over-Pulling: When attempting to pull an opponent down with an overhook, white belts often crane their neck backward to get a grip, straining cervical vertebrae and muscles.
    • Correct Alternative: Use your core and hips to drive your chest into their chest, creating downward pressure without excessive neck extension.
    • Knee Injury from Improper Framing: In guard, incorrectly posting your knee against the opponent's hip while trying to create an overhook can lead to knee valgus (inward collapse), stressing the ACL and MCL.
    • Correct Alternative: Frame with your forearm against their hip, keeping your knee joint stable and your leg slightly extended.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Rotation & Frame Drill: (10 reps per side) Practice rotating your hips and creating the frame with your non-overhooking arm without resistance, focusing on the hip angle and shoulder pressure.
    2. Partnered Overhook Setup (No Resistance): (10 reps per side) With a partner, practice securing the overhook grip and establishing the proper body angle. Partner offers no resistance. Reps: 5 per side.
    3. Overhook Control Drill (Light Resistance): (5 mins) Partner attempts to peel your arm off or turn into you. Focus on maintaining the overhook and hip angle. Resistance: 25%.
    4. Overhook to Sweep Attempt (Controlled): (5 mins) From guard, attempt a sweep using the overhook (e.g., hip bump sweep). Partner offers light resistance to the sweep. Resistance: 50%.
    5. Overhook to Pass Attempt (Controlled): (5 mins) From side control with an overhook, practice driving forward and controlling the opponent's posture. Partner attempts to shrimp or bridge. Resistance: 75%.
    6. Live Rolling (Focus on Overhook): (3 x 5 min rounds) During rolling, actively seek to establish and utilize the overhook in various scenarios. Resistance: 90-100%.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent is posturing up heavily from your closed guard, creating an opportunity to snake your arm around their torso.
    • When you've successfully transitioned to side control and can isolate an opponent's arm, preventing their escape.
    • When an opponent attempts a guard pass and leaves their arm exposed to an overhook.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Elbow Escape (from Side Control): If your opponent has an overhook on your right arm, place your left hand on their hip and drive your left elbow into the mat, creating space to shrimp your hips out and regain guard.
    • Posture Up and Break Grip (from Guard): If your opponent has an overhook from your guard, maintain a strong base, drive your chest into them, and use your free arm to forcefully pry their overhooking arm off by pushing their elbow away from your body.
    • Turn to Your Overhooking Arm (from Side Control): If you are on the bottom and your opponent has an overhook, turn your body towards their overhooking arm, tucking your chin and using your shoulder to drive into their chest, creating a "crumple" effect to escape.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an overhook in BJJ?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are forcing the overhook with excessive external rotation of your shoulder joint or by pulling with your arm instead of driving with your body. Ensure your shoulder is not internally rotating and that your elbow stays relatively close to your body, using your torso to generate the power for the hook.

    Q: How can I get an overhook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, biomechanical leverage is paramount. Focus on using your hips to generate angular momentum rather than trying to match their strength. Create a strong frame with your non-overhooking arm, and use your hips to drive your shoulder into their center of mass, making them off-balance before securing the overhook.

    Q: When should I transition from an overhook to a sweep or submission in BJJ?

    Transition when you feel the opponent's base significantly compromised or their posture broken. For sweeps, look for their weight to be on their heels or when they are leaning too far forward. For submissions, the overhook often sets up armbars or triangles by controlling their upper body and creating opportunities to isolate limbs.
