Inside Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanically Accurate Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 茶帯昇格要件

The Inside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the opponent's ankle and knee, applied from positions where you can isolate a leg. It aims to create a rotational force that forces a tap.

White belts often struggle by rushing, using excessive strength, or failing to understand the precise angles required, leading to missed submissions or injury.

The key is controlling the opponent's hip and foot to create the necessary torque, allowing a controlled application of pressure, not brute force.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a 50-50 guard or similar leg entanglement, secure the opponent's foot with your armpit, heel of your hand pressing into the crease of their ankle. Your forearm should be flush against the outside of their shin.
    2. Grip the opponent's foot with your opposite hand, fingers pointing towards their toes, thumb wrapped around the top of the foot. This is a gable grip on the foot.
    3. Your hips must be positioned to face the opponent's knee, creating a perpendicular angle to their leg. Your chest should be close to their thigh.
    4. Drive your weight into the opponent's knee, pushing it away from your body slightly. This stretches their leg and prevents them from easily turning.
    5. Your free leg's foot should be planted firmly on the mat, providing a stable base and allowing you to hip out.
    6. With your free hip, perform a controlled hip extension, pushing your hips towards the opponent's knee. This drives your opponent's heel into your armpit.
    7. Simultaneously, apply a slight upward pressure with your grip on their foot, while your armpit controls the heel's rotation. This creates torque on the ankle and knee.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly applying pressure before establishing the correct hip angle: This can lead to the knee joint's anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) being overstretched or torn when the opponent attempts to escape by turning into you.
    • Gripping the foot too loosely or with fingers extended: This allows the opponent's foot to slip, potentially causing your own knee or ankle to twist awkwardly as you attempt to compensate for the lost grip.
    • Rushing the finish without controlling the hip and knee: This can result in the opponent's ankle being hyper-extended or the fibular collateral ligament (LCL) being stressed if they manage to rotate their leg out of the lock biomechanically.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip and body positioning without a partner, focusing on achieving the correct perpendicular angle to an imaginary opponent's leg. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a cooperative partner, practice securing the foot and establishing the hip angle. Partner remains completely passive. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    3. Controlled entry drill: From a starting position (e.g., 50-50 guard), one partner attempts to secure the leg lock while the other offers minimal resistance, allowing the attacker to isolate and control the leg. (5 minutes per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled submission drill: Progress to applying the lock with light pressure, stopping immediately upon the partner's tap. Focus on the exact finishing mechanics. (5 minutes per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring with leg lock focus: Spar with a focus on attacking and defending leg locks from specific positions, allowing for the introduction of the Inside Heel Hook. (5 minutes rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling with Inside Heel Hook attempts: Incorporate the Inside Heel Hook as a regular attacking option during rolling, emphasizing control and safety over immediate submission. (5 minutes rounds, 90% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When in a 50-50 guard and you can isolate one of the opponent's legs.
    • When you have successfully swept or transitioned to a position where the opponent's leg is extended and accessible.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg becomes exposed and available for control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Straighten and Turn": As the opponent attempts to secure the lock, immediately straighten your trapped leg as much as possible and turn your hips away from their pressure, creating slack and relieving torque.
    • The "Knee Shield and Push": If your leg is being attacked, use your free leg to create a knee shield against the opponent's hip or chest, and push away to create distance and regain a safer guard.
    • The "Roll Through" (Escape): If the opponent has a deep lock, and you cannot escape via straightening or pushing, attempt a controlled roll in the direction of the lock to attempt to unwind your leg, though this carries its own risks.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do an Inside Heel Hook?

    Ankle pain during an Inside Heel Hook attempt usually stems from applying the lock without proper control of the opponent's hip and knee. If the opponent's leg is not perpendicular to your body, or if you are not driving your hips into their knee, you are creating shear forces on your own ankle instead of rotational force on theirs. Ensure your hips are facing the opponent's knee and you are driving your weight into their thigh, creating a solid base before applying rotational pressure.

    Q: How do I apply an Inside Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise angles become paramount. Focus on isolating their leg effectively, perhaps by using a closed guard or half guard to control their hips first. Once you have their foot secured, your hip extension and driving weight into their knee are crucial. Do not try to overpower them; instead, use your body weight and the biomechanics of the lock to create the necessary torque. A strong grip on their foot, with your armpit deep on their heel, is vital.

    Q: When is the best time to go for an Inside Heel Hook in BJJ?

    The Inside Heel Hook is best attempted when you have achieved a dominant position that allows you to isolate and control an opponent's leg. This typically occurs after a successful sweep, when transitioning to a leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard, or when the opponent exposes their leg during a guard pass attempt. Avoid attempting it from disadvantageous positions where you can be easily swept or countered, as it requires a stable base and control of the opponent's hips.
