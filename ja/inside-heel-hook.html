The Inside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the knee joint, typically initiated from positions where you can isolate an opponent's leg, such as the 50-50 guard or certain half-guard scenarios.

White belts often rush this technique, leading to poor control and potential injury for both parties, neglecting the crucial hip mechanics required for safe and effective execution.

The key to a successful Inside Heel Hook lies in controlling the opponent's hip and ankle while simultaneously applying rotational pressure to their knee, creating leverage through your own body's structure.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Grip: Secure a deep gable grip around the opponent's ankle, with your thumb on the instep and fingers wrapped around the Achilles tendon. Your arms should be fully extended, creating a stable frame.
    2. Hip Position: Drive your hips forward and to the side, perpendicular to the opponent's leg. Your chest should be pressing into their thigh, preventing them from easily rotating away.
    3. Knee Alignment: Ensure your opponent's knee is bent at approximately 90 degrees. If their knee is straight, the lock is less effective and increases injury risk.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your hips, creating a fulcrum point. Your hips are the power source, not your arms pulling.
    5. Ankle Control: Maintain constant pressure on the ankle with your gable grip, preventing them from straightening their leg or escaping.
    6. Rotation: Using your hips as the pivot, rotate your torso away from the opponent's leg. This rotation, combined with the ankle control, generates torque on the knee.
    7. Finishing Pressure: As you rotate, slightly extend your hips and drive your chest forward. Apply pressure slowly and controlled, listening for any reaction.
    8. Leg Placement: Your free leg should be hooked inside the opponent's thigh, preventing them from bridging or turning into you. This also helps stabilize your base.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Error: Grabbing the opponent's foot with a loose grip and pulling upwards with your arms without hip drive. Damage: This can cause hyperextension or spraining of the opponent's ankle and knee ligaments (ACL, MCL) due to uncontrolled torque.

    Correct: Secure a tight gable grip on the ankle and use your hips to drive forward and create rotational pressure, not arm strength.

    • Error: Allowing the opponent's knee to remain straight or hyperextended during the attack. Damage: This puts excessive shear force on the knee joint, potentially tearing the meniscus or ACL.

    Correct: Ensure the opponent's knee is bent to approximately 90 degrees before initiating the finishing rotation.

    • Error: Not controlling the opponent's hip, allowing them to shrimp or bridge out easily. Damage: This uncontrolled movement can lead to awkward joint stress on your own body, potentially injuring your hips or back.

    Correct: Drive your chest into their thigh and use your hips to anchor their pelvis, creating a stable base.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Drill (0%): Practice the gable grip on your own ankle, focusing on hip positioning and the feeling of the rotation. 10 reps per side.
    2. Solo Drill (0%): With a training partner lying on their back, practice securing the gable grip and driving your hips forward while they offer no resistance. 10 reps per side.
    3. Assisted Drill (25%): Partner lies on their back. You secure the gable grip, drive hips, and they gently resist by trying to straighten their leg. Focus on maintaining grip and hip pressure. 5 reps per side.
    4. Controlled Entry (50%): From a controlled position (e.g., 50-50 guard), practice entering the Inside Heel Hook. Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to focus on the hip and ankle control. 5 reps per side.
    5. Progressive Resistance (75%): Practice the entry and submission from a more dynamic position. Partner actively defends but allows you to work through the steps, stopping if you feel excessive resistance. 3 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (90-100%): Attempt the Inside Heel Hook in live rolling, prioritizing safety and control. Tap early if you feel caught or if your partner is struggling to escape. Focus on correct mechanics over finishing.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have achieved a stable 50-50 guard position, with your opponent's leg trapped between yours.
    • When you are in a half-guard where you can isolate and control the opponent's ankle and hip.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg becomes accessible and isolated.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape Hip: If the opponent attempts the Inside Heel Hook, immediately shrimp your hips away and try to create space. This breaks the rotational pressure on your knee.
    • Straighten Leg: If you feel the grip applied, attempt to straighten your leg and pull it out. This reduces the ankle control and the effectiveness of the heel hook.
    • Turn In: If you have enough space, try to turn your body into the attacker, collapsing their base and potentially escaping the leg lock.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do an Inside Heel Hook as a white belt?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not controlling the opponent's hip, allowing them to create uncontrolled rotation. Also, you might be trying to finish with arm strength instead of hip drive, leading to hyperextension. Always ensure the opponent's knee is bent to 90 degrees and focus on driving your hips forward to generate the torque.

    Q: How can I safely attempt an Inside Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, precise control of their ankle and hip is paramount. Use your entire body to anchor their hip by driving your chest into their thigh. Focus on a deep gable grip and use your hips to rotate, rather than trying to overpower them. Prioritize maintaining your structure and leverage.

    Q: When is the best time to attack the Inside Heel Hook if I'm a white belt?

    As a white belt, the safest and most opportune times to attempt an Inside Heel Hook are when you have achieved a very stable 50-50 guard or a dominant half-guard position where you have complete control of the opponent's ankle and hip. Avoid attempting it in transitional phases where control is not yet established.
