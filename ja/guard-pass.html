This technique begins when your opponent establishes closed guard, aiming to break their posture and pass their legs to achieve a dominant control position like side control.

White belts often fail by trying to force the pass with raw strength or by neglecting the importance of posture and base, leading to them being swept or controlled.

The key insight is to consistently disrupt the opponent's hip connection and maintain a strong, stable base while applying controlled forward pressure.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Establish a strong "collar and sleeve" grip on the opponent's gi, placing your palms on their collar and your other hand on their opposite sleeve.
    2. Angle your hips at approximately 45 degrees away from the opponent's legs, creating a base wider than shoulder-width.
    3. Drive your chest towards their hip on the same side as your collar grip, initiating a slight forward weight transfer.
    4. Use your knee on the side of your collar grip to "step" your leg inside their guard, aiming to get your knee past their hip line.
    5. Simultaneously, use your "frame" hand (sleeve grip) to push their knee away or control their leg, preventing them from re-establishing guard.
    6. Drive your hips forward and down, collapsing their posture and creating space to slide your other knee past their hips.
    7. Once your knees are past their hips, secure a "cross-face" grip on their head and a "hip control" grip on their far hip to maintain side control.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Attempting to "muscle" through a closed guard by pushing straight down on the opponent's knees can overextend your own knee joint, leading to ACL or MCL tears.
    • Incorrectly framing with your arms outstretched and elbows locked when they attempt a sweep can result in elbow hyperextension, causing ulnar collateral ligament damage.
    • Trying to "jump" your legs over the opponent's hips without proper hip angling and weight transfer can cause your hips to collide with theirs, potentially leading to hip flexor strains or labral tears.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip angle, leg step, and frame movement without a partner. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (static): With a compliant partner, practice the initial grips and hip angle. (5 minutes, 25% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (controlled movement): Practice the leg step and frame without full guard closure. (5 minutes, 50% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (closed guard simulation): Partner maintains a loose closed guard, and you practice the full pass sequence. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    5. Flow rolling: Incorporate the guard pass into a controlled rolling session, focusing on timing and execution. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the guard pass during a live roll, prioritizing safety and technique. (5 minutes, 100% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent's hips are within your reach and their legs are closed.
    • When the opponent's posture is slightly elevated, making it easier to break.
    • When you have established dominant grips on their collar and sleeve.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If the opponent begins to pass, immediately hip escape to create distance and attempt to re-establish guard or a different defensive position.
    • Shrimping and Framing: When the opponent attempts to drive past your legs, actively "shrimp" your hips away and use your arms to create frames, preventing their hips from getting past yours.
    • Tuck and Roll: If the opponent achieves a dominant passing position, tuck your chin to your chest and attempt a forward roll to escape the position and reset.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to break their closed guard in BJJ?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you are trying to pry open their legs by pushing directly against their knees with your hands. Instead, focus on gripping their collar and sleeve, and use your body's leverage by driving your chest towards their hip while angling your hips away. This transfers the force to their posture and hips, not your wrists.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, rely on precision over power. Focus on breaking their posture by controlling their collar and sleeve, and use your hip angle to create leverage. Instead of trying to muscle your way through, aim to "cut" your knee through their guard by getting your hips low and driving forward, using their weight against them.

    Q: I keep getting swept when I try to pass their guard, what am I doing wrong?

    Getting swept usually means you're losing your base and posture. Ensure your hips are angled correctly (45 degrees) and your base is wide. When you step your leg in, drive your chest towards their hip to prevent them from using your posture against you. Maintain constant forward pressure and keep your weight distributed over their hips.
