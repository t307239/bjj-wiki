Wrist Lock BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

The Wrist Lock is a joint lock targeting the opponent's wrist, typically applied from a dominant position like side-control or mount.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precision, attempting to 'muscle' the submission rather than using leverage and anatomical alignment.

The key insight is to isolate the wrist joint and apply a controlled flexion or extension, leveraging the opponent's own grip strength against them.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From side-control, secure your opponent's wrist with your forearm across their forearm, forming a perpendicular frame. Your elbow should be tucked, not flared.
    2. Grip your own right bicep with your left hand (if attacking opponent's right wrist). Your left palm faces upward.
    3. Drive your hips slightly forward and to the side, creating a stable base and preventing your opponent from easily bridging or escaping.
    4. Your left knee should be angled towards the opponent's head, maintaining tight control of their posture and preventing them from posturing up.
    5. Apply gentle pressure by extending your elbow (the one controlling their forearm) slightly, while simultaneously pulling your bicep grip towards your chest.
    6. The opponent's wrist should be flexed downwards (pronation), with your forearm acting as the fulcrum and your bicep grip as the lever arm.
    7. Maintain a stable base with your hips low and wide, ensuring your weight is distributed over your opponent, not just pushing their arm.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Elbow Flaring: Flaring your elbow outwards when applying the lock places excessive rotational stress on the opponent's elbow and shoulder, potentially causing ligamentous tears (e.g., UCL). The correct movement is to keep the elbow tucked and apply pressure through forearm extension.
    • Excessive Wrist Extension/Flexion: Trying to bend the opponent's wrist too far beyond its natural range of motion can cause sprains or even fractures of the carpal bones. The biomechanically correct application involves controlled flexion/extension using leverage, not brute force.
    • Lifting the Opponent's Arm: Attempting to lift the opponent's entire arm upwards while applying the wrist lock, instead of controlling their forearm and wrist, can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strains for both parties. Focus on isolating the forearm and wrist.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Drill (0% resistance): Practice the grip and body positioning on your own arm, focusing on the feel of the forearm frame and bicep grip. 20 repetitions.
    2. Partner Drill - Static Grip (25% resistance): Partner remains still, offering no resistance to the grip. Focus on establishing the correct forearm frame and bicep grip. 10 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner Drill - Controlled Movement (50% resistance): Partner offers light resistance to you establishing the grip and framing, but does not actively defend. Focus on smooth transitions. 10 repetitions per side.
    4. Partner Drill - Basic Defense (75% resistance): Partner attempts a basic defense (e.g., pulling their arm out). You practice maintaining the frame and adjusting. Focus on understanding how resistance changes the mechanics. 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Situational Drilling (90% resistance): Apply the Wrist Lock from a specific position (e.g., side-control) and allow the partner to defend, focusing on finishing the technique against light resistance. 3 repetitions per side.
    6. Live Rolling (100% resistance): Attempt the Wrist Lock during rolling, but tap immediately if you feel any discomfort or if your partner taps. Focus on timing and opportunity identification. 5-minute rounds, focus on Wrist Lock attempts.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent's arm is extended and isolated from their body, particularly when they are trying to create space or push away.
    • During transitions where you have secured a strong grip on their wrist and can establish a forearm frame.
    • When the opponent is attempting to create a grip on your gi or body and their wrist is accessible.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Fleeing the Joint: The opponent immediately pulls their arm out of the locked position by rotating their wrist away from the flexion/extension and simultaneously moving their elbow in the opposite direction of the applied pressure. This breaks the line of attack.
    • Stacking the Opponent: The opponent drives their weight forward, collapsing their posture and bringing their hips onto your shoulders. This alleviates pressure on the wrist and allows them to escape the submission attempt.
    • Re-establishing Base and Framing: The opponent uses their free hand to push against your shoulder or head, creating space to untangle their arm. They then widen their base to prevent you from re-establishing control.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Wrist Lock in BJJ?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are applying pressure incorrectly, often by attempting to bend the opponent's wrist beyond its natural range of motion with brute force. The Wrist Lock in BJJ relies on isolating the wrist joint and using leverage. Ensure your forearm is acting as a solid frame against the opponent's forearm, and that you are applying pressure by extending your elbow and pulling your bicep grip, rather than directly bending their hand.

    Q: How can I apply a Wrist Lock against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, focus on the biomechanical advantage of leverage. Isolate their arm and wrist effectively. Ensure your forearm frame is tight and perpendicular to their forearm. Drive your hips forward and low to create a stable base and use your body weight to control their posture, rather than relying solely on upper body strength. The goal is to use their size against them by creating a mechanical disadvantage at the wrist joint.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Wrist Lock in BJJ, and shouldn't I focus on chokes first?

    Wrist Locks are excellent opportunistic submissions, often available when an opponent's arm is extended or isolated during transitions or defense. While chokes are fundamental, Wrist Locks can be a valuable secondary submission. Attempt them when you have a clear grip and frame, especially if the opponent is defending chokes aggressively or leaving their wrist exposed. They can be a quick way to finish a fight if applied correctly and at the right moment.
