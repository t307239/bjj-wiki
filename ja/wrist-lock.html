The Wrist Lock is a joint lock targeting the opponent's wrist, typically applied from control positions like closed guard. Its goal is to force a submission by hyperextending or hyperflexing the wrist joint.

White belts often fail by applying excessive, uncontrolled force, leading to injury rather than a tap. They misunderstand the subtle leverage needed, trying to muscle the submission.

The key insight is isolating the opponent's wrist and using your body's leverage to create a controlled, precise angle of pressure, rather than brute strength.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, establish control of the opponent's arm by securing their wrist with your right hand, thumb pointing towards their knuckles. Your left arm grips their bicep, preventing them from pulling their arm out.
    2. Position your hips at a 45-degree angle to the opponent's torso, creating a stable base. Your knees should be tight to their hips, maintaining the closed guard.
    3. Slowly begin to open your guard, allowing the opponent's arm to extend slightly away from their body. Maintain constant pressure on their bicep with your left hand.
    4. With your right hand, begin to cup the opponent's wrist, ensuring your fingers wrap around the back of their hand and your thumb is on the palm side, directly opposite your fingers.
    5. Apply a slight upward pressure with your right hand, lifting their wrist towards their elbow. Simultaneously, begin to lower your hips, creating a fulcrum effect at their wrist.
    6. Your left arm, gripping the bicep, should now begin to pull the opponent's arm slightly downwards, towards your chest. This creates a breaking tension.
    7. As you continue to lower your hips and pull the bicep, the opponent's wrist will be forced into hyperextension. Apply steady, controlled pressure until they tap.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect grip on opponent's wrist: Applying pressure with your fingers wrapped around their wrist bone instead of cupping their hand. This can cause ligament tears or fractures in your own hand and wrist from the impact.
    • Jerking motion instead of controlled pressure: Suddenly snapping your hips down or pulling hard on the arm. This can lead to severe sprains or even fractures of the opponent's wrist or elbow joint.
    • Allowing opponent to pull their arm out: Not maintaining a tight bicep grip or not angling your hips correctly. This results in a loss of control and can lead to the opponent applying a counter-grapevine or escape, potentially injuring your own limbs if caught in an awkward position.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Wrist Isolation Drill: Practice cupping and controlling your own wrist with your opposite hand, simulating the grip. Perform 20 repetitions per wrist, 0% resistance.
    2. Partnered Grip Familiarization: With a partner in closed guard, practice establishing the correct wrist and bicep grips without any movement. Partner remains completely still. 10 repetitions per side, 0% resistance.
    3. Controlled Extension Drill: From closed guard, gently extend the opponent's arm to the point of the wrist lock, but stop before applying pressure. Partner provides minimal assistance to feel the extension. 10 repetitions per side, 25% resistance.
    4. Slow Motion Wrist Lock Application: Apply the wrist lock at a very slow, controlled pace, focusing on hip movement and weight transfer. Partner provides verbal cues and taps early. 5 repetitions per side, 50% resistance.
    5. Partial Resistance Wrist Lock: Apply the wrist lock with increasing pressure as the opponent attempts to resist slightly by pulling their arm away or tensing. Partner offers light, controlled resistance. 5 repetitions per side, 75% resistance.
    6. Live Rolling Wrist Lock Attempts: In rolling, actively look for opportunities to apply the wrist lock from closed guard, focusing on correct mechanics and speed. Partner defends and taps immediately if caught. 2 minutes of rolling, 90-100% resistance.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up and you have a secure closed guard.
    • When the opponent's arm is extended slightly away from their body, creating an opening.
    • When you have established a strong bicep control with your non-attacking arm, preventing them from easily retracting their limb.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Arm Retraction: The opponent can immediately retract their arm by bringing their elbow towards their own hip, breaking the extension needed for the lock. This requires maintaining a tight base and grip.
    • Hip Escape and Rotation: The opponent can hip escape to the side and rotate their body, creating space to pull their arm out and relieving the pressure on the wrist. This is facilitated by not having your hips glued to their body.
    • Wrist Flexion/Extension Adjustment: The opponent can subtly adjust the angle of their wrist by flexing or extending it in the opposite direction of the applied pressure, finding a 'dead zone' where the lock is ineffective. This requires a very precise application of pressure from the attacker.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Wrist Lock from closed guard?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are trying to muscle the submission or are not isolating the opponent's wrist properly. When applying the wrist lock, focus on using your hips to create leverage. Ensure your grip is on the opponent's hand, not their forearm, and apply steady, controlled pressure. Avoid jerky motions, which can lead to hyperextension and injury to yourself or your partner.

    Q: How can I apply a Wrist Lock from closed guard against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Focus on creating a strong base with your hips, angled at 45 degrees, to prevent them from posturing up. Use your legs to control their torso and prevent them from generating power. When attacking the wrist, ensure you have a secure bicep grip with your other arm to prevent them from simply pulling their arm out. Slowly extend their arm and apply the lock with your hips, not your arms.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Wrist Lock from closed guard in BJJ?

    The optimal time to attempt a wrist lock from closed guard is when the opponent is posturing up, extending their arm away from their body to create space or prepare for a pass. This posture creates the necessary extension for the lock. If their arm is tucked in tightly or they are flat on their back, it's usually not the right moment. Look for the moment their arm is accessible and extended, but before they can fully retract it.
