The Ankle Lock is a submission targeting the opponent's ankle joint, applied from various control positions. It aims to force a tap by hyperextending or hyperflexing the ankle.

White belts often fail by relying on brute strength, ignoring joint mechanics, and creating dangerous leverage angles. This leads to ineffective attempts and potential injury to themselves or their partner.

The key insight is using your body weight and hip rotation to create a fulcrum, isolating the ankle and applying controlled pressure, rather than simply pulling with your arms.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Starting Position: Opponent on their back, you are in a position to isolate one of their legs (e.g., inside half guard or controlling from the side).
    2. Grip: Secure your opponent's ankle with a "figure-four" grip: your left hand grips your opponent's ankle bone (malleolus) from the outside, and your right hand grips your own left wrist.
    3. Hip Angle: Drive your hips forward and slightly to the side, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's hips. Your hips should be lower than your opponent's hips.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your opponent's leg, pinning it between your thighs. Your chest should be in contact with their thigh.
    5. Leverage Creation: Arch your back and lift your hips upward. Simultaneously, pull your gripped wrist towards your chest, keeping your elbows tucked in.
    6. Ankle Isolation: Ensure your opponent's foot is not able to turn or escape. Your grip should be tight around the ankle bone, preventing rotation.
    7. Pressure Application: Apply steady, controlled pressure by extending your hips and pulling your wrist. The pressure should be felt on the back of the opponent's ankle and the front of their shin.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Hip Drive: Driving hips straight forward without a 45-degree angle can twist the opponent's knee, causing ACL/MCL tears.
    • Arm Pulling Instead of Hip Extension: Relying solely on arm strength to pull the ankle can hyperextend your own elbow or shoulder, leading to ligamentous injury.
    • Loose Grip on Ankle: A loose grip allows the opponent's foot to rotate, potentially snapping the tibia or fibula if excessive force is applied.
    • Uncontrolled Release: Releasing the submission suddenly can cause the opponent's ankle to snap back, damaging ligaments and tendons.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Grip Practice: Practice the figure-four grip on your own leg or a training dummy. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo Hip Movement: Practice the hip drive and arching motion without a partner. (30 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partnered Static Hold: With a compliant partner, achieve the grip and hip position. Partner offers no resistance. You hold for 5 seconds. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    4. Controlled Pressure Drill: With a compliant partner, apply gentle, increasing pressure. Partner taps early. (20 reps, 25% resistance)
    5. Live Leg Isolation Drill: From a specific position (e.g., closed guard), work to isolate the leg and establish the grip. Focus on control, not submission. (15 reps, 50% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling with Tap Rule: Attempt the ankle lock in live rolling. Tap immediately if you feel any joint pressure on yourself, and your partner taps immediately when they feel pressure. (5 minute rounds, 75% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is on their back and their leg is accessible, especially if they are trying to pass your guard.
    • When you have secured a strong leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard or have managed to isolate a single leg.
    • After successfully sweeping or taking down an opponent and landing in a position where you can control their legs.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Escape by Turning Hip: The opponent turns their hips away from your pressure, creating space and relieving the direct angle of attack on the ankle. This often involves a "dog walk" motion.
    • Stomp and Leg Release: The opponent forcefully stomps their free foot on the mat to create space and then "stomps" down on your hips to break your structure and release the leg.
    • Counter-Sweep/Guard Pass: If the attacker's base is compromised, the defender can attempt to use the pressure of the ankle lock attempt to initiate a sweep or guard pass in the opposite direction.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle lock not work and my hips hurt?

    Your hips likely hurt because you are not using the correct biomechanical angle. Instead of driving your hips forward at a 45-degree angle and arching your back to create leverage, you are probably pushing straight forward, which strains your lower back. Ensure your hips are lower than your opponent's and your weight is transferred through your hips, not just your back.

    Q: How can I ankle lock a much bigger opponent without getting squashed?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on precise mechanics over strength. Ensure you isolate their leg effectively and maintain a tight figure-four grip. Drive your hips forward at a sharp 45-degree angle to create leverage, and use your arch to lift your hips. Your goal is to use their weight against them by controlling the ankle's pivot point.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an ankle lock in BJJ sparring?

    The ideal time to attempt an ankle lock is when your opponent is on their back, and you have successfully isolated one of their legs. This often happens after a failed guard pass attempt by them, or when you've managed to secure a strong leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard. Timing is crucial; do not force it if the position is not secure.
