Ankle Lock Technique for BJJ White Belts

BLUE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

The ankle lock is a powerful submission targeting the opponent's ankle joint. It's typically applied from controlling positions like half guard or 50-50 guard.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of body awareness and controlling their own limbs, leading to ineffective attempts and injury risk. They may "pull hard" instead of using leverage.

The key insight is isolating the opponent's heel and using your own body to create a precise lever against their ankle, preventing them from escaping by rotating their foot.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a controlling position (e.g., 50-50 guard), establish a "figure-four" grip: your right arm wraps under the opponent's right ankle, and your left hand grips your own right bicep. Your right forearm presses against the opponent's shin, just above the ankle.
    2. Your hips must be perpendicular to the opponent's leg being attacked. Your left leg should extend, creating a stable base with your foot flat on the mat.
    3. Your right knee should be on the mat, with your shin behind the opponent's thigh, preventing them from kicking their leg free.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly upward, while simultaneously pulling your opponent's heel towards your chest with your figure-four grip.
    5. Apply pressure by extending your right leg slightly and driving your hips into the opponent's ankle, creating a straight-line force on the joint.
    6. Ensure your own foot on the attacked leg is pointed away from the opponent, preventing your own ankle from being trapped or twisted.
    7. Maintain a tight grip; your left hand should be firmly on your right bicep, creating a solid lever. Your chest should be close to the opponent's heel.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect hip angle: If your hips are parallel to the opponent's leg, you cannot generate sufficient leverage, and the force is distributed poorly. This can lead to hyperextension of the opponent's knee or uncontrolled twisting of their ankle, potentially damaging ligaments like the ATFL or CFL.
    • Loose grip on the ankle: A "loose" figure-four grip allows the opponent to escape their heel by rotating their foot. This can result in your own ankle being twisted or your calf being squeezed incorrectly, risking ligament damage in your own ankle.
    • Pulling with arms only: Relying solely on arm strength to "pull hard" without hip drive is inefficient and dangerous. It places excessive strain on your shoulders and elbows, and the opponent can easily escape by straightening their leg. The correct motion involves hip extension and leverage, not brute force.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the figure-four grip on your own leg or a training dummy. Focus on the hip-to-chest distance and arm positioning. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): With a willing partner, practice the initial setup and grip from a static position. Partner does not resist. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Practice the full motion of the ankle lock setup and control, with the partner gently resisting movement. Focus on maintaining control throughout. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Apply the ankle lock with a partner who offers mild resistance to escape. Focus on maintaining your hip angle and grip as they try to post or shrimp. (5 reps per side)
    5. Partner drilling (75% resistance): Practice the ankle lock against a partner who actively attempts to defend and escape. Focus on transitioning smoothly and applying pressure correctly. (3 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the ankle lock during controlled sparring, prioritizing technique over submission. Tap early if caught. (3 minutes per person)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established control in the 50-50 guard with your hips perpendicular to your opponent's leg.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you from half guard, creating an opportunity to isolate their heel.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg is extended within your reach.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Straightening the leg: If the opponent attempts to apply the ankle lock, immediately straighten your leg and create space by pushing their hip away with your free hand. This breaks the figure-four grip and relieves pressure.
    • "Spinning out": If the ankle lock is not fully locked, attempt to spin your hips away from the pressure. This often involves turning your body towards the attacked leg and using your free leg to push off the mat to create distance.
    • "Foot sweep" escape: If you feel the initial pressure, try to lift your heel and "sweep" your foot in an arc away from the opponent's chest. This can disrupt their grip and allow you to escape their control.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to ankle lock my opponent?

    Your ankle is likely hurting because you are incorrectly applying pressure, possibly by twisting your own ankle or by not achieving a proper figure-four grip. Ensure your own foot is pointed away from the opponent and your hips are perpendicular to their leg. The pressure should be directed at their heel, not a twisting force on your own joint.

    Q: How can I ankle lock a bigger opponent if they are too strong?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage and precise hip drive are paramount, not strength. Focus on getting your hips completely perpendicular to their leg and maintaining a tight figure-four grip. Your hip extension and pushing your own heel towards your chest will create the necessary mechanical advantage without needing to match their strength.

    Q: Ankle locks are generally not recommended from closed guard for white belts due to the high risk of them escaping and you losing position. The ideal times are when you've transitioned to a position like the 50-50 guard or have isolated an opponent's leg during a guard pass attempt, where you have better control and a clearer angle.
