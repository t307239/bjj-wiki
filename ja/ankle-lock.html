Ankle Lock Technique Guide for BJJ White Belts

BLUE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

The Ankle Lock is a straight-line, non-twisting leg lock targeting the plantar flexion of the foot and the knee. It's often initiated from positional advantages like the 50-50 guard or by isolating a leg from open guard.

White belts often fail due to insufficient control, rushed submissions, or improper hip engagement, leading to ineffective pressure or injury. They may also over-rely on grip strength instead of body mechanics.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to drive forward while securing a strong grip on the opponent's foot and ankle, creating a lever that hyperextends the knee and ankle simultaneously.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Grip: Secure a "figure-four" grip on the opponent's ankle. Your choking arm's forearm presses against the opponent's shin, just above the ankle bone. Your other arm wraps around the opponent's shin, with your hand cupping their heel. Your hands interlock, creating a strong, non-slipping hold on the entire foot and ankle complex.
    2. Positioning: Lie on your back, facing the opponent's leg you intend to attack. Your hips should be positioned directly underneath the opponent's hips, creating a stable base and leverage point.
    3. Leg Placement: Bring the opponent's attacked leg across your body, trapping it between your own legs. Your outside leg (the one not directly involved in the grip) should be positioned behind the opponent's thigh, preventing them from easily extracting their leg.
    4. Hip Drive: Initiate a powerful forward drive with your hips, pushing them towards the opponent's hips. This movement is crucial for generating the necessary torque and pressure.
    5. Core Engagement: Engage your core muscles to maintain a stable torso and transfer power from your hips to the ankle lock. Avoid arching your back excessively.
    6. Choking Arm Extension: As your hips drive forward, extend your choking arm slightly, pushing your forearm into the opponent's shin. This elongates the lever arm.
    7. Ankle Flexion: Simultaneously, pull your heel towards your glutes, forcing the opponent's foot into extreme plantar flexion (pointing toes down). This action stresses the ankle joint.
    8. Knee Extension: The combination of hip drive and ankle flexion will cause the opponent's knee to extend and hyperextend, creating the submission.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Grip and Hip Position: A white belt might grip only the foot and not the entire ankle/shin, or place their hips too far away. This leads to the opponent's ankle slipping out and creates a "dead zone" where no effective pressure can be applied, potentially causing the opponent to stumble or fall awkwardly, risking knee hyperextension for them.
    • Jerking Motion Instead of Smooth Drive: A common error is trying to "pull" the ankle lock with arm strength and jerky movements. This can cause the opponent's knee to twist or hyperextend unnaturally, leading to ACL or meniscus tears for the opponent and a missed submission for the attacker.
    • Allowing Opponent to Turn In: If the white belt fails to control the opponent's hips and allows them to turn their hips into the attacker, the ankle lock is broken. The opponent can then escape, and the attacker's leg is left exposed, potentially to a counter leg lock or a sweep.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Grip Practice: Practice the "figure-four" grip on your own leg, focusing on tightness and comfort. (50 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Solo Hip Drive and Grip: Lie on the mat, practice the figure-four grip, and drive your hips forward, feeling the extension. (50 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partnered Grip and Isolation: With a partner lying on their back, practice isolating one leg and securing the figure-four grip. Partner remains passive. (20 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partnered Hip Drive and Extension (No Submission): From the isolated leg position, practice the hip drive and ankle flexion. Partner gently resists but does not actively defend. (30 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled Submission Attempt: Attempt the Ankle Lock with a partner who is instructed to tap early and safely. Focus on smooth execution and proper hip drive. (15 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling (Controlled): In live rolling, specifically look for opportunities to set up the Ankle Lock. Communicate with your partner if you feel you have a strong position, allowing them to tap safely. (5 minutes, 90% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From 50-50 Guard: When you have secured the opponent's leg in the 50-50 position, controlling their heel and ankle.
    • From Open Guard (e.g., Spider Guard, De La Riva Guard): When you have successfully swept or transitioned to a position where you can isolate and control an opponent's leg.
    • When Opponent is Off-Balance: If an opponent attempts a sweep or pass and their weight distribution is compromised, leaving a leg vulnerable.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • The "Sit-Out" Escape: The opponent, while being attacked, uses their free leg to "sit out" and create space, lifting their hips away from the attacker's hips. This breaks the critical leverage.
    • The "Straighten Leg" Defense: The opponent attempts to straighten their leg by pushing their heel away from their glutes. This reduces the plantar flexion and stress on the ankle.
    • The "Turn In" Escape: The opponent attempts to turn their hips towards the attacker, relieving pressure on the knee and ankle joint. This is often combined with pulling their leg free.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle lock feel weak and my opponent can easily escape?

    Your ankle lock likely feels weak because your hip drive is insufficient, or your grip is not tight enough. Ensure your hips are driving forward directly underneath the opponent's hips, creating a strong fulcrum. Your "figure-four" grip must encompass the entire foot and shin, pressing your forearm into the shin and cupping the heel firmly.

    Q: How can I apply an Ankle Lock against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paramount. Focus on securing a very tight "figure-four" grip and ensuring your hips are positioned directly under their hips to maximize your leverage. Use your entire body weight and a controlled, powerful hip drive rather than relying on brute strength. Prioritize isolating their leg to prevent them from using their weight advantage.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an Ankle Lock and what if I miss it?

    The best time to attempt an Ankle Lock is when you have established positional control and can isolate the opponent's leg, such as in the 50-50 guard or when they attempt a pass. If you miss the Ankle Lock, immediately transition to a defensive posture, such as pulling your legs back to guard or moving to a safer position, to avoid exposing yourself to a counter-attack or submission.
