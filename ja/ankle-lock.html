Ankle Lock BJJ Technique for White Belts

BLUE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

An ankle lock is a submission that targets the opponent's ankle joint, typically applied from a seated or supine position. It aims to hyperextend or invert the ankle, forcing a tap.

White belts often fail by applying excessive, uncontrolled force or by not understanding proper body mechanics. This leads to injury and ineffective submission attempts.

The key insight is to use your hips and body weight to create leverage, isolating the ankle and applying controlled pressure rather than brute strength.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From your guard (closed, open, or half-guard), secure your opponent's ankle by placing your forearm across the front of their shin, just above the ankle bone. Your other arm wraps around the back of their calf, securing a Gable grip (interlocking fingers) or a S-grip (thumb-to-thumb).
    2. Position your hips so they are perpendicular to your opponent's leg, creating a stable base. Your knees should be tucked towards your chest, drawing their leg into your body.
    3. Drive your hips forward and slightly up, keeping your back flat on the mat. This action stretches their leg and begins to isolate the ankle.
    4. Use your forearm on the shin to control the direction of their foot. Your grip on the calf should be tight, preventing them from pulling their foot out.
    5. Lift your hips, creating a fulcrum with your own body. The pressure is applied by extending your hips and pushing your chest towards their knee.
    6. Simultaneously, use your legs to create a frame, preventing them from stacking you. Your feet should be flat on the mat or hooked behind their hips.
    7. Apply slow, steady pressure by extending your hips and arching your back slightly. Focus on the ankle joint's natural range of motion.
    8. Your opponent will tap when they feel discomfort or pain in their ankle joint.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Applying the ankle lock by pulling your opponent's leg directly towards your chest with your arms: This can strain your own shoulders and elbows, and rarely generates sufficient leverage. The correct movement is to drive your hips forward, using your body as a lever.
    • Twisting the opponent's ankle with your hands: This can lead to ligament tears (sprains) in the ankle and knee. The ankle lock should be a straight hyperextension or inversion, not a rotational force.
    • Attempting the ankle lock when your opponent is postured high or standing: This puts you in a dangerous position and risks injury to your own knee or spine. Wait until you have established a stable position where their leg is accessible and controlled.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip and hip movement without a partner, focusing on body positioning. (10 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Static partner drill: With a compliant partner, practice securing the grip and hip drive. Partner remains still. (10 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    3. Controlled leg isolation drill: Partner offers mild resistance to leg extraction. Focus on maintaining the grip and hip pressure. (10 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    4. Entry and finish drill: From a common guard position (e.g., closed guard), practice entering the ankle lock and applying controlled pressure. Partner taps early. (5 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    5. Escapable ankle lock drill: Practice applying the ankle lock with the intention of letting the partner escape if they defend correctly. Focus on the submission mechanics. (5 reps each side, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling with emphasis: During rolling, specifically look for opportunities to attack the ankle lock. Communicate with your partner to ensure safety and tap early. (3 minutes focus, 100% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent extends their leg away from you while you are in guard (closed, open, or half-guard).
    • When you have successfully swept your opponent and their leg is accessible as they try to regain guard.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass that leaves one of their legs exposed and isolated.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stacking: The opponent drives their weight forward and down onto your hips, attempting to compress your body. To counter, maintain a tight frame with your legs and hips, and try to shift your hips to the side to relieve pressure.
    • Leg Extraction/Twisting: The opponent attempts to pull their leg out by straightening it or twisting it away. To counter, maintain a strong gable grip and drive your hips forward to keep their leg taut and controlled.
    • Rolling/Bridging: The opponent attempts to roll you over or bridge to escape. To counter, keep your base wide and stable, and use your arms and legs to maintain control of their body and leg.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle lock not feel strong and my opponent doesn't tap?

    This is usually due to insufficient hip engagement. You are likely pulling with your arms too much instead of driving your hips forward. Ensure your hips are perpendicular to their leg and actively push them forward to create leverage. Your grip on the shin should also be tight, controlling their foot's angle.

    Q: How can I apply an ankle lock against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paramount. Focus on controlling their hips and preventing them from posturing up. Once you have their leg, use your entire body weight by driving your hips forward and arching your back, making their leg your fulcrum. A tight grip on the shin and calf is crucial to prevent them from using their size to escape.

    Q: The optimal time from closed guard is when your opponent tries to pass your guard by extending one of their legs, or when they try to stand up and create space. This exposes their ankle. If they are too close and postured, it's difficult to get the proper leverage. Wait for them to create the opening by extending their leg.
