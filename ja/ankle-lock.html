Ankle Lock: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide to BJJ

BLUE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

This submission targets the ankle joint from various grappling positions, aiming to hyperextend or invert it for a tap.

White belts often fail by muscling the submission, neglecting proper body mechanics and creating dangerous joint stress.

The key insight is using your body weight and leverage to isolate and attack the ankle, not just brute force.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a controlled position (e.g., 50-50 guard), secure the opponent's ankle with your forearm behind their Achilles tendon, thumb up.
    2. Grip your own bicep with your opposite hand, creating a strong "figure-four" grip around the opponent's lower leg.
    3. Position your hips close to the opponent's hip, creating a tight connection and preventing them from creating space.
    4. Lie back, bringing your hips off the mat and anchoring your feet to the opponent's hips or upper thighs with your own feet.
    5. Extend your legs slightly, creating tension in the ankle lock by pushing your hips forward and slightly up.
    6. Maintain a slight knee-on-belly pressure with your upper body if applicable, keeping your chest close to their thigh.
    7. Drive your hips forward in a controlled manner, applying pressure to the ankle joint without twisting or torquing the knee.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly "pulling hard" on the opponent's foot: This can lead to hyperextension of the opponent's ankle or, worse, create a torque on their knee ligament (ACL/MCL) if the leg is not properly isolated.
    • Applying the submission while standing or with poor hip connection: This often results in the opponent easily escaping and can cause you to fall awkwardly, risking your own knee or ankle.
    • Gripping the opponent's shin instead of the heel: This shifts the pressure away from the ankle joint, making the submission ineffective and potentially leading to a "calf slicer" type of injury if pressure is maintained incorrectly.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the "figure-four" grip and hip placement. (50 reps each side)
    2. Partial resistance drilling: With a partner offering minimal resistance, practice securing the ankle and establishing the initial body connection. (20 reps each side)
    3. Controlled submission setup: Partner allows you to fully establish the ankle lock position, but does not resist the tap. Focus on the hip drive. (30 reps each side)
    4. Light resistance rolling: Roll with a partner who understands the submission and will tap early. Focus on smooth transitions and proper application. (5 minutes, 25% resistance)
    5. Moderate resistance rolling: Roll with a partner who offers more realistic defense but still taps to avoid injury. Focus on recognizing opportunities and executing the lock. (5 minutes, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the ankle lock into sparring with partners of similar skill and weight, prioritizing safety and technique over winning. (10 minutes, 75% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent's foot is accessible from 50-50 guard, and you can secure their ankle with your forearm.
    • During transitions where your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their foot becomes exposed.
    • When you have established a strong base and control from a position like deep-half guard, allowing you to isolate a leg.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Stomp" defense: The defender forcefully stomps their heel into the mat, using the mat's resistance to prevent their ankle from being controlled. The attacker must release or risk a painful ankle injury to themselves.
    • "Spinning out" defense: The defender uses their free leg to "spin" their hips away from the attacker's body, creating space and breaking the critical hip-to-hip connection. This immediately relieves pressure.
    • "Bridging and shrimping" defense: The defender attempts to bridge their hips upwards and shrimp their body away, simultaneously trying to pull their trapped foot free. This requires explosive movement and good timing.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle lock feel weak and I can't get a tap, even when I'm pulling hard?

    You are likely not achieving the correct biomechanical isolation of the ankle joint. Ensure your forearm is deep behind the opponent's Achilles tendon and your grip is tight on your bicep. Crucially, your hips must be pressed firmly against their hips, and you need to extend your legs to create a straight line of force, driving your hips forward, not just pulling.

    Q: How can I apply an Ankle Lock in BJJ against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Focus on securing their ankle with extreme precision, ensuring your forearm is deep and your grip is locked. Lie back fully, anchoring your feet to their hips, and drive your hips forward with all your weight. The goal is to use their size against them by creating a fulcrum at their hip and applying pressure directly to their ankle, not trying to overpower them.

    Q: Is it safe to do Ankle Locks as a white belt, and when should I start trying them?

    Ankle locks are considered a relatively safe leg lock when applied with proper technique and control. As a white belt, you should focus on understanding the biomechanics and practicing with compliant partners or instructors. Start by drilling the mechanics solo and then with light resistance, only attempting them in live rolling when you have a solid grasp of the setup and execution, prioritizing your partner's safety.
