Sitting Guard is a dynamic open guard position initiated from a seated stance, aiming to control distance and off-balance opponents for sweeps and submissions.

White belts often struggle by sitting too upright, losing their base, or relying on brute strength instead of leverage and structure.

The core principle is using your legs as flexible frames to manage distance and create angles for attack, not just as a passive barrier.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Start seated with knees bent, feet flat on the mat, shins vertical. Hips are slightly tucked, creating a neutral or posterior pelvic tilt.
    2. Grip opponent's ankles or pant cuffs with both hands, thumbs inside the cuff if possible. Elbows are tucked into your sides.
    3. Shift your weight slightly back onto your hips, lowering your center of gravity. Your back should remain relatively straight, avoiding excessive rounding.
    4. Extend one leg, placing the foot on the opponent's hip or thigh, creating a frame. The knee points slightly outward, not directly at the opponent.
    5. Simultaneously, bring the opposite knee towards your chest, creating a second frame with your shin and thigh. This knee should point slightly inward.
    6. Maintain constant pressure with your feet against the opponent's hips/thighs to prevent them from closing the distance.
    7. If the opponent is standing, use your grips to pull them forward and off-balance. Your hips remain anchored to the mat.
    8. To transition to a sweep, drive your foot into the hip while simultaneously pulling with your grips and rocking your hips.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly extending legs too straight and rigid: Can lead to knee hyperextension or ligament tears (ACL/MCL) if the opponent applies sudden pressure or a leg drag pass.
    • Rounding the back excessively to create space: Puts undue stress on the lumbar spine, risking disc herniation or muscle strain.
    • Gripping only the opponent's feet without pant control: Allows the opponent to easily pull their feet out, losing your primary control and leaving your legs exposed to passes.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the seated posture, leg extensions, and frame creation without a partner. Focus on hip angle and weight distribution. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Practice the initial setup and leg frame placement with a compliant partner. Focus on grip accuracy and frame strength. (25 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light pressure): Partner gently pushes forward. Practice maintaining frames and hip connection. (25 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (simulated pass): Partner attempts a simple guard pass (e.g., knee slice). Practice using frames to create space and recover guard. (25 reps per side)
    5. Flow drill: Transition between Sitting Guard and other open guards (e.g., Spider Guard) with a partner, focusing on smooth movement and grip changes. (5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (50% intensity): Apply Sitting Guard against a resisting opponent. Focus on maintaining posture, frames, and attempting sweeps. (3 rounds)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is posturing up from your closed guard and you want to open it to create space.
    • When the opponent is standing in front of you and you want to establish a controlling guard.
    • When you are transitioning from another guard and want to reset to a stable seated position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Pass Defense: If the opponent attempts to stack you, widen your base, drive your feet into their hips, and use your grips to pull them forward and to the side, avoiding being flattened.
    • Leg Drag Defense: If the opponent grabs a leg for a leg drag, immediately bring your other knee to your chest, create a strong frame with that leg, and use your free hand to push their hip away.
    • Knee Slice Pass Defense: If the opponent attempts a knee slice, use your frames to create distance. Drive your foot into their hip while pulling their arm across your body to disrupt their pressure.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to extend my leg in Sitting Guard?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are hyperextending it, forcing the joint beyond its natural range of motion. Instead of locking your knee straight, maintain a slight bend and focus on using your quadriceps and hip flexors to create a strong, active frame. Ensure your foot is placed on the opponent's hip or thigh, not their shin, which allows for better leverage and reduces direct pressure on your knee joint.

    Q: How can I use Sitting Guard effectively against a much bigger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your Sitting Guard must prioritize distance management and off-balancing. Focus on using your feet as wide, stable frames against their hips and thighs to prevent them from collapsing your guard. Employ controlled, sharp pulls with your grips to disrupt their base, and be ready to shift your hips laterally to create angles for sweeps. Never let them establish heavy pressure directly on your chest or hips; use your frames to create space and maintain mobility.

    Q: Should I always keep my back completely straight in Sitting Guard, or is a slight curve okay?

    While you should avoid excessive rounding, a very slight, natural curve in your lower back is acceptable and can help you maintain a lower center of gravity. The key is to avoid a pronounced C-curve which stresses the spine. Aim for a neutral or slightly tucked pelvic tilt, keeping your core engaged. This posture allows your hips to act as a stable base while your legs provide the dynamic control and frames.
