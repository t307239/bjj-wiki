Sitting Guard is initiated from a seated position, facing your opponent, to control distance and create sweeping or submission opportunities.

Many white belts struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage, leading to being easily passed or swept.

The key insight is using your legs as a flexible frame to prevent your opponent from closing the distance and establishing control.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Start seated with knees bent, feet flat on the mat, hips slightly off the mat.
    2. Grip your opponent's ankles with your hands, palms facing each other, thumbs on the inside of their ankles.
    3. Maintain a slight bend in your elbows, creating a frame with your forearms against their shins.
    4. Keep your back straight and chest up, preventing your opponent from collapsing your posture.
    5. Shift your weight to one hip, creating an angle that makes it harder for them to drive forward.
    6. Use your feet to push against their hips or thighs, maintaining a consistent distance.
    7. As they advance, adjust your hip angle and leg extension to block their forward momentum.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Gripping too low on the shin: This allows the opponent to easily step over your legs, risking a knee hyperextension injury (ACL/MCL) as your leg is trapped and forced backward. Always grip the ankle bone area.
    • Collapsing posture: Leaning forward with a rounded back invites the opponent to stack you, potentially causing lumbar spine compression or disc injury. Maintain an upright posture with your chest lifted.
    • Extending legs fully without engagement: This leaves your legs vulnerable to being attacked or used for a pass, risking hamstring strains or groin pulls. Keep a slight bend in your knees and actively use your feet to create frames.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice sitting up, gripping imaginary ankles, and shifting hip angles for 5 minutes (0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill: Sit guard against a compliant partner who simply stands and walks forward. Focus on maintaining distance and frames for 5 rounds (25% resistance).
    3. Partner drill: Partner attempts a simple knee-slice pass. Focus on maintaining frames and hip angles to prevent the pass for 5 rounds (50% resistance).
    4. Partner drill: Partner attempts a guard pass from a standing position. Focus on maintaining frames, hip angles, and using foot pushes to prevent penetration for 5 rounds (75% resistance).
    5. Live rolling: Start in sitting guard and focus on maintaining the guard and preventing passes for 3 rounds (90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Full rolling. Focus on applying the principles of sitting guard against an uncooperative opponent for 5 rounds (100% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is standing and trying to establish a passing position.
    • When you are on your back and your opponent is in front of you, trying to advance past your legs.
    • When you want to create distance and control from a seated position before initiating a sweep or submission.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Pass Defense: If the opponent attempts to stack pass, immediately shift your weight to one hip, extend the opposite leg to block their hip, and pull their head down with your free arm to break their posture.
    • Toreando Pass Defense: When the opponent tries to circle around your legs, use your foot pushes on their hips to maintain distance and pivot your hips to face them, keeping your legs between you.
    • Knee Slice Pass Defense: If the opponent drives a knee across your body, use your opposite leg to hook their hip and your gripping leg to push their knee away, creating a frame to prevent the slice.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to grip in Sitting Guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping too tightly or with an unnatural angle. Ensure your grip is firm but not strained. Try to keep your wrists straight and avoid bending them backward or inward. Focus on gripping the ankle bone area firmly with your fingers, using your thumbs to secure the grip, which distributes pressure more evenly.

    Q: How do I maintain Sitting Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and angles are crucial. Focus on using your legs to create frames and keep them at bay; don't let them get their weight on top of you. Use your hip mobility to constantly adjust your angles, making it difficult for them to generate forward pressure. Employ strong foot pushes against their hips or thighs to maintain distance.

    Q: Sitting Guard is excellent for controlling distance and setting up sweeps, but it can be vulnerable if the opponent consistently penetrates your frames. If your opponent is successfully bypassing your leg frames and establishing a strong pressure pass, consider transitioning to a half guard or butterfly guard where you can more effectively tie up their legs and regain control.
