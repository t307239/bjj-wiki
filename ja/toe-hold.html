The Toe Hold is a leg lock applied to the opponent's foot, primarily from positions like the 50-50 guard or when the opponent is on their back.

It aims to hyperextend the metatarsals or cause internal rotation of the ankle, forcing a submission.

White belts often fail by applying excessive, uncontrolled force, leading to injury rather than a tap.

The key mechanical insight is to control the opponent's foot and ankle with your entire body, creating leverage through hip pressure and precise grip placement.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Grip the opponent's foot: Place your forearm across the top of their foot, securing their toes between your elbow and forearm.
    2. Secure the heel: With your other hand, grip the opponent's heel, ensuring your thumb is on the sole of their foot and fingers wrap around the Achilles tendon.
    3. Position your hips: Turn your hips away from the opponent, creating a perpendicular angle between your torso and their leg.
    4. Transfer weight: Drive your hips forward and slightly downward, perpendicular to the opponent's leg.
    5. Apply pressure: Use your forearm to push the opponent's shin forward, while your heel grip pulls their heel backward.
    6. Control the ankle: Ensure your grip on the heel prevents any rotation or movement of the opponent's ankle.
    7. Lock the structure: Maintain a strong connection between your forearm on their foot and your heel grip, creating a stable lever.
    8. Extend the foot: Slowly and deliberately extend your hips, hyperextending the opponent's foot and ankle in the direction of the toes.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Applying the Toe Hold by "pulling hard" on the opponent's foot without establishing proper biomechanical control: This can cause severe ligamentous tears in the ankle (e.g., ATFL, CFL) due to uncontrolled inversion or eversion.
    • Trying to "crank" the submission by rotating your hips aggressively before securing the heel grip: This can lead to ankle sprains or even fractures from sudden, sharp forces.
    • Over-extending the ankle by pushing the opponent's foot too far, too fast, without feeling resistance: This risks tearing the plantar fascia or causing metatarsal fractures from excessive dorsiflexion.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Drill: Practice the grip sequence and hip positioning without a partner. Focus on the feeling of securing the heel and foot. (50 reps)
    2. Partner Drill (0% Resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence. The partner offers no resistance, allowing you to focus on mechanics. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner Drill (25% Resistance): The partner offers slight resistance to leg movement, forcing you to adjust your grips and hip pressure. (15 reps per side)
    4. Partner Drill (50% Resistance): The partner attempts to defend by pulling their leg away or rotating their ankle. Focus on maintaining your control. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partner Drill (75% Resistance): The partner actively tries to escape the submission using basic defensive movements. You must adapt your technique to maintain the hold. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling (Controlled): Attempt the Toe Hold in a live roll scenario, but verbally tap out if you feel any discomfort or if your partner is in a compromised position. Focus on timing and transition. (3-5 attempts per roll)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • From the 50-50 guard, when you have established control of the opponent's leg and can isolate their foot.
    • When the opponent attempts a guard pass and their leg becomes accessible for a leg entanglement.
    • From a scramble where you have managed to secure one of the opponent's legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Ankle rotation: The opponent attempts to rotate their ankle inward or outward, away from the direction of the Toe Hold pressure. This disengages the lever.
    • Leg escape by bridging/shrimping: The opponent uses their free leg to push off the mat and bridge their hips away, creating space to pull their trapped leg free.
    • Switching to a different leg lock: If the Toe Hold is not fully secured, the opponent can transition to a different submission or escape by changing their body angle.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try the Toe Hold, even if my partner doesn't tap?

    This often occurs because you are applying pressure to the ligaments of the ankle itself, rather than creating a fulcrum with your body and the opponent's foot. Ensure your forearm is across the top of their foot, pressing their toes into your forearm, and your heel grip is firm. The pressure should be on the entire foot, not just the ankle bones.

    Q: How can I apply a Toe Hold against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paramount. Focus on establishing a strong 50-50 guard or similar entanglement to control their base. Ensure your hips are positioned perpendicularly to their leg, and drive your hips forward to create the hyperextension. Do not rely on brute strength; use your body weight and the opponent's own limb against them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Toe Hold if I'm a white belt?

    As a white belt, the Toe Hold should only be attempted when you have a very clear and stable opportunity, such as a perfectly established 50-50 guard where the opponent's foot is completely isolated. Avoid attempting it during chaotic scrambles or when the opponent is actively defending. Prioritize learning the mechanics safely through drills before attempting it live.
