Precise Toe Hold Technique for BJJ White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

The Toe Hold is a leg lock targeting the ankle, applied from positions like the 50-50 guard or when isolating an opponent's leg.

White belts often struggle by applying excessive, uncontrolled force, leading to injury and missed submissions due to poor mechanics.

The key is to create a fulcrum with your body, using leverage to hyperextend the ankle, not brute strength.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Grip: Secure your opponent's foot with your near-side arm, palm facing up, fingers gripping the toes and ball of the foot. Your thumb should be on the sole side.
    2. Control Arm: Place your other arm under the opponent's calf, cupping the back of their knee. This arm is the fulcrum.
    3. Hip Angle: Rotate your hips 90 degrees away from the opponent's hips, creating a perpendicular line.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your hips and the mat, driving your chest towards your opponent's knee.
    5. Leg Position: Your legs should form a frame, with your controlling leg (under the calf) extended and your other leg bent, trapping the opponent's thigh.
    6. Ankle Compression: Squeeze your knees together, drawing your controlling leg's shin towards your opponent's ankle.
    7. Finish: Extend your hips and apply upward pressure with your chest, forcing hyperextension of the opponent's ankle joint.
    8. Release: Immediately loosen pressure and release the grip upon tap or verbal tap.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Applying pressure by pulling the foot upwards with the grip arm: This directly stresses the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) of the ankle, leading to sprains or tears. The correct movement is to create leverage by driving your body weight and using your other arm as a fulcrum.
    • Rotating the hips too early or too far: This misaligns the body and can cause the knee joint to torque unnaturally, risking collateral ligament damage. Maintain a controlled 90-degree hip turn relative to the opponent's hips.
    • Attempting to 'crank' the foot with the grip arm: This puts immense shearing force on the ankle bones and ligaments, leading to fractures or severe sprains. Focus on extending your hips and chest to create a controlled hyperextension.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Grip and Body Positioning Drill: Practice securing the grip and achieving the correct hip angle and leg frame without a partner. (50 reps)
    2. Mirror Drill with Partner (No Resistance): Partner lies on their back, you practice the grip, hip turn, and body position. Partner offers no resistance. (20 reps each side)
    3. Controlled Leg Isolation Drill: From a stable position (e.g., 50-50 guard), partner allows you to isolate their leg and practice the initial grip and body framing. (25% resistance) (15 reps)
    4. Toe Hold Setup and Control Drill: Practice setting up the Toe Hold and achieving the correct body mechanics from a specific starting position (e.g., from passing). Partner offers light resistance to leg movement. (50% resistance) (10 reps)
    5. Submission Application Drill: Practice applying the full Toe Hold mechanics. Partner taps early and clearly. Focus on control and proper movement. (75% resistance) (5 reps)
    6. Live Rolling with Focus: Implement the Toe Hold during rolling, but only when the opportunity arises naturally. Focus on proper setup and execution, prioritizing safety. (90% resistance) (3-5 mins)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent's leg is extended and isolated, particularly in positions like the 50-50 guard or when they attempt a guard pass and their leg is exposed.
    • During transitions where an opponent's ankle is presented, such as when they are trying to recover guard or change their base.
    • When you have established a strong control of one of the opponent's legs, preventing them from easily withdrawing it.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Withdrawal and Base Adjustment: The opponent can attempt to withdraw their leg by straightening their knee and moving their hips away, while simultaneously adjusting their base to prevent a stable grip.
    • Hip Escape and Framing: If the Toe Hold is being applied, the opponent can hip escape to create space and use their free leg or arms to frame against the attacker's hips and chest, disrupting the leverage.
    • Re-Grip and Counter-Attack: A skilled opponent might use the pressure to their advantage, attempting to re-grip the attacker's leg or transition to a different submission, like an ankle lock or a sweep, by exploiting the attacker's commitment to the Toe Hold.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do the Toe Hold?

    Your ankle likely hurts because you are pulling on the foot with your grip arm instead of using your body weight to create leverage. This puts direct strain on the ankle ligaments. The correct biomechanics involve using your other arm to cup the back of the opponent's knee as a fulcrum, and driving your hips and chest into their leg to hyperextend the ankle joint, not pulling the foot.

    Q: How can I apply a Toe Hold against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise biomechanics are paramount. Focus on isolating their ankle completely and ensuring your hips are perpendicular to theirs to maximize your body weight's effectiveness. Do not attempt to overpower them; instead, use your arms to secure their foot and your torso to drive into their calf and knee, creating a strong fulcrum point for the submission. Ensure your control is tight before initiating the final hip extension.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Toe Hold in BJJ?

    The Toe Hold is most effectively applied when an opponent's leg is isolated and extended, such as in the 50-50 guard or when they are attempting a guard pass and their ankle becomes vulnerable. It's also a good option during transitions where an opponent's leg is exposed, preventing them from easily withdrawing it. Avoid attempting it when the opponent's legs are tightly controlled or when their hips are mobile enough to easily counter.
