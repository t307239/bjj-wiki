BJJ Frame: The Essential Defensive Tool for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Frame is a defensive posture used to create space between your body and an opponent's, typically from guard positions.

It prevents the opponent from collapsing your posture and executing submissions or passes.

White belts often fail by using their arms passively, leading to immediate pressure and submission attempts.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, establish forearm-to-hip contact with your opponent. Your elbows should be tucked inside their thighs, not flared out.
    2. Flex your biceps and triceps to create a rigid lever. Your forearms should be perpendicular to your upper arms, forming a 90-degree angle.
    3. Maintain a neutral pelvis, with your hips slightly elevated off the mat. Avoid letting your hips collapse towards your opponent.
    4. Your knees should be driving slightly upwards and outwards, maintaining a wide base.
    5. If the opponent attempts to pass, extend your arms, pushing their hips away. This action uses your entire arm structure as a unit.
    6. Simultaneously, shrimp your hips back to increase the distance, re-establishing your guard.
    7. Grip: Forearm on opponent's hip bone or upper thigh. Secondary grip: Sleeve or collar to control their posture.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect elbow flare: When elbows are flared out (away from the body), the shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) is exposed to excessive external rotation and abduction, risking a rotator cuff tear.

    The correct movement involves keeping elbows tucked, creating a strong, stable lever.

    • Passive arm extension: Simply pushing with straight, limp arms allows the opponent to easily grip and manipulate your limbs, risking elbow hyperextension injuries.

    The correct frame utilizes active tension in the entire arm, creating a solid structure.

    • Hip collapse: Allowing your hips to sink towards the opponent's body removes your base and allows them to establish dominant control, risking knee or ankle sprains during subsequent sweeps or passes.

    Maintain hip elevation and posterior pelvic tilt.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice creating the frame against an imaginary opponent, focusing on elbow tuck and forearm-to-hip contact. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice establishing frames and pushing them away, focusing on body mechanics. (10 reps each side)
    3. Light pressure drilling: Partner applies minimal pressure. Practice holding the frame and resisting their initial forward movement. (2 minutes)
    4. Guard retention drilling: Partner attempts to break your closed guard and pass. You focus solely on maintaining frames and shrimping to recover guard. (3 minutes)
    5. Positional sparring (guard retention): Start from closed guard. Partner attempts to pass. You focus on frames and hip escapes to prevent the pass. (5 minutes, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (guard focus): During rolling, actively prioritize maintaining frames when in guard and creating space. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is inside your guard and attempting to collapse your posture.
    • When your opponent is attempting to establish a dominant grip on your collar or sleeves.
    • As a preemptive measure when you feel your opponent beginning to drive forward into your guard.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Against a stack pass: Extend both arms simultaneously, pushing the opponent's chest/shoulders away while shrimping your hips back. The frame creates space for your legs to re-establish guard.
    • Against a knee-slice pass: Use one forearm to frame against the opponent's hip or thigh on the side of their slicing knee, preventing them from advancing. Extend the other arm to create distance.
    • Against posture control: If the opponent grabs your collar, use your forearms to push their biceps or elbows away, breaking their grip and creating space to improve your guard.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to Frame in BJJ?

    Wrist pain during framing often indicates hyperextension of the wrist joint. This happens when you push with your hand flat instead of creating a rigid lever with your entire forearm. Ensure your forearm is perpendicular to your hand, and maintain a slight flexion in your wrist, as if you are gripping something firmly, to protect the joint.

    Q: How do I Frame against a much bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your frame must be biomechanically sound. Focus on engaging your entire arm as a unit, not just pushing with your hands. Utilize your hips and legs to generate power by shrimping away, creating space. Think of your arms as structural beams, not flexible noodles, and use your body weight to support the frame.

    Q: When should I stop using Frame and try to sweep in BJJ?

    The frame is primarily a defensive tool to create space and prevent passes, not a direct offensive weapon. You should transition from framing to sweeping when you have successfully created sufficient space and control. For example, after framing to stop a pass, use that created distance to establish a sweep entry like a scissor sweep or hip bump sweep.
