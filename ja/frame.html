BJJ Frame: The Essential Defensive Posture for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The frame is a defensive posture used from guard, primarily closed guard, to prevent an opponent from passing by creating space and leverage.

White belts often fail by collapsing their frames, allowing the opponent to close the distance and negate the defensive advantage.

The key is to maintain rigid, extended limbs to create a solid barrier, like a wall, that the opponent cannot easily breach.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Starting Position: Assume closed guard, knees hugged to chest, hips slightly elevated off the mat.
    2. Arm Placement: Extend both forearms and hands towards the opponent's hips or upper thighs, palms facing them.
    3. Elbow Position: Keep elbows slightly bent, not locked out, to absorb impact and maintain tension.
    4. Forearm Angle: Angle forearms outwards at approximately 45 degrees from the midline of your body.
    5. Grip (Optional but Recommended): For stronger frames, grip opponent's sleeves near the elbow or their gi pants at the thigh.
    6. Core Engagement: Brace your core, drawing your belly button towards your spine to create a stable trunk.
    7. Weight Distribution: Distribute your weight evenly through your hips and glutes, keeping your base wide.
    8. Pushing Action: Apply a constant, controlled pushing pressure with your forearms into the opponent's hips or thighs.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Movement: Locking elbows straight and pushing with flattened hands. Injury Risk: Hyperextension of elbow joints, leading to ligament sprains (UCL/MCL).

    Correct Alternative: Maintain a slight bend in the elbow and push with the fleshy part of the forearm.

    • Incorrect Movement: Allowing hips to collapse and bringing knees too close to the chest without forearm extension. Injury Risk: Compression injuries to the lumbar spine and potential knee joint strain from uncontrolled pressure.

    Correct Alternative: Actively extend forearms to create space while maintaining a slight posterior pelvic tilt.

    • Incorrect Movement: Gripping too tightly with fingers only, leading to wrist strain. Injury Risk: Wrist sprains and potential carpal tunnel irritation from sustained, rigid grip.

    Correct Alternative: Use a broader grip on sleeves or pants, engaging the forearm and elbow for power transfer.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Drill (0% Resistance): Practice extending arms and legs, engaging the core, and creating a pushing sensation against the air for 30 seconds, 5 reps.
    2. Partner Drill - Static Frame (25% Resistance): Partner lies on their back. You establish closed guard and practice framing with correct arm and hip angles. Partner gently tries to close the distance. 30 seconds, 5 reps.
    3. Partner Drill - Gentle Push (50% Resistance): You frame from closed guard. Partner gently pushes into your frame. Focus on maintaining the frame and resisting the forward pressure. 30 seconds, 5 reps.
    4. Partner Drill - Controlled Advance (75% Resistance): You frame from closed guard. Partner attempts a slow, controlled guard pass (e.g., hip bump). You use your frame to create space and prevent them from flattening you out. 30 seconds, 5 reps.
    5. Live Rolling - Defensive Focus (90% Resistance): In sparring, make it a goal to maintain your frame from closed guard for the first 30 seconds of every round. Focus on not letting the opponent establish dominant passing grips.
    6. Live Rolling - Integrated Frame (100% Resistance): Use the frame as a natural defensive reaction whenever an opponent tries to pass your guard. Focus on the biomechanics learned in drilling.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent is attempting to pass your guard, particularly from closed guard or when they are inside your guard.
    • When an opponent tries to "smash" your guard by driving their weight directly forward.
    • As a fundamental defensive posture to create space and reset your guard if it's being compromised.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention (using the frame): Maintain rigid forearms against the opponent's hips/thighs. Apply a controlled outward push, widening your base and slightly lifting your hips to create space, preventing them from flattening you.
    • Sweeping (using the frame): Once space is created with the frame, transition to a sweep by posting one hand on the mat, elevating your hips, and swinging your legs to unbalance and invert the opponent.
    • Guard Reset: If the opponent penetrates your frame, use a strong push to create immediate space, then quickly bring your knees back to your chest to re-establish closed guard or transition to a different guard.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to frame in BJJ?

    Wrist pain during framing often occurs from gripping too tightly with just your fingers or by locking your elbows straight. This puts excessive torque on the wrist joint. To fix this, ensure you are gripping with the fleshy part of your forearm and maintain a slight bend at the elbow. Distribute the pushing force across the entire forearm, not just the wrist.

    Q: How do I frame against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your frame needs to be more about leverage and creating space than brute force. Focus on extending your arms and legs maximally to create a barrier. Use your hips to actively push outwards, widening your base and tilting your pelvis to make yourself a harder target to compress. Gripping their sleeves near the elbow can also give you more control and leverage.

    Q: When should I stop framing and try to sweep or submit in BJJ?

    Framing is primarily a defensive tool to maintain distance and prevent a pass. You should transition from framing when you have successfully created significant space and the opponent is off-balance or has lost their dominant passing position. This is your opportunity to initiate a sweep by using that created space to elevate your hips or to transition to a submission if they overcommit to a pass.
