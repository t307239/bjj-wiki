The Essential BJJ Frame for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Frame is a defensive posture used from guards like closed guard or half guard to create space and prevent an opponent's forward pressure. It is crucial for maintaining distance and controlling the engagement.

White belts often fail by relying on weak muscle tension or incorrect limb positioning, which is easily overpowered. This leads to conceding dominant positions and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is using your bones and structural alignment to create a rigid barrier, not just muscle. This leverages leverage over brute strength.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, extend both arms with elbows slightly bent, forearms parallel to the mat. Maintain a neutral spine, hips slightly elevated.
    2. Grip the opponent's collar (lapel) with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel, fingers outside. Your left hand grips the opponent's opposite sleeve near the wrist.
    3. Create a slight outward pushing force with your forearms, driving your elbows away from your body while keeping them bent at approximately 30 degrees.
    4. Simultaneously, push your hips back and down, creating a slight gap between your pelvis and the opponent's torso.
    5. Ensure your knees are driving inwards towards your chest, creating a strong base and preventing the opponent from passing.
    6. Maintain a slight, constant tension in your arms and legs, like a spring. Your weight should be distributed evenly over your hips.
    7. If the opponent closes the distance, you can use a 'frame and shrimp' motion: extend your frames to create space, then shrimp your hips out to re-establish distance.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Extending arms fully straight: This hyperextends the elbow joint, risking ligament tears (ulnar collateral ligament). Always maintain a slight bend.
    • Gripping too wide or too narrow: A grip too wide makes the frame weak; too narrow can invite armbars. Grip the lapel near the collarbone and the sleeve at the wrist.
    • Rounding the back (hunching): This compromises spinal alignment and reduces the structural integrity of the frame, making it easier to break. Maintain a neutral, slightly arched back.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the frame extension and retraction motion with no partner, focusing on limb angles and hip movement. (10 reps).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Partner stands, you are in closed guard. Practice establishing grips and pushing away with frames, partner offers no resistance. (10 reps).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner attempts to step forward, you use frames to maintain distance. Partner offers 25% resistance. (5 reps each side).
    4. Partner drill (controlled pressure): Partner attempts to break posture from closed guard, you use frames to prevent them from getting their hips close. Partner offers 50% resistance. (5 reps).
    5. Flow drill (closed guard retention): Start in closed guard, partner attempts to pass. You use frames and hip movement to maintain closed guard. Partner offers 75% resistance. (2 minutes).
    6. Live rolling (closed guard focus): Implement frames defensively during sparring. Focus on maintaining closed guard against initial passes. (3 minutes, 90% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent is in your closed guard and attempting to break your posture.
    • When an opponent is pressuring forward in your half guard, trying to flatten you out.
    • As a primary defensive tool to create space after a failed sweep or submission attempt.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent posts on your hip: Immediately shrimp your hips away and re-establish your frames on their shoulders or chest. Your frame is broken, so prioritize creating space and resetting.
    • Opponent steps their hips in deep: Use a powerful frame extension on their shoulders while simultaneously hip bumping them backwards. This breaks their base and allows you to regain guard.
    • Opponent uses a 'stack' pass: Brace your frames against their chest and shoulder, then use your legs to drive your hips back, creating a wedge to push them off.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why do my elbows hurt when I try to frame in BJJ?

    Elbow pain usually stems from over-extension or excessive muscle tension. Ensure you always maintain a slight bend in your elbows (around 30 degrees) and focus on using your bone structure for rigidity, not just muscle. Think of it as creating a stable, unyielding structure rather than forcefully pushing.

    Q: How do I frame against a much bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, biomechanical leverage is paramount. Focus on precise grip placement on their collar and sleeve, and use your hips to create the initial space. Once a small gap is established, extend your frames with your bones aligned to create a strong, rigid barrier. Think of it as using a lever to push a heavy object, rather than trying to muscle it.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Frame in BJJ, and when should I stop?

    The Frame is primarily a defensive tool to create space and prevent your opponent from advancing their position, especially from guard. You should stop using it if your frames are consistently being bypassed or broken; this indicates you need to adjust your positioning or grips. If the opponent has already established a dominant passing position, focusing on frames might be less effective than other defensive maneuvers.
