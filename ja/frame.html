BJJ フレーム：白帯のための必須ディフェンス

The Frame is a defensive posture used to create space and prevent an opponent from advancing their position, typically from guard or half-guard.

White belts often fail by collapsing their frames, losing structure, and allowing immediate pressure.

The core principle is using your limbs as levers to push against your opponent's body, maintaining a stable base.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Assume a seated or kneeling guard position, with your back on the mat.
    2. Grip your opponent's collar (lapel) with one hand, specifically the fabric near the sternum.
    3. Grip your opponent's sleeve with the opposite hand, just above the elbow joint.
    4. Place your forearms and hands against your opponent's biceps or shoulders, creating a rigid structure.
    5. Keep your elbows bent at approximately 90 degrees, forming a strong, triangular shape with your arms.
    6. Push your hips away from your opponent, creating a slight angle and extending your legs to increase distance.
    7. Maintain a wide base with your feet on the mat or hips engaged, distributing your weight evenly.
    8. Your head should be up, looking at your opponent, not down at your lap.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect grip on the sleeve: Gripping the wrist or hand allows the opponent to easily break your structure and pass your guard. This can lead to wrist hyperextension, damaging the carpal bones and ligaments.
    • Collapsing the elbows: Allowing your elbows to bend too much or tuck in creates a weak structure, making you vulnerable to pressure passes like the stack pass. This can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears.
    • Pushing with straight arms: Extending your arms fully and pushing can make you unstable and easily swept or controlled. This can overextend the elbow joint, risking ligamentous damage.
    • Slouching the back: A rounded back reduces your base and leverage, making it easy for opponents to control your posture and pass. This can strain the lumbar spine and lead to disc issues.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the frame position with no resistance, focusing on grip placement and elbow angles for 2 minutes.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner stands in front of you. Practice establishing the frame with specified grips and maintaining it for 30 seconds, then reset. 5 repetitions.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner gently tries to close the distance or advance. You maintain the frame and create space by pushing. 5 repetitions, 25% resistance.
    4. Partner drill (controlled pressure): Your partner applies light pressure to advance. You focus on using the frame to push them back and reset your guard. 5 repetitions, 50% resistance.
    5. Positional sparring: Start in closed guard or half-guard. Your partner attempts guard passes, and you focus solely on establishing and maintaining a frame to prevent advances. 3 rounds, 2 minutes each, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Implement the frame as a primary defensive tool throughout the roll, focusing on maintaining structure and creating space. 1 round, 5 minutes, 90% resistance.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • When your opponent is inside your guard and trying to break your posture or establish side control.
    • When you are in half-guard and your opponent is trying to flatten you out or pass to side control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention: Use the frame to create space and re-establish your guard by pushing your opponent's hips away and bringing your knees back to your chest.
    • Sweep: Once space is created, use the frame to off-balance your opponent and execute a sweep (e.g., scissor sweep, hip bump sweep).
    • Posture Control: Use the frame to prevent your opponent from breaking your posture, allowing you to set up submissions or transition to a better guard.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to Frame in BJJ?

    Wrist pain during framing often stems from gripping too tightly or with a bent wrist. Ensure your wrist is straight and neutral, and that your grip on the sleeve is firm but not tense. Focus on pushing with your forearms and maintaining a stable structure, which transfers the force to your stronger shoulder and elbow joints, not your delicate wrist.

    Q: How do I Frame against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, the Frame becomes even more critical for leverage. Focus on using your entire body to create the frame, not just your arms. Engage your core, push with your hips, and use the angle of your legs to create space. Grip choice is also key; aim for the opponent's biceps or shoulders to maximize your leverage against their mass.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Frame versus just trying to pull guard back?

    The Frame is not about 'pulling guard back' but about actively maintaining distance and preventing the pass. Use the Frame immediately when your opponent enters your guard or attempts to advance from half-guard. It’s a proactive defense, not a reactive pull. If you wait too long, the opponent's weight and pressure will make framing ineffective.
