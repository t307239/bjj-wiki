Master the Technical Stand-Up in BJJ: A White Belt's Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Technical Stand-Up is a fundamental transition from a grounded position to standing, typically from guard or bottom half-guard. It allows you to escape bad positions and create offensive opportunities.

White belts often struggle with this due to a lack of core engagement and improper weight distribution, leading to instability and injury. They tend to push with their hands instead of driving with their hips.

The key mechanical insight is to use your hips to elevate your base, creating a stable platform before transferring weight to your feet, like a controlled elevator ascent.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From bottom guard (e.g., closed guard), establish a collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (same side) with your left hand. Your opponent is sitting or kneeling.
    2. Bring your left knee up, foot flat on the mat, creating a 90-degree angle at the knee. Your right leg remains extended or bent slightly.
    3. Simultaneously, drive your hips UP and slightly FORWARD, creating space by pushing your opponent's shoulders away with your forearms.
    4. As your hips rise, plant your right foot firmly on the mat, aligning it with your left knee to form a stable base.
    5. Transfer your weight onto your feet, keeping your hips elevated and your base wide. Your back should be straight, not rounded.
    6. Drive your left foot forward, stepping past your opponent's hip line, while maintaining pressure on their shoulders with your grips.
    7. Once your left foot is past their hip, stand up fully, keeping your base wide and maintaining control of the grips. Your right leg follows.
    8. Release grips only when fully stable and ready to engage in the next phase (e.g., striking, takedown defense, or guard recovery).

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Hip Movement: Pushing off the mat with hands only while keeping hips low. This hyper-extends the elbows and wrists, risking ligament tears (e.g., UCL tear in elbow) and wrist fractures. Correct: Drive hips UP first, then transfer weight.
    • Knee and Ankle Twist: Standing up with legs too close together or rotating the ankle inward while weight is on it. This can lead to ACL/MCL tears in the knee or severe ankle sprains due to uncontrolled torsional forces. Correct: Maintain a wide base with knees tracking over toes.
    • Rounding the Back: Arching the back while trying to stand, leading to spinal compression. This can cause disc herniation or muscle strains in the lumbar region. Correct: Keep the spine neutral and engage the core to support the upright posture.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Elevation: From closed guard, practice the hip elevation and base creation without a partner. Focus on the upward hip drive and stable foot placement. (10 reps per side)
    2. Assisted Hip Elevation: Have a partner gently push into your guard. Practice the hip elevation and frame creation to create space. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Static Technical Stand-Up: With a partner kneeling or sitting, perform the full Technical Stand-Up without any movement from the partner. Focus on perfect biomechanics and grip control. (5 reps per side)
    4. Light Resistance: Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to execute the stand-up. Focus on maintaining your base and driving through. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Dynamic Resistance: Partner actively tries to prevent the stand-up by maintaining grips or pressure. You must adjust your weight and hip drive to overcome. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Controlled Rolling: Integrate the Technical Stand-Up into live rolling scenarios, starting with situations where you might naturally end up in a position to attempt it. (5-10 attempts, 90% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up from your closed guard, creating space.
    • When you are in bottom half-guard and have established a strong frame to create separation.
    • When you have successfully swept your opponent and are transitioning to a dominant position, but need to stand safely.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Pull Counter: If the opponent attempts to pull guard as you stand, maintain your grips, keep your base wide, and drive your hips forward to prevent them from establishing their guard. Your weight distribution should be low and stable.
    • Knee-to-Chest Counter: If the opponent drives a knee into your chest as you try to stand, use your forearm frame against their knee and hip, and simultaneously drive your hips backward slightly to reset your base before attempting the stand-up again.
    • Standing Base Collapse Counter: If the opponent tries to sweep your legs as you stand, immediately widen your base further and lower your hips, creating a solid, grounded stance. Use your grips to pull them slightly off-balance.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I do the Technical Stand-Up in BJJ?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you push solely with your hands on the mat instead of using your hips for elevation. This puts excessive hyperextension force on the wrist joint. To fix this, focus on driving your hips UP first, creating a stable base with your feet planted firmly, and using your forearms as frames against your opponent's shoulders or chest, not as primary pushing tools.

    Q: How do I do the Technical Stand-Up against a much bigger BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your hip drive is even more crucial. Focus on establishing a strong frame with your forearms against their chest or shoulders to create space before initiating the stand-up. Ensure your base is extremely wide and your feet are planted firmly. Think of it as 'elevating your base' with your hips, rather than 'pushing yourself up' with your arms, to maintain leverage.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Technical Stand-Up in BJJ sparring?

    The Technical Stand-Up is best used when you have created significant space from your opponent, such as when they posture up from your guard or when you've just defended a takedown attempt and are on your knees. It's also a valuable tool when you've swept an opponent but need to safely disengage and establish a better position before they can recover guard or take your back.
