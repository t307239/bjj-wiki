Stack Pass BJJ: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Stack Pass is a guard passing technique initiated from a close-range engagement, aiming to collapse the opponent's guard and establish side control or mount.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and body alignment, leading to ineffective pressure or vulnerability.

The core insight is to drive your hips forward and down while maintaining a strong, upright posture, effectively 'stacking' your opponent's base.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Assume a knee-on-belly or similar close-range position. Secure a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left sleeve) with your left hand.
    2. Place your left knee firmly on the mat, directly beside the opponent's hip, creating a solid base.
    3. Drive your hips forward and slightly down, aiming your sternum towards the opponent's sternum.
    4. Use your right hand to pull the opponent's collar down and towards your left hip, initiating a slight rotation.
    5. Simultaneously, use your left hand to control the opponent's left sleeve, preventing them from creating frames.
    6. Shift your weight forward, transferring your body mass over the opponent's hips and thighs.
    7. Maintain a strong base by keeping your left knee and right foot grounded, preventing them from bucking you.
    8. As the opponent's base collapses, step your right leg across their body and secure side control.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect hip drive: Attempting to drive hips straight forward without downward pressure can lead to the opponent easily shrimping away, potentially straining the lower back (lumbar spine hyperflexion).
    • Weak collar grip: A loose collar grip allows the opponent to establish frames with their hands, preventing the stack and risking shoulder impingement (rotator cuff strain) if you force the pass.
    • Poor base: Standing too upright or having too narrow a base makes you susceptible to sweeps; falling forward without controlled weight transfer can injure the knees or ankles (ligamentous sprain) if you land awkwardly.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip drive and weight transfer motion without a partner. Focus on the feeling of driving forward and down. (100 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Start in the initial position. Execute the full Stack Pass motion, with your partner offering no resistance. Focus on precise grip and body placement. (50 reps)
    3. Partner drill (passive resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and apply the grips correctly. (25 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner actively tries to resist but does not actively attack submissions or sweeps. Focus on maintaining pressure and completing the pass. (50% resistance, 25 reps)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Engage in light sparring, specifically attempting the Stack Pass when the opportunity arises. Focus on application and timing. (75% resistance, 5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (full): Attempt the Stack Pass during regular sparring, applying the learned mechanics and adapting to your opponent's reactions. (100% resistance, 10 minutes)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is trying to close their guard from a distance.
    • When the opponent attempts a knee shield or butterfly guard and their hips are slightly angled away.
    • When you have successfully established a strong knee-on-belly position and the opponent is flat on their back.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): As the stacker drives forward, the defender's primary response is to drive their hips away and to the side, creating space to re-establish guard or escape.
    • Frame Creation: The defender uses their arms to create frames between their hips and the stacker's chest, preventing the crushing pressure and allowing for hip escape.
    • Guard Retention/Re-guarding: If the stacker's pressure is insufficient, the defender can use their legs to re-establish their guard, often by bringing their knees back to their chest or hooking the stacker's legs.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try the Stack Pass?

    Your lower back pain likely stems from hyperextending your spine instead of driving your hips forward and down. Focus on keeping your core engaged and initiating the drive from your glutes and hips, not just your upper body. Think of it as pushing your pelvis into their hips, rather than arching your back.

    Q: How can I Stack Pass a bigger opponent who is very heavy?

    Against a heavier opponent, the Stack Pass requires impeccable structure and consistent pressure. Do not rely solely on pulling; instead, use your weight distribution to your advantage. Drive your hips lower and ensure your collar and sleeve grips are tight, creating a leverage point to break their posture before driving your weight forward.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Stack Pass, and what if my opponent is already flat on their back?

    The Stack Pass is most effective when the opponent is trying to create distance or recover guard. If they are already flat on their back, attempting a direct Stack Pass can be risky as they have a strong base to resist. Instead, consider transitioning to a knee-slice or pressure pass from that position while maintaining strong hip pressure.
