Open Guard BJJ: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

Open Guard starts from a supine position, aiming to control distance and create attack angles. It involves using your legs to create frames and manipulate your opponent's base.

White belts often struggle by creating too much space or by allowing their legs to become static targets, leading to passes or submissions.

The key insight is maintaining active leg tension and consistent frame creation, turning your legs into dynamic tools for control and offense.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From supine position, place the soles of your feet on opponent's hips, knees bent at 90 degrees. Maintain a hip angle of approximately 45 degrees off the mat.
    2. Grip opponent's sleeves with both hands, thumbs inside the cuff. Your elbows should be tucked to your ribs, creating a stable base.
    3. To advance, extend one leg while simultaneously retracting the other, creating a subtle hip scoot. Your hips should pivot on the mat.
    4. If opponent attempts to close distance, immediately extend both legs, pushing their hips away. This creates a frame with your shins against their thighs.
    5. To initiate a sweep like the scissor sweep, extend your right leg fully while simultaneously hooking your left foot behind their right knee.
    6. Shift your weight to your left hip, arching your back slightly, and pull their right sleeve with your right hand to unbalance them.
    7. As they fall, drive your extended right leg forward and drive your left leg backward, scooping their anchored leg to complete the sweep.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Knee hyperextension: Extending legs too forcefully without core engagement can lead to hyperextension of the knee joint, damaging the ACL and PCL.

    Correct: Maintain slight knee bend and use hip flexors and core to control leg extension.

    • Ankle sprains: Grasping opponent's ankles without proper foot placement or stability can lead to ankle rolls when they shift weight.

    Correct: Grip opponent's pant legs or sleeves, maintaining a stable base with your feet.

    • Lower back strain: Arching the back excessively without hip engagement to create frames can strain lumbar ligaments and discs.

    Correct: Use your hips and glutes to push, not just your back, maintaining a neutral spine when possible.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice leg extension and retraction with no resistance, focusing on hip movement. (50 reps)
    2. Solo drill: Practice grip acquisition on sleeves and maintaining frames against a wall. (50 reps)
    3. Partner drill: Light resistance, opponent stands. Practice pushing them away with frames. (2 minutes, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill: Opponent attempts to pass. Practice maintaining distance with frames and hip escapes. (3 minutes, 50% resistance)
    5. Partner drill: Practice one sweep (e.g., scissor sweep) with light resistance. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Focus on maintaining open guard and attempting sweeps. (3-minute rounds, 90% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent is in your guard and has not yet established a strong base or passed your legs.
    • When opponent is posturing up, creating space you can exploit with your legs.
    • When opponent is attempting to close the distance aggressively, providing an opportunity to create frames.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Guard Pass (e.g., Toreando Pass): If opponent attempts to circle their hips around your legs, immediately extend your legs to create space and shrimp your hips to the side, re-establishing frames.
    • Stack Pass: If opponent drives forward and tries to stack you, use your frames to push their hips away and simultaneously look to establish a grip on their collar to pull them into your guard or off-balance them.
    • Knee-on-Belly: If opponent attempts to transition to knee-on-belly, use your legs to push their knee away and immediately look to reclaim guard by bringing your legs back together.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to use Open Guard?

    Wrist pain in Open Guard usually stems from gripping too hard with your fingers only or not utilizing your forearm and bicep for support. Ensure your grip is firm but relaxed, with thumbs inside the sleeve. Keep your elbows tucked to your sides, creating a stable base that transfers force through your arms and shoulders, not just your wrists.

    Q: How do I maintain Open Guard against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage your legs' reach and frames to maintain distance. Focus on pushing their hips away with your shins and thighs, creating a consistent barrier. Grip their sleeves or collar to control their posture and prevent them from collapsing into you. Use your hips to shrimp and create angles, making it harder for their weight to be effective.

    Q: When should I start using Open Guard instead of Closed Guard as a white belt?

    As a white belt, transition to Open Guard when your opponent successfully breaks your Closed Guard or when you feel them posturing up, making it difficult to maintain control. Open Guard allows you to create space and re-establish control from a distance, which is often safer and more effective than trying to force a broken Closed Guard against a determined opponent.
