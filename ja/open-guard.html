Mastering Open Guard in BJJ: A White Belt's Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

Open Guard starts from a seated or supine position, using your legs to control distance and create attacking angles. It allows for dynamic sweeps and submissions, but requires precise body mechanics.

White belts often struggle due to relying on brute strength or poor framing, leading to loss of control and vulnerability. The key is understanding leverage and creating a consistent frame.

The ONE key mechanical insight is maintaining a constant, active frame with your shins and forearms, preventing your opponent from collapsing your guard and establishing dominant pressure.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From supine position, place soles of feet on opponent's hips, knees bent at 90 degrees.
    2. Maintain a hip angle of approximately 45 degrees to the opponent, creating space.
    3. Actively extend one leg while the other remains in a strong base, pushing opponent's hips away.
    4. Grip opponent's sleeves with both hands, elbows tucked to your sides to prevent them from breaking grips easily.
    5. When opponent steps forward, use the extended leg to push their hip, shifting your hips slightly to the opposite side.
    6. If opponent attempts to pass, use your free leg to hook their ankle or knee for control, maintaining hip separation.
    7. To initiate a sweep, drive with the foot on the hip while simultaneously pulling the sleeve and rotating your hips to off-balance them.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Knee Hyperextension: Overextending your leg to push an opponent's hip with a straight knee can cause anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears or meniscus damage. The correct movement involves a slight bend in the knee and pushing with the ball of the foot, maintaining a soft joint.
    • Wrist Sprains: Gripping sleeves too tightly with a completely straight wrist, especially when the opponent pulls, can lead to radial collateral ligament (RCL) sprains or even fractures. Maintain a neutral wrist, slightly flexed, and absorb pulling force with your forearm muscles.
    • Lower Back Strain: Attempting to lift or dramatically shift your hips without proper core engagement and hip rotation can strain lumbar ligaments and muscles. Engage your core and use controlled hip movements, not just leg strength.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Push Drill: Practice extending one leg and pushing an imaginary opponent's hip, focusing on hip angle and leg extension. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Frame and Extend Drill: With a partner lightly holding your ankles, practice creating space by extending your legs and maintaining a frame. (10 reps, 25% resistance).
    3. Sleeve Grip and Space Drill: Partner stands in front. Practice establishing sleeve grips and using your legs to create and maintain space. (15 reps, 40% resistance).
    4. Basic Sweep Entry: Partner provides minimal resistance. Practice entering the open guard, establishing grips, and initiating a scissor sweep or tripod sweep. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled Pass Defense: Partner attempts a basic pass (e.g., torreando). Practice using your legs to block and re-establish guard. (15 reps, 60% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling (Open Guard Focus): Implement open guard for short intervals (30 seconds) within rolling, focusing on maintaining guard and attempting sweeps. (5 rounds, 75% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent is posturing up and trying to apply pressure from a distance.
    • When opponent is attempting to close the distance aggressively.
    • When you want to create dynamic attacking opportunities from a defensive position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass: Opponent drives forward, attempting to stack you by putting weight on your hips. Counter by framing with your forearms on their shoulders, pushing them away, and shifting your hips to the side to create space and potentially transition to a sweep.
    • Knee Slice Pass: Opponent moves their knee to the inside of your leg to pass. Counter by using your opposite leg to hook their passing knee or ankle, preventing them from advancing and using your free leg to create a frame on their hip.
    • Head and Arm Control: Opponent secures a head and arm grip to pressure pass. Counter by controlling their arm with a grip and using your legs to create distance, preventing them from flattening you out. You can then look to transition to a submission or sweep.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do Open Guard in BJJ?

    Wrist pain typically occurs when you grip your opponent's sleeves with a straight, hyperextended wrist, especially when they are pulling. This puts excessive stress on your wrist ligaments. To prevent this, maintain a neutral or slightly flexed wrist, absorbing pulling forces with your forearm muscles, and keep your elbows tucked to your sides to create a more stable frame.

    Q: How do I use Open Guard effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and distance management are paramount in Open Guard. Focus on creating and maintaining space with your legs, using your shins and feet to push their hips away and prevent them from closing the distance. Prioritize grip fighting to control their sleeves, and look for opportunities to use your legs to off-balance them for sweeps rather than trying to power through them.

    Q: When should I switch from Closed Guard to Open Guard in BJJ?

    You should consider switching from Closed Guard to Open Guard when your opponent is effectively controlling your legs and preventing you from opening them, or when they are posturing up and creating significant pressure. Open Guard allows you to reset the distance, create angles, and initiate sweeps or submissions that might be difficult from a tightly locked Closed Guard.
