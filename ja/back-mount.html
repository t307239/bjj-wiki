バックマウント：BJJ初心者向け究極ポジション

Back Mount is a dominant position where you control your opponent from behind, facing their back. It offers the highest submission potential and control over their movement.

White belts often struggle with Back Mount because they focus on holding on rather than controlling the opponent's structure and base.

The key to secure Back Mount is establishing a tight "seatbelt" grip and controlling the opponent's hips with your legs, preventing them from turning in.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Secure a "seatbelt" grip: Right hand grabs opponent's left collar, left hand grabs opponent's right collar. Keep elbows tight to your body.
    2. Position your hips low and tight to their hips, preventing them from creating space.
    3. Hook your right leg inside opponent's right thigh, with your shin pressing against their hip.
    4. Hook your left leg inside opponent's left thigh, with your shin pressing against their hip.
    5. Ensure your feet are "squeezed" together, creating a "body triangle" effect around their waist.
    6. Keep your chest pressed against their upper back, preventing them from posturing up.
    7. Maintain a stable base by keeping your weight centered over their hips, not too far forward or back.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly hooking legs: Extending legs too wide or not squeezing them together can lead to your opponent escaping or bridging. This can strain the hip flexors and groin. The correct action is to keep your shins tight to their hips and squeeze your feet together.
    • Over-extending arms for grip: Reaching too far for the collar grip with straight arms leaves your elbows exposed to armbars. This can cause shoulder impingement or elbow hyperextension. Keep your elbows tucked and grip the collar firmly.
    • Allowing opponent to turn in: If your hips are not tight or your leg hooks are loose, your opponent can turn their hips into you, creating space and potentially escaping. This can lead to knee or ankle sprains. Maintain constant pressure with your hips and legs.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling "seatbelt" and hip positioning: Practice securing the seatbelt grip and lowering your hips while standing. (5 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo drilling leg hooks: Practice hooking your legs around an imaginary opponent's hips from a seated position. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill: Secure Back Mount on a compliant partner. Focus on maintaining tight hooks and seatbelt grip without any submission attempts. (10 reps each side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill with light control: Work on transitioning from a dominant position (like side control) to Back Mount, then holding for 10 seconds. (10 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Controlled sparring: Attempt to achieve and maintain Back Mount for 30 seconds against a partner who is actively defending but not attacking. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Focus on achieving and maintaining Back Mount during regular sparring. (Full rounds, 90-100% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is on their hands and knees (turtle position).
    • When you successfully take their back during a takedown or scramble.
    • When you secure a dominant position like side control and can transition to their back.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Uchi-mata" (Inner Thigh Sweep) Counter: If the opponent is loose on your back, use your arm that is under their armpit to pull their head down and execute an "uchi-mata" sweep, creating space to turn in. This requires precise timing and hip movement.
    • "Elbow Escape" Counter: If the opponent's leg hooks are not tight, bring your hips to the mat and "walk" your hips away, creating space to bring your knee between your hips and their body. This is a fundamental escape that requires breaking their base.
    • "Bridging and Rolling" Counter: While less effective against experienced opponents, a strong bridge combined with a hip escape can sometimes dislodge a loose Back Mount. Focus on turning your hips towards the arm that is not controlling your head.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why do my hips hurt when I try to get to Back Mount?

    Your hip pain is likely due to an incorrect pelvic tilt and lack of core engagement. When you try to attach to your opponent's back, ensure your pelvis is tucked under, creating a posterior tilt. This allows your hamstrings and glutes to engage, supporting your spine and preventing hyperextension. Imagine "squeezing" your opponent's hips with your own, rather than just draping over them.

    Q: How can I maintain Back Mount against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your leverage is crucial. Focus on a "super-glue" grip with your seatbelt and ensure your leg hooks are extremely tight, squeezing their hips like a vice. Your chest must remain glued to their upper back, preventing them from creating space to use their weight advantage. Prioritize control over brute force; use their size against them by controlling their movement with precision.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a submission from Back Mount?

    The optimal time to attack from Back Mount is when you have achieved a truly secure position with both leg hooks and a tight seatbelt grip, and your opponent is unable to effectively defend. Look for moments when they are scrambling to escape or have lost their posture. Attacking too early, before establishing control, often leads to losing the position and the submission opportunity.
