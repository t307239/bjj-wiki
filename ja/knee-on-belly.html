Knee on Belly is a dominant top position achieved after a successful guard pass, aiming to control your opponent's torso and limit their movement. It sets up sweeps and submissions while denying your opponent escape options.

White belts often struggle because they apply force with their arms or legs, neglecting the core principle of weight distribution and hip pressure. This leads to inefficient control and potential injury.

The key is using your hips to anchor your weight onto the opponent's sternum, creating a stable base and making their escape biomechanically difficult.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Begin from a passed guard position, typically side-control or headquarters-pass. Your opponent is on their back.
    2. Place your lead knee (the one closer to their head) onto their sternum, aiming for the center of their chest, just below the collarbones.
    3. Your lead shin should be perpendicular to their torso, creating a stable platform.
    4. Slide your trail leg back, placing your trail foot flat on the mat behind your opponent's hip, with toes pointing away from them.
    5. Your hips should be low, driving forward and slightly to the side of your opponent's center line. Aim for your pelvis to be over their diaphragm.
    6. Your lead hand grips their far collar (opposite side of your lead knee) with a deep grip, thumb inside.
    7. Your trail hand grips their opposite sleeve or wrist, creating a frame to prevent them from bridging or turning into you.
    8. Maintain a narrow base with your feet, about shoulder-width apart, to allow for quick adjustments and prevent them from easily sweeping you.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Placing your lead knee too high on the throat or too low on the abdomen can cause pressure on the trachea or digestive organs, leading to choking sensations or nausea. Correct by centering the knee on the sternum.
    • Extending your trail leg straight back and lifting your hips too high puts excessive pressure on your own lower back, potentially leading to strains. Correct by keeping hips low and driving forward.
    • Gripping the collar with fingers only or without sufficient depth allows the opponent to easily escape the grip. This can lead to them turning into you or creating space for escapes. Correct by using a deep thumb-inside grip on the far collar.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side-control to Knee on Belly and back, focusing on hip placement and knee positioning. (50 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner lies on their back. You execute the Knee on Belly transition, holding for 5 seconds, then releasing. (25 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance (e.g., attempts to shrimp slightly). You maintain Knee on Belly, adjusting your base. (25 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drilling (moderate resistance): Your partner actively tries to escape (e.g., attempts to shrimp or hip escape). You hold Knee on Belly, focusing on maintaining hip pressure and grip integrity. (20 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Flow rolling: Incorporate Knee on Belly into positional sparring. Aim to achieve and hold Knee on Belly for at least 10 seconds before moving to another position. (5 minutes, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Focus on achieving and maintaining Knee on Belly as a primary goal during rolling. (5 minutes, 90-100% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Immediately after successfully passing your opponent's guard, transitioning from side-control or headquarters-pass.
    • When your opponent attempts to bridge or shrimp but fails to create significant space, leaving an opening to land your knee.
    • When you have established a dominant side-control position and your opponent is flat on their back, making them vulnerable.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): As the knee lands, the opponent immediately executes a hip escape, creating space to bring their guard back. This requires precise timing to move before the weight is fully settled.
    • Bridge and Roll: The opponent bridges their hips upwards sharply, using the momentum to roll their opponent over. This is often initiated when the knee on belly is not fully anchored and the hips are not low.
    • Guard Recovery (Knee Shield): If the opponent's trail leg is still somewhat close, they can bring it up to create a knee shield against your chest, preventing you from driving forward and allowing them to reset their guard.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my knee on belly feel unstable and my opponent keeps escaping?

    Your instability likely stems from incorrect weight distribution. Ensure your hips are low and driving forward over your opponent's diaphragm, not just resting on their chest. Your lead knee should be perpendicular to their torso, creating a strong pivot point. Also, check your trail foot is flat on the mat, providing a stable base, and that your grips are tight to control their upper body.

    Q: How can I maintain knee on belly against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are key. Focus on maintaining a very low hip position to get your weight directly over their center of mass. Use your lead hand to grip their far collar deeply and your trail hand to control their sleeve or wrist to prevent them from posturing up. Drive your lead knee into their sternum with constant pressure, forcing them to carry your weight.

    Q: Switching legs is most effective when your opponent attempts to roll or shrimp away from your current knee position. As they create space, you can use that moment to pivot your hips and bring your other knee onto their sternum. This transition is also useful if they try to turn into you; you can use their momentum to switch sides and maintain control.
