Guard retention starts from a closed guard or open guard position, aiming to prevent your opponent from passing your legs.

White belts often fail by panicking and losing their structure, allowing the opponent to establish dominant positions.

The core principle is maintaining frames and hip connection to control distance and leverage your legs as a barrier.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, establish a strong two-on-one grip on the opponent's sleeves, just above the elbows.
    2. Sit up, keeping your back straight, and drive your knees towards your chest, creating a strong "frame" with your shins.
    3. If the opponent tries to stand, use your grips to pull their arms forward, forcing them to break their posture.
    4. Shift your hips to one side, creating an angle, and extend one leg to push their hip away, creating space.
    5. Maintain your grip on the sleeve and use your free hand to control their opposite wrist or bicep, preventing them from posturing.
    6. When they attempt to pass, use your feet on their hips as "bases" to push them away, maintaining your leg barrier.
    7. If they attempt a knee-slice, bring your knee to your chest and use your shin to block their knee, maintaining a tight frame.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly bridging your hips towards your opponent while they are postured up can hyperextend your lower back, risking lumbar sprains.
    • Grabbing the opponent's gi too low on their torso without proper structure allows them to easily pull their arms free and pass your guard.
    • Failing to create frames with your forearms and shins when the opponent is inside your guard allows them to collapse your structure and pass easily.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice sitting up, establishing sleeve grips, and extending legs to push hips, 20 reps each side.
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): Opponent stands in your closed guard. Practice pulling their arms forward and creating hip angles.
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Opponent attempts to stand and break grips. Focus on maintaining sleeve grips and hip separation.
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Opponent attempts to posture up and drive forward. Practice maintaining frames and pushing hips away.
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Opponent actively tries to pass your guard. Focus on keeping your legs between you and them.
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Apply guard retention principles against an opponent attempting to pass.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is postured up inside your closed guard.
    • When the opponent attempts to break your closed guard by stacking or driving forward.
    • When the opponent is trying to establish a knee-slice pass.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Sleeve and wrist control: Maintain two-on-one sleeve grips and control their opposite wrist to prevent them from posturing and driving forward.
    • Hip-to-hip frame: Use your shin and forearm to create a strong frame against the opponent's hip, pushing them away and creating space.
    • Guard recovery: If your guard is being passed, immediately bring your knees to your chest and extend your legs to re-establish your guard or transition to a safer position.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to keep my guard closed?

    Your lower back is likely hurting because you are arching your back excessively to compensate for a lack of core engagement. Instead of arching, focus on sitting up tall with a neutral spine and engaging your abdominal muscles to pull your knees towards your chest. This creates a strong, stable base rather than putting undue stress on your lumbar.

    Q: How do I keep my guard against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise angles are key. Focus on maintaining a tight frame with your shins and forearms against their hips and biceps, preventing them from closing the distance. Use your grips to control their posture and create angles by shifting your hips. Think of your legs as springs, constantly pushing and creating space.

    Q: When is the best time to switch from closed guard to open guard for retention?

    You should consider transitioning to an open guard if your opponent is consistently able to break your closed guard by posturing up or driving forward effectively. Open guard allows for more dynamic framing and distance management. However, ensure you have established strong grips and understand how to maintain frames before committing to the transition.
