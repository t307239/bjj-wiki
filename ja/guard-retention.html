Guard Retention: The Foundation of BJJ Defense for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

Guard retention starts from being on your back and prevents your opponent from passing your legs to a dominant position like side control.

White belts often fail by abandoning their frames, creating large gaps, or relying on brute strength instead of leverage.

The single most important mechanical insight is maintaining constant contact and creating strong, independent frames with your limbs.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Starting Position: On your back, knees bent, feet off the opponent's hips.
    2. Frame Creation: Place your forearms against the opponent's biceps, elbows slightly bent, creating a "frame" away from your body.
    3. Hip Escape (Shrimping): Drive your hips to one side, creating space. Keep your knees tucked towards your chest.
    4. Leg Movement: Simultaneously, extend the leg on the side you are shrimping towards, pushing the opponent's hip away.
    5. Grip Fighting: If opponent grips your collar or sleeves, use your free hand to break their grip by pushing their elbow away or prying their fingers open.
    6. Weight Distribution: Maintain a low base. If the opponent shifts weight, adjust your frames and hips to match their pressure, preventing them from flattening you.
    7. Regaining Guard: As space is created, bring your knees back together and re-establish your closed guard or a strong open guard position.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Frame: Extending arms straight and pushing directly forward. This risks elbow hyperextension (valgus stress on the UCL) and allows the opponent to easily break your structure. The correct alternative is to maintain slightly bent elbows and push at an angle, creating leverage.
    • Losing Hip Connection: Allowing the opponent to chest-to-chest your hips. This leads to knee or ankle sprains as your legs are trapped and unable to move freely. The correct alternative is to actively shrimp and maintain space between your hips and the opponent's.
    • Panicked Leg Flailing: Randomly kicking your legs to create space. This can result in knee ligament tears (ACL/MCL) if your leg gets trapped or twisted awkwardly. The correct alternative is controlled, deliberate leg movements integrated with hip escapes.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Escapes: Lie on your back, practice shrimping motion, focusing on hip movement and leg extension away from the body. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Solo Frame Drills: Practice creating and holding arm frames against an imaginary opponent, maintaining bent elbows and pushing angles. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    3. Partnered Frame and Shrimp: With a compliant partner, practice shrimping away from their chest pressure and maintaining arm frames to create space. (10 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Guard Retention Against Light Pressure: Partner applies minimal forward pressure. Focus on shrimping and framing to prevent them from flattening your guard. (15 reps, 50% resistance).
    5. Guard Retention Against Basic Passes: Partner attempts a simple pass (e.g., knee slice). Focus on maintaining frames and shrimping to recover guard. (10 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling - Guard Retention Focus: Roll with a partner, specifically focusing on maintaining your guard and preventing passes for designated periods. (3 x 2-minute rounds, 90% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is attempting to flatten your guard by driving forward.
    • When your opponent establishes a strong chest-to-chest pressure.
    • When your opponent begins to advance their hips past your knees.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): Drive hips to the side, extending one leg to create space and prevent the opponent from flattening your base. This moves your hips out of the line of attack.
    • Arm Frames: Create a strong base with your forearms against the opponent's biceps or shoulders. Push at an angle to off-balance them and create distance, preventing them from collapsing your guard.
    • Knee Shield: If your opponent gets past your initial frames, bring one knee up to create a barrier between your chest and their hips. This allows you to control distance and re-establish your guard.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to keep my guard?

    Lower back pain often stems from an anterior pelvic tilt and overextension when trying to create space. Instead of arching your back, focus on engaging your core and driving your hips to the side (shrimping). Imagine your hips are rotating, not just lifting. Keep your knees closer to your chest to shorten the lever arm and reduce strain.

    Q: How can I maintain guard retention against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your frames become even more critical. Instead of pushing directly, use your forearms to create angles and off-balance their weight. Focus on the subtle hip escape (shrimping) to create just enough space to reset your guard, rather than trying to push them completely away. Use your legs to create a tight, "sticky" guard.

    Q: When should I stop trying to retain guard and accept a guard pass?

    You should accept a guard pass and transition to a defensive position (like turning into them to attempt a sweep or scrambling back to guard) when your opponent has clearly established a dominant position that you cannot escape. This includes situations where they have flattened your hips, secured a strong cross-face, and are driving their weight through your body with no immediate escape route. The goal is to minimize damage and work back to a better position.
