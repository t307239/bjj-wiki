Spider Guard originates from the closed guard, transitioning to an open guard structure where your legs control the opponent's arms.

White belts often struggle with the delicate balance and precise foot placement, leading to control loss and injury.

The core principle is creating a strong, flexible frame with your legs and hips to dictate distance and angle, not just brute force.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, break opponent's posture by pulling their collar grips down with both hands.
    2. Place the sole of your left foot on the opponent's right bicep, ensuring your knee is tracking towards your left shoulder.
    3. Simultaneously, place the sole of your right foot on the opponent's left bicep, knee tracking towards your right shoulder.
    4. Extend your hips slightly, creating a frame with your legs, pushing their arms away from your center line.
    5. Grip opponent's left sleeve with your right hand and their right sleeve with your left hand (spider grip).
    6. Maintain a slight bend in your knees and keep your hips elevated off the mat, creating tension.
    7. Shift your hips to your left (90 degrees to opponent's center line) to set up sweeps or transitions, keeping both feet engaged.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Placing feet too high on the opponent's shoulders: This hyper-extends the opponent's shoulders and can cause rotator cuff tears; maintain foot placement on the bicep.
    • Straightening legs completely: This removes the spring-like tension and makes you vulnerable to sweeps and passes; keep a micro-bend in your knees.
    • Gripping opponent's wrists instead of sleeves: Wrist grips offer less control and can lead to wrist sprains for both parties; focus on the forearm/sleeve area.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo leg extension and contraction drills: Practice extending and retracting legs while maintaining foot placement on imaginary opponents (10 reps per leg).
    2. Spider guard frame building: Sit up in closed guard, break posture, and practice placing spider grips and foot placement, holding for 5 seconds (10 reps).
    3. Partner drill - static spider guard: Partner lies on their back, you establish spider guard and hold the position with minimal movement (30 seconds, 5 reps).
    4. Partner drill - light resistance: Partner attempts to straighten their arms or pass, you maintain spider guard and respond with slight adjustments (1 minute, 3 reps, 25% resistance).
    5. Partner drill - sweep attempts: Partner maintains posture, you attempt to initiate a sweep from spider guard (e.g., scissor sweep) (1 minute, 3 reps, 50% resistance).
    6. Light rolling: Incorporate spider guard into live rolling, focusing on maintaining the guard and attempting sweeps (3 minutes, 1 round, 75% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent establishes a strong collar grip and tries to pass your closed guard.
    • When opponent is posturing up and trying to create space to pass.
    • When you want to control distance and prevent your opponent from getting close for a pass.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Knee Slice Pass: Opponent drives their knee towards your hip, stepping their other leg over your body and stacking you. Maintain frame with your legs and attempt to shrimp your hips away.
    • Torreando Pass: Opponent steps their legs around your hips to pass to the opposite side. Use your spider grips to pull them back into your guard or transition to another guard.
    • Stacking Pass: Opponent drives their weight forward, attempting to compress your guard and pass your legs. Use your spider grips to create frames and push their weight away, then shrimp out.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I do Spider Guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping too tightly on the opponent's wrist or allowing your own wrist to bend excessively. Ensure you are gripping the opponent's sleeve or forearm, not their wrist, and maintain a neutral or slightly extended wrist on your own hand. Your grip should be firm but not a death grip, allowing for subtle adjustments.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Focus on using your hips to create angles and your legs to control their arm extension, preventing them from using their weight effectively. Your spider grips should be used to disrupt their posture and create opportunities to off-balance them for sweeps, rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: The optimal time to transition from closed guard to Spider Guard is when your opponent is posturing up and attempting to establish control over your legs for a pass. This is your window to break their posture, establish your spider grips, and place your feet on their biceps to create distance and control.
