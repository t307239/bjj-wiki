This sweep is initiated from closed guard, aiming to elevate your opponent and transition to a dominant position. It's a fundamental sweep for controlling distance and creating offensive opportunities.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength instead of precise hip movement and weight transfer. This leads to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Understand the mechanics, not just the motion.

The core principle is using your hips as a lever, combined with a specific leg and arm action, to create a powerful upward and forward motion that unbalances your opponent. Think of your hips lifting their base.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Secure collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside the gi, and sleeve grip on their left sleeve with your left hand. Your hips are on the mat, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Place your left foot on their right hip bone, creating a frame. Your right foot is positioned by their left knee pit.
    3. Shift your weight slightly onto your left hip, lowering your right shoulder towards the mat.
    4. Execute a powerful hip lift (bridge) by extending your glutes and lower back upwards and forwards, simultaneously pulling their collar and sleeve towards your left shoulder.
    5. As your hips lift, drive your left foot (on their hip) into their hip bone to create leverage and push their base away.
    6. Simultaneously, extend your right leg, pushing off their left knee pit with your right foot to create an upward trajectory for their body.
    7. Continue the hip lift and pulling motion, elevating their body upwards and forwards, aiming to break their base and land in a superior position like side control or mount.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly extending your legs without a proper hip lift: This can hyperextend your knee joint, leading to ACL or MCL tears due to excessive anterior or medial stress.
    • Correct movement: Always initiate with a strong hip bridge, using legs to drive upwards, not just extend.
    • Gripping too tightly on sleeves without proper body alignment: This can cause wrist sprains or dislocations as you attempt to pull without generating leverage from your hips and core.
    • Correct movement: Use your entire body to generate the sweep, not just arm strength; maintain a stable base with your feet.
    • Trying to force the sweep by only pulling the arms: This places immense strain on your shoulder joint and rotator cuff, risking tears or impingement due to improper biomechanical leverage.
    • Correct movement: Focus on the hip elevation and pushing with your legs as the primary drivers of the sweep.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Bridge Drill: Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Practice explosive hip bridges, focusing on lifting your hips high off the mat. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Solo Leg Extension Drill: From the same starting position, practice extending your legs outwards as you lift your hips, simulating the push-off motion. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    3. Partnered Isolation Drill (No Resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice the grip acquisition and the hip lift/leg extension motion without any resistance. Focus on the timing. (10 reps per side, 10% resistance).
    4. Partnered Sweep Drill (Controlled): With your partner on their back, secure grips and perform the Elevator Sweep. Your partner gently helps you complete the motion, minimizing their base. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    5. Live Sparring Drill (Controlled): Engage in light sparring with a partner. Focus on attempting the Elevator Sweep whenever the opportunity arises, allowing for natural resistance. (5 minutes, 50% resistance).
    6. Full Sparring: Apply the Elevator Sweep in live rolling, focusing on correct mechanics and timing against full resistance. (10 minutes, 75-90% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent extends their legs slightly, creating space between their hips and yours.
    • When your opponent is posturing up and their weight is forward, making them top-heavy.
    • When you have strong grips on their collar and sleeve, allowing you to control their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stacking Pass: If the sweep is initiated, the defender can immediately drive their weight forward and down onto the attacker's hips, effectively neutralizing the lift. This involves keeping the hips low and driving the shoulders into the attacker's chest.
    • Leg Escape/Base Adjustment: The defender can quickly bring their knees back towards their chest and widen their base, making it harder for the attacker's hips to get under them. This involves a quick retraction and widening of the legs.
    • Grip Release and Posture Control: The defender can break the collar and sleeve grips by rotating their wrists or by using their free hand to peel the attacker's hands off. They then immediately regain a strong upright posture.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to Elevator Sweep?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you rely solely on arm strength to pull your opponent. Your wrists are not designed for this type of sustained, high-force pulling without proper biomechanical support. To fix this, focus on generating the sweep's power from your hips and legs. Imagine your arms are guiding the movement, not creating the force. Ensure your grips are secure but not overly tense, and prioritize a strong hip bridge.

    Q: How can I Elevator Sweep against a much bigger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, brute force pulling is ineffective and risky. The key is leverage and timing. Focus on securing tight collar and sleeve grips, and use your body to create a tight frame with your legs. When you bridge, exaggerate the upward and forward hip motion, using your entire core. Your goal is to get *under* their center of gravity, not to pull them over you. Think of lifting their hips off the mat, rather than pulling their upper body.

    Q: When is the best moment to initiate the Elevator Sweep, and what if my opponent is already low?

    The Elevator Sweep is most effective when your opponent is posturing up or has a slight forward lean, making their base less stable. If your opponent is already low and their hips are close to yours, it becomes very difficult to get your hips underneath them. In this scenario, consider transitioning to a different sweep like a Scissor Sweep or attempting to open your guard and work for a pass. Patience and observation of your opponent's posture are crucial.
