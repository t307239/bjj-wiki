The X-Pass is a guard passing technique designed to break open an opponent's closed guard and establish side control.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength instead of coordinated body mechanics, leading to stalled passes and potential injury.

The key insight is to use your hip pressure and shoulder rotation to create a wedge, forcing the opponent's legs open and off-balancing them.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Opponent is in closed guard. Your goal is to break their grip on your hips.
    2. Grip: Secure a collar grip with your right hand on their left collar, and your left hand on their right collar (cross-collar grip).
    3. Hip Angle: Lower your hips, creating a nearly 90-degree angle between your torso and the mat, aiming your left hip towards their right hip.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your left hip, driving it forward into their pelvis.
    5. Leg Action: Extend your right leg straight back, creating a base, while keeping your left knee slightly bent and tucked under your chest.
    6. Shoulder Rotation: Simultaneously rotate your upper body to your right, using your collar grips to pull their shoulders down and to your right.
    7. Break: This combined pressure forces their legs apart, allowing you to drive your chest through the opening into side control.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Error: Attempting to force open the legs by pushing straight back with your hips while maintaining an upright posture. Damage: This can strain your lower back ligaments (lumbar strain) due to excessive hyperextension. Correction: Lower your hips drastically and drive them forward at a 90-degree angle to your torso, using your shoulders to help.
    • Error: Gripping the opponent's pants or gi material too low on their legs and pulling upwards. Damage: This can cause wrist hyperextension or a sprain as you fight against their leg strength and potentially snap your grip. Correction: Focus on collar grips and driving your body weight into their hips and shoulders.
    • Error: Extending your legs too far back and straight, creating a 'crab' like position, when trying to create base. Damage: This can lead to knee hyperextension injuries if the opponent shifts their weight unexpectedly or you lose balance. Correction: Keep your rear leg slightly bent and use it as a stable, controlled base, not a rigid lever.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Isolation Drill: Practice lowering your hips and rotating them forward without a partner. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Grip & Hip Drive Drill: With a partner lying on their back, practice securing the cross-collar grip and driving your hip into theirs. Partner offers minimal resistance (resisting by just lying there). (20 reps per side, 10% resistance).
    3. Leg Break & Drive Drill: Partner maintains a loose closed guard. You execute the hip drive and shoulder rotation to create a small opening. Partner allows their legs to be opened slightly. (30 reps, 25% resistance).
    4. Pass Completion Drill: Execute the X-Pass to the point of breaking the guard and stepping over. Partner offers slight resistance to your transition to side control. (25 reps, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled X-Pass: Execute the X-Pass against a compliant partner actively trying to maintain their guard. Focus on maintaining proper mechanics and pressure. (20 reps, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling X-Pass Attempt: In rolling, actively look for opportunities to initiate the X-Pass against a resisting opponent. Focus on applying the learned mechanics. (5 rounds, 90% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's hips are relatively close to yours, making it difficult to establish a deep base for other passes.
    • When the opponent has a tight, strong closed guard that is difficult to pry open with simpler methods.
    • When you are in a position where your hips are naturally low and you can drive forward.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The 'Sit Up' Escape: If the passer drives their hip into you, shift your weight to your hips and sit up, bringing your knees towards your chest to reclaim guard or create space. This requires a quick, reactive weight shift to avoid being flattened.
    • The 'Shrimp and Re-guard': As the passer attempts to drive their shoulder into your chest, shrimp your hips away and bring your knees up to your face, creating a frame to re-establish your guard. This is a reactive escape to prevent the pass.
    • The 'Leg Pummel': If the passer tries to isolate one leg, actively pummel your leg back into a defensive position, maintaining a strong frame with your arms and hips to prevent them from getting a dominant angle.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try the X-Pass?

    Your lower back pain likely stems from hyperextending your spine. Instead of driving your hips forward with a neutral or slightly flexed spine, you might be arching your back to create pressure. Correct this by significantly lowering your hips and ensuring your core is engaged, driving the pressure through your pelvis rather than your lumbar spine.

    Q: How do I X-Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise angles are even more critical. Focus intensely on breaking their posture by pulling their shoulders down with your collar grips as you drive your hip into their pelvis. Do not try to match their strength; instead, use their size against them by off-balancing them with your forward hip pressure and rotational force.

    Q: When is the best time to start thinking about the X-Pass?

    The X-Pass is best initiated when you feel the opponent's hips are close enough to yours that you can effectively drive your own hip into them. If they are very far away, other passes like the torreando might be more efficient. Look for the moment their guard feels 'stuck' and you can't easily break it with a simple leg push.
