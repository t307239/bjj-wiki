Kimura: A White Belt's Biomechanically Accurate Guide to the BJJ Arm Lock

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock primarily initiated from closed guard, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's arm at the shoulder joint. White belts often struggle due to a lack of control and understanding of leverage.

The key to a successful Kimura for beginners lies in understanding how to isolate the arm and create a strong, stable base to apply rotational pressure.

Focus on controlling the opponent's posture and using your hips to generate the torque, rather than brute strength, to avoid injury and ensure effectiveness.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, secure a "gummy bear" grip on the opponent's wrist with your left hand (thumb inside the fist). Your right arm wraps around their upper arm, securing a "figure-four" grip on their forearm with your right hand on your own left wrist.
    2. Maintain a tight closed guard, ensuring your hips are close to their hips, preventing them from posturing up.
    3. With your left foot pressing against their hip and your right foot on their bicep, create a slight angle to your left, opening your guard momentarily.
    4. As you open your guard, drive your left hip forward and your right hip back, creating a rotational force around their shoulder.
    5. Simultaneously, pull your left arm (the one gripping the wrist) towards your own chest, keeping your elbow tucked.
    6. Use your right hand to pull their gripped forearm towards their own torso, creating immense rotational pressure on the shoulder joint.
    7. Maintain a stable base by keeping your knees tight and your back flat against the mat, avoiding arching your back.
    8. Apply steady, controlled pressure, avoiding sudden jerking motions, until the opponent taps.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Applying excessive upward force with your arms while the opponent is postured high: This can cause a rotator cuff tear in your own shoulder due to the unnatural angle of pull.

    Correct: Keep your elbows tucked and focus on hip rotation and pulling their arm towards their body.

    • Attempting to "crank" the lock by arching your back forcefully: This puts immense strain on your lumbar spine, risking a disc herniation.

    Correct: Maintain a flat back and use your hips to generate the torque.

    • Allowing the opponent to stack you by not controlling their posture: This can lead to your own shoulder being hyperextended or a knee injury if their weight comes down awkwardly.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip sequence and hip movement with no resistance (5 reps per side).
    2. Partner drill (static): With a compliant partner, practice the grip and body positioning without any pressure (10 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (light pressure): Apply the grip and slowly apply pressure, stopping immediately if resistance is met (15 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (controlled submission): Execute the Kimura with controlled pressure, stopping just before the submission is fully applied, allowing the partner to tap safely (20 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live drilling (Kimura attempts): Focus on attempting the Kimura from closed guard during positional sparring, with the goal of executing the sequence correctly even if the submission is not completed (3 rounds, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Kimura as a natural attacking option during regular sparring, prioritizing correct technique over immediate tap-outs (5 rounds, 90-100% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is inside your closed guard and attempting to pass or posture up.
    • When you have excellent hip control and can prevent the opponent from "stacking" you.
    • When the opponent leaves one arm exposed and you can secure a strong figure-four grip.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stacking": The opponent drives their weight forward and down, attempting to crush your hips and break your guard. To counter, maintain a strong base and try to hip escape to create space.
    • "Arm Escape": The opponent isolates the trapped arm and pulls it back towards their body, relieving pressure. To counter, ensure your figure-four grip is tight and your hips are angled correctly to prevent this.
    • "Posturing Up": The opponent forcefully straightens their back and pulls their arm out of the grip. To counter, use your legs to control their hips and prevent them from gaining leverage.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Kimura from closed guard?

    Your wrist pain likely stems from gripping the opponent's wrist too tightly with your fingers extended or from trying to "crank" the lock with your wrist. Instead, aim for a "gummy bear" grip where your thumb is inside their fist and your fingers are curled around their wrist. Focus on using your shoulders and hips to create the rotational pressure, not your wrist.

    Q: How can I do a Kimura against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Do not try to match their strength. Instead, focus on breaking their posture down using your legs and hips. Once their posture is broken, use a sharp hip movement to create the angle, then drive your hips forward to isolate their arm and apply the rotational force. A tight figure-four grip is crucial to maintain control.

    Q: I keep losing my Kimura attempt because my opponent just pulls their arm out, what am I doing wrong?

    Your opponent is likely pulling their arm out because you are not creating enough rotational pressure with your hips or your figure-four grip is not tight enough. Ensure your left foot is on their hip and your right foot on their bicep to create an angle, then drive your hips forward. Your right hand on your own wrist must be locked in tightly to prevent their arm from sliding out. Keep your own elbow tucked to maintain that strong connection.
