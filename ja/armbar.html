BJJ Armbar: A Precise Biomechanical Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

The armbar is a fundamental joint lock originating from various positions, most commonly closed guard, aiming to hyperextend the elbow.

White belts often struggle due to uncontrolled hip movement and insufficient control of the opponent's posture.

The key is isolating the arm and creating a fulcrum with your hips, not just pulling.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, establish control by placing your feet on the opponent's hips, creating a frame.
    2. Secure a S-grip (thumb-in) on the opponent's gi sleeve or wrist with your right hand, keeping your elbow tucked.
    3. With your left hand, grip the opponent's opposite bicep, creating a strong cross-face frame.
    4. Swing your right leg over the opponent's head, ensuring your shin crosses their neck, not their face.
    5. Immediately pivot your hips 90 degrees to the left, aligning your body perpendicular to the opponent.
    6. Bring your left leg up and over the opponent's head, hooking your left foot behind your right knee to create a tight "figure-four" grip around their torso.
    7. Lower your hips towards the mat, creating a fulcrum point directly under the opponent's elbow joint.
    8. Extend your legs upwards, lifting your hips while simultaneously driving your weight downwards through your chest onto the opponent's upper torso, applying hyperextension to the elbow.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Attempting to "pull" the arm straight without hip rotation: This can lead to shoulder dislocation or bicep tears as the humerus rotates abnormally.
    • Not creating a strong hip fulcrum by keeping hips too low or too far away: This results in insufficient leverage and potential knee hyperextension or ankle sprains when trying to force the lock.
    • Crossing your feet in the figure-four lock before hip rotation: This traps your own legs, preventing proper hip elevation and leading to a loss of control or a failed submission attempt, potentially injuring your own knee.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip rotation and leg-over-head motion with a rolled-up gi or pillow (10 reps).
    2. Solo drill: Practice the grip sequence (S-grip and bicep grip) and hip elevation without a partner (10 reps).
    3. Partner drill: From closed guard, practice the initial grip establishment and leg-over-head placement with no resistance (25% resistance, 5 reps).
    4. Partner drill: Practice the full armbar sequence with a compliant partner, focusing on hip movement and extension, stopping before full pressure (50% resistance, 5 reps).
    5. Partner drill: Execute the armbar with increasing pressure, allowing the partner to tap early (75% resistance, 5 reps).
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the armbar from closed guard in sparring, prioritizing technique over submission (90% resistance, 3 attempts per round).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent attempts to posture up from your closed guard, creating space to swing your leg over.
    • When the opponent tries to stack you from closed guard, bringing their weight forward and creating an opportunity to isolate an arm.
    • When the opponent's arms are extended during a guard pass attempt, especially if they are reaching for your legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Reguarding" or "Shrimping": If the opponent's arm is being isolated, immediately shrimp your hips away and bring your guard back to closed or open guard to prevent the leg-over-head.
    • "Stacking" or "Posturing Up": If the opponent has your arm and is attempting to swing their leg over, their base is compromised. Immediately post your feet on their hips and drive your weight forward to stack them, breaking their posture and potentially freeing your arm.
    • "Tucking the Elbow" and "Fighting the Grip": Actively pull your elbow towards your body and resist the grip with your free hand. If the armbar is not fully locked, try to roll towards the side of the trapped arm to relieve pressure.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my elbow hurt when I try to do an armbar from closed guard?

    Your elbow likely hurts because you are not properly isolating the opponent's arm and creating a fulcrum with your hips. Instead of just pulling, focus on driving your hips up and rotating them 90 degrees to create a strong hinge point under their elbow, allowing their own resistance to extend their arm.

    Q: How can I do an armbar from closed guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paramount. Ensure your feet are firmly on their hips to control their base, and establish a tight S-grip on their sleeve or wrist. The key is the 90-degree hip rotation to create a strong fulcrum; don't try to overpower them, use their size against them by controlling their posture and driving your hips.

    Q: When should I abandon an armbar attempt and defend myself instead?

    You should abandon an armbar attempt if you feel your own joints being stressed, your posture is completely broken, or the opponent has successfully countered your setup. Continuing a poorly executed armbar can lead to injury for you, and if they have established a strong counter like a stack or escape, it's better to reset and defend your position.
