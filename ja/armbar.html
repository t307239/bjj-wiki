The Fundamental Armbar Technique in BJJ

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 青帯ガイド

The armbar is a fundamental joint lock from the closed guard, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's elbow. White belts often struggle due to a lack of control and understanding of leverage.

The key insight is using your hips to create the extension, not just pulling with your arms.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, secure opponent's right arm with your left arm wrapped around their bicep, controlling their wrist with your right hand (sleeve grip). Maintain a tight closed guard.
    2. Shift your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's body. Your right hip should be elevated.
    3. Swing your right leg over your opponent's head, placing your right foot on the mat behind their head for base. Your left leg remains across their chest.
    4. Bring your right shin across your opponent's neck, securing it tightly. Your left leg should cross your right shin, creating a figure-four grip around their head and upper body.
    5. Untuck your opponent's trapped arm from their body, ensuring it is extended away from their torso. Your left arm continues to control the wrist.
    6. Lower your hips towards the mat, driving your heels towards your glutes. This action compresses their upper body and isolates the elbow joint.
    7. Extend your hips upwards, simultaneously driving your knees downwards. This creates a lever action, hyperextending the elbow. Maintain wrist control throughout.
    8. Squeeze your knees together to prevent them from posturing up. Keep your back flat on the mat, avoiding arching, which reduces leverage.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly driving your hips up while the opponent's arm is still bent: This can cause a severe tear of the biceps tendon or rotator cuff as the shoulder is forced into an unnatural position.
    • Forcing the submission by pulling upwards with your arms only: This often results in the opponent escaping or, worse, hyperextending their own elbow in the wrong direction, risking a ligament tear.
    • Not controlling the opponent's posture or head: This allows them to stack you, potentially injuring your own spine or neck, and negates the leverage needed for the armbar.
    • Releasing wrist control prematurely: This allows the opponent to pull their arm out of the lock, reducing the effectiveness and increasing the risk of a joint injury to yourself if they yank.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement, leg placement over the head, and figure-four grip without a partner. 10 repetitions each side.
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Partner holds still. Execute the armbar from closed guard, focusing on precise body mechanics and grips. 5 repetitions each side.
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to posture or arm extension. Focus on maintaining control and completing the mechanics. 5 repetitions each side.
    4. Controlled drilling (25% resistance): Partner attempts to defend by posturing or pulling their arm. You focus on maintaining your structure and applying the hip extension. 5 repetitions each side.
    5. Controlled drilling (50% resistance): Partner actively attempts to escape. Focus on adapting your grips and hip movement to counter their defense. 5 repetitions each side.
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the armbar in a rolling scenario, but tap early if the submission isn't secure or if you feel yourself in danger. 3 rounds.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is postured down in your closed guard, offering their arm within reach.
    • When you successfully break down their posture and isolate one arm.
    • Immediately after a failed sweep or guard pass attempt where you can transition to a control position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Guard Pass (Stack Pass): The opponent can attempt to "stack" you by driving their weight forward and down, posturing up and falling onto your hips. This breaks the armbar angle and allows them to escape.
    • Hip Escape and Arm Pull: The opponent can often escape by hip escaping to their side, away from the direction of the armbar, and simultaneously pulling their trapped arm free.
    • Rolling Escape: A more advanced defense involves the opponent rolling in the direction of the armbar, often tucking their head and using their free arm to create space and escape the lock.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my opponent's arm bend when I try to armbar from closed guard?

    This typically happens because you are not controlling their wrist effectively with your hand and are not creating a strong enough lever with your hips. Ensure your right hand has a firm sleeve grip on their wrist, and actively drive your hips up while simultaneously squeezing your knees together. Imagine lifting your hips off the mat.

    Q: How do I armbar a much larger opponent from closed guard in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paramount. Focus on meticulously controlling their posture by keeping your guard tight and preventing them from posturing up. Once you isolate an arm, ensure your hips are at a 45-degree angle and your legs form a tight figure-four around their head. Drive your hips up with precision, not brute force.

    Q: When should I abandon an armbar attempt in BJJ?

    You should abandon an armbar attempt if your opponent successfully stacks you and breaks your structure, if they pull their arm out of your grip, or if you feel your own joints are being put in a compromised position. Prioritize safety; it's better to reset and look for another opportunity than to risk injury.
