The armbar is a submission from closed guard, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's elbow. White belts often struggle due to a lack of control and understanding of leverage.

The key insight is to use your hips to create the extension, not just your arms, by rotating your body and controlling their posture.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, secure a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (near the wrist) with your left hand on the opponent's right arm.
    2. Shift your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to the opponent's torso.
    3. Bring your right knee across the opponent's face, tucking it tightly to their neck, while your left leg wraps around their head and neck, locking your ankles.
    4. Arch your lower back, lifting your hips upwards and slightly forward, ensuring your opponent's arm is trapped between your thighs.
    5. Extend your legs, keeping your ankles locked, and pull your hips forward and slightly down, creating a fulcrum with your hips.
    6. Grip your own shin (right shin) with your right hand, maintaining the cross-collar grip. Your left hand remains on the sleeve grip.
    7. Continue to drive your hips forward and upward, creating a controlled extension of the opponent's elbow joint. Apply pressure by squeezing your thighs and extending your hips.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Attempting to pull the opponent's arm directly towards your chest using only arm strength: This can cause severe rotator cuff tears or biceps tendon ruptures in the attacker's shoulder as they force the movement against resistance.
    • Not controlling the opponent's posture by keeping their head trapped: This allows them to stack you, potentially leading to a knee injury (ACL tear) if their weight is driven directly into your knee joint.
    • Releasing the leg lock around the head prematurely while attempting to extend: This leaves your own head vulnerable to strikes or allows the opponent to escape easily, risking a shoulder injury if you twist incorrectly trying to maintain the submission.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling armbar mechanics from closed guard: Practice the hip movement, leg crossing, and hip lift without a partner (50 reps).
    2. Partner drilling hip movement and leg placement from closed guard (0% resistance): Focus on correct angles and grip setup with a compliant partner (20 reps per side).
    3. Partner drilling the full armbar sequence with controlled resistance (25%): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing focus on hip extension and control.
    4. Partner drilling with increasing resistance (50%): Partner begins to defend slightly, requiring adjustments in hip pressure and leg control.
    5. Partner drilling with significant resistance (75%): Partner actively tries to escape or counter, simulating a higher-level roll.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Apply the armbar in a sparring situation, focusing on timing and pressure.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating an opening for your legs to attack their arm.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their weight is distributed in a way that leaves one arm accessible.
    • When you have successfully swept or reversed an opponent and are transitioning to a dominant position like mount or side control, but they expose an arm.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape by tucking the attacked elbow tightly to your own body: This minimizes the lever arm and prevents hyperextension. Simultaneously, turn your body away from the attacker's hips.
    • Stack defense: If the attacker attempts to extend their hips, drive your weight forward and down onto their hips, attempting to "stack" them and break their posture. This relieves pressure on the elbow.
    • Bridge and roll: While the arm is trapped, attempt a powerful bridge towards the side of the attacked arm and simultaneously roll over your shoulder to escape the hold. This requires explosive movement and timing.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do an armbar from closed guard?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are gripping the opponent's sleeve too loosely or relying on a grip that isn't stabilized by your forearm. Ensure your cross-collar grip is tight and your sleeve grip is near their wrist, with your forearm perpendicular to their arm. This creates a solid frame that transfers force efficiently to their elbow, not your wrist.

    Q: How can I armbar a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is not brute strength but precise body mechanics and leverage. Focus on isolating their arm effectively by cutting your hips sharply to the side (45 degrees). Then, elevate your hips high and drive them forward, using your legs to create a strong fulcrum around their elbow. Control their posture by trapping their head with your legs.

    Q: When should I switch my grips during an armbar attempt from closed guard?

    You generally do not switch your primary grips (cross-collar and sleeve) during the execution of a standard closed guard armbar. The cross-collar grip provides posture control and leverage, while the sleeve grip on their wrist stabilizes the arm being attacked. Your focus should be on hip movement and extension, not grip switching, which can create openings for escape.
