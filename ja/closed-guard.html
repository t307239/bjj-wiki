Closed Guard: The Foundation of BJJ for White Belts

WHITE★☆☆☆☆Beginner
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

Closed Guard is a fundamental BJJ position where you control your opponent from your back, with your legs wrapped around their waist.

White belts often struggle by being too rigid or too loose, leading to easy passes or an inability to generate offense.

The key is creating a tight, flexible "frame" with your legs and hips to control distance and leverage.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Lie on your back with your knees bent at 90 degrees, feet flat on the mat. Your hips should be slightly tucked under.
    2. Bring your opponent into your guard by stepping your feet on their hips, creating a "frame" with your shins and ankles.
    3. Wrap your ankles in a "figure-four" grip behind your opponent's back, ensuring your heels are close to your glutes.
    4. Maintain a slight outward pressure with your knees, preventing your opponent from straightening their legs or posturing up.
    5. Control your opponent's posture by gripping their sleeves or collar with both hands, pulling them slightly towards you.
    6. Shift your hips to the side to create angles, making it harder for your opponent to maintain a stable base.
    7. Use your feet on their hips to push and pull, adjusting the distance and creating opportunities for sweeps or submissions.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Gripping your own shins instead of the opponent's back: This puts excessive stress on your knee ligaments (ACL/MCL) due to hyperextension, as the joint is not stabilized by the opponent's body. Always secure your ankles around the opponent's waist.
    • Letting your knees splay outwards while in guard: This opens up your hips and allows your opponent to easily "stack" you, potentially causing lumbar spine compression or facet joint injury. Keep your knees actively pressing inwards and upwards.
    • Allowing your opponent to "post" their hands on your hips with straight arms: This disconnects your frames and allows them to establish strong base for passing, potentially leading to wrist or elbow hyperextension if you try to resist improperly. Always keep your arms connected and elbows tucked.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the leg wrap and ankle lock motion without resistance. Focus on smooth transitions and achieving a tight "figure-four" grip. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Have a partner stand in front of you. Practice stepping your feet onto their hips and closing your guard. Focus on the ankle lock and hip control. (30 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Have a partner gently try to stand up or posture. Practice maintaining your guard by adjusting your hip angle and leg pressure. (20 reps per side)
    4. Sweep drill: With light resistance, practice a simple scissor sweep from closed guard. Focus on timing your hip movement with your leg sweep. (15 reps per side)
    5. Submission drill: Practice a basic armbar from closed guard. Focus on controlling posture and creating the correct angle for the submission. (10 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (50% resistance): Implement closed guard in a controlled rolling session. Focus on maintaining control and attempting one submission or sweep. (3 x 2 minute rounds)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent "shoots" for a takedown and you can catch them in your guard.
    • When you are on your back and your opponent is in your guard, attempting to pass.
    • When you have just been swept and are looking to regain guard control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass Defense: If your opponent stacks into you, immediately widen your knees and push their hips away with your feet on their hips, creating space to re-establish frames or transition to a sweep.
    • Hip Escape to Open Guard: If your opponent is persistently "stacking" and you cannot create space, hip escape to the side and transition to an open guard like butterfly or half guard to reset your position.
    • "Knee Shield" Control: If your opponent is close and trying to "smash" pass, use one knee as a "shield" between their hip and your body, while the other leg maintains a hook. This prevents them from getting their hips too close.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to close my guard?

    Your back likely hurts because you are not tucking your hips enough and are allowing your opponent to "stack" you. This puts excessive lordosis (arch) on your lumbar spine. To fix this, consciously tuck your tailbone under and keep your knees slightly elevated, creating a "shell" with your legs to absorb pressure and protect your spine.

    Q: How can I keep my closed guard closed against a much heavier opponent?

    Against a heavier opponent, focus on "framing" with your legs and hips to create leverage, rather than brute strength. Keep your ankles "locked" tightly behind their back, and use your feet on their hips to control distance and create angles. A slight "pelvic tilt" forward will help you anchor yourself and prevent them from easily driving through your guard.

    Q: When should I transition from Closed Guard to another guard?

    You should consider transitioning if your opponent consistently "posts" their hands on your hips with straight arms, preventing you from creating angles or executing submissions. This indicates they are establishing a strong base for a pass. Transitioning to an open guard like butterfly or half guard allows you to reset and create better leverage for attacks.
