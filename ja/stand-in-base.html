Stand In Base: The Foundation of BJJ Transitions for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

Stand In Base is a crucial transition from a compromised position, like being swept or losing control, to a stable, upright fighting stance. It allows you to re-establish control or initiate new attacks.

White belts often struggle because they rush, collapsing their base or exposing their joints to attacks. They lack the precise body alignment needed for stability.

The key is understanding your center of gravity and maintaining a wide, stable base with your feet and hips, creating a strong, unmovable structure.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a compromised position (e.g., near the ground after a sweep attempt), immediately extend your legs to create distance.
    2. Drive your hips back and down, lowering your center of gravity towards the mat.
    3. Place your feet flat on the mat, hip-width apart or slightly wider, toes pointing slightly outwards.
    4. Keep your knees bent at approximately 90 degrees, aligned over your toes.
    5. Engage your core muscles to create a rigid torso, preventing your back from rounding.
    6. Your hands can be on the mat for support, palms flat, fingers spread, or extended forward to create frames.
    7. Maintain a neutral spine, avoiding excessive arching or rounding, keeping your head up and looking forward.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly lifting your hips too high and extending your legs straight: This exposes your lower back to hyperextension injuries and makes you unstable, leading to a "banana back" injury.
    • Placing knees too far forward or inwards: This puts excessive stress on the knee joint, risking ligament tears (ACL/MCL) due to valgus or varus collapse.
    • Rounding your back excessively while attempting to stand: This compresses the lumbar spine, risking disc injuries and nerve impingement.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip-down, knee-bent, foot placement sequence without any resistance. (50 reps)
    2. Assisted drilling: Have a partner gently push you from the side while you practice maintaining your base. (25 reps per side)
    3. Controlled partner drilling: Start from a neutral position and have your partner attempt a light sweep. Your goal is to immediately execute Stand In Base. (10 reps per pair)
    4. Introduction to movement: Your partner attempts a light sweep, and you execute Stand In Base, then immediately transition to a basic guard retention. (15 reps per pair)
    5. Low-intensity positional sparring: Focus on specific scenarios where Stand In Base is likely needed (e.g., after a failed takedown attempt). (5 minutes, 25% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Apply Stand In Base as a natural reaction to losing your base during rolling. (10 minutes, 50% resistance)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Immediately after being swept and losing your guard control.
    • When your opponent successfully breaks your guard and is posturing up.
    • After a failed takedown attempt where you end up on your knees.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Opponent attempts a leg drag: If your opponent tries to drag your leg across your body, widen your base by pushing your hips back and digging your feet into the mat, maintaining your knee bend to absorb the pressure and prevent them from isolating a leg.
    • Opponent attempts a knee-slice pass: Maintain a wide base and keep your knees bent. Use your hands to frame against their hips or thighs, preventing them from driving their knee into your center line and collapsing your base.
    • Opponent attempts to stack you: Keep your hips low and your core engaged. If they try to stack, drive your feet into the mat and use your hips to create upward pressure, preventing them from driving your head and shoulders down.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Stand In Base?

    Your lower back pain likely stems from hyperextending your spine when lifting your hips too high and straightening your legs. Instead, focus on driving your hips *down* towards the mat while keeping your knees bent. This lowers your center of gravity and protects your lumbar spine by maintaining a neutral or slightly flexed posture.

    Q: How do I Stand In Base against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Prioritize lowering your center of gravity significantly by bending your knees deeply and pushing your hips far back. Use your feet as anchors, digging them into the mat to create a wide, stable base. Your hands can be used to frame against their hips or thighs to create distance and prevent them from collapsing your structure.

    Q: Should I always try to get completely upright when I Stand In Base?

    No, you don't always need to stand completely upright. The primary goal of Stand In Base is to achieve a stable, controlled position from which you can either defend or re-engage. Often, a low, wide, crouched base with bent knees and engaged core is more effective and safer for white belts than attempting a full stand-up, especially if your opponent is still pressuring.
