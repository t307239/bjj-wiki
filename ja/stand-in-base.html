Stand In Base: The Essential BJJ Transition for White Belts

Stand In Base is a crucial transition, typically from a broken guard or failed sweep, to re-establish a stable, upright base.

White belts often rush this, leading to loss of balance and immediate submission opportunities.

The key is controlled, deliberate movement with a wide, low base, creating an unshakeable foundation.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From guard recovery or scramble, grip opponent's collar (lapel grip) with your right hand and their opposite wrist (cross-collar grip) with your left hand.
    2. Simultaneously, place your left knee firmly on the mat, directly beneath your left hip, creating a 90-degree angle at the knee.
    3. Drive your right knee towards the mat, aiming to place it slightly behind your right hip, maintaining a wide base.
    4. Shift your weight downwards, driving your hips towards the mat while keeping your chest proud and upright.
    5. Ensure your base width (distance between your knees) is at least shoulder-width apart to prevent easy sweeps.
    6. Maintain constant upward pressure through your grips, preventing the opponent from pulling you down.
    7. Your body should form a stable tripod: two knees on the mat and your upper body as the third point of contact, balanced over your hips.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly placing knees too close together allows the opponent to easily use a "head and arm" control to tilt you, risking a knee ligament tear (MCL/ACL) due to uncontrolled lateral force.
    • The correct alternative is to maintain a base width at least shoulder-width apart, keeping knees tracking over toes.
    • Standing up too quickly without establishing hip control and weight transfer can lead to a "slam" or uncontrolled fall, risking spinal injury or concussion.
    • The correct alternative is to lower your hips significantly before attempting to rise, using grips to control the opponent's posture.
    • Forgetting to grip the opponent's collar or sleeve allows them to easily push you away or circle out, potentially leading to a knee hyperextension if your leg gets caught.
    • The correct alternative is to always secure at least one strong grip on the opponent's upper body before initiating the stand-up.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip and knee placement with imaginary opponent. 50 reps.
    2. Solo drilling with resistance band: Place band around your hips, practice lowering and widening base. 50 reps.
    3. Partner drill (static): Partner lies on their back, you practice Stand In Base from a broken guard scenario. 5 reps each side.
    4. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner actively resists by trying to pull you down. Focus on maintaining base. 5 reps each side, 25% resistance.
    5. Partner drill (controlled movement): Partner attempts to pass guard after you establish Stand In Base. You focus on maintaining base and transitioning to guard. 5 reps each side, 50% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Integrate Stand In Base into rolling situations, focusing on re-establishing guard or passing. 2 rounds, 75% resistance.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After a failed sweep attempt where your guard is broken and you are on your knees.
    • When the opponent has achieved a dominant position (e.g., side control) and you are trying to recover guard from your knees.
    • During scrambles where balance is lost, and you need to reset to a stable, upright position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Bump Sweep Counter: If the opponent attempts a hip bump sweep while you are standing up, widen your base further, lower your hips, and use your collar grip to pull their head towards your hip.
    • Leg Drag Counter: If the opponent attempts a leg drag, immediately bring your free leg back to a wide base and use your grips to prevent them from isolating and pulling your leg.
    • Arm Drag Counter: If the opponent secures an arm drag, use your collar grip to pull them closer and plant your opposite knee firmly, creating a strong base to resist the pull.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Stand In Base?

    Knee pain often arises from improper knee alignment and insufficient hip control. If your knees are too close together, or your hips are not driven low enough, lateral forces from the opponent can easily stress your knee ligaments. Ensure your knees are at least shoulder-width apart and your hips are significantly lower than your shoulders when you plant them.

    Q: How do I Stand In Base against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary advantage is control and leverage. Focus on securing strong collar and sleeve grips to disrupt their posture. Drive your hips down and widen your base even further to make yourself a heavier, more stable target. Use their weight against them by not allowing them to gain full control of your upper body.

    Q: Should I always try to get back to my feet when I Stand In Base?

    Not necessarily. Stand In Base is fundamentally about establishing a stable, low base. From this position, you can choose to either: 1) re-establish your guard by bringing your legs back into play, or 2) use the stability to initiate a takedown yourself. The goal is control, not just standing up.
