Stand In Base is a fundamental transition used to re-establish a strong, upright posture from various guard positions, particularly when your opponent attempts to pass. It allows you to regain control and set up your own offensive or defensive sequences.

White belts often struggle because they lack the nuanced weight distribution and framing required, leading to them being easily swept or passed. They try to muscle their way up instead of using leverage and balance.

The key mechanical insight is to create a stable platform by widening your base, lowering your hips, and actively pushing into your opponent's base, making yourself a heavy, immovable object.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a closed guard, establish collar grips with both hands on the opponent's gi lapels, approximately one inch from the opponent's neck.
    2. Simultaneously, secure wrist grips on the opponent's gi sleeves, just above the elbow joint.
    3. Begin to elevate your hips slightly off the mat, creating a small gap between your pelvis and the mat.
    4. Drive your knees outward, widening your base to a comfortable shoulder-width apart, ensuring your feet are flat on the mat.
    5. Shift your weight forward, pushing your hips towards your opponent's hips, creating a strong forward pressure.
    6. With your collar and wrist grips, pull your opponent forward and down, forcing them to bend at their waist.
    7. As they bend, simultaneously extend your legs, driving your feet into the mat and pushing your hips further forward, creating a stable upright base.
    8. Maintain a neutral spine, avoiding arching your back, and keep your head up and eyes forward.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly pushing up with straight legs: This can place excessive stress on the knees and lower back, potentially leading to ligament tears (ACL/MCL) or lumbar disc herniation.
    • Gripping too low on the sleeves: This allows the opponent to easily break your grip and pass, and can lead to wrist sprains if the opponent forcefully rotates your wrist.
    • Allowing hips to remain high and narrow base: This creates an unstable platform, making you susceptible to sweeps and potentially causing ankle or knee sprains from awkward falls.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip elevation, base widening, and forward weight shift without a partner. Focus on feeling the stability. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner stands passively. Execute Stand In Base from closed guard, focusing on grip integrity and weight transfer. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to your upward movement, but does not actively attempt to pass. Focus on maintaining your base. (20 reps per side)
    4. Controlled guard passing drill: Partner attempts a very slow, predictable guard pass. You execute Stand In Base to re-establish your guard. (15 reps per side)
    5. Flow rolling (50% resistance): Execute Stand In Base when the opportunity arises during light sparring. Focus on smooth transitions and maintaining your posture. (5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (75-90% resistance): Actively look for opportunities to use Stand In Base in sparring. Adapt to your partner's pressure and timing. (5 minutes)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is attempting a knee-slide pass and their weight is forward.
    • When your opponent tries to pry open your closed guard with their hands and elbows.
    • When you feel your opponent's base is compromised and they are leaning too far forward.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guillotine choke: If the opponent drives forward aggressively into your Stand In Base attempt, secure a head-and-arm grip and fall back to finish the choke.
    • Scissor sweep: As you establish your upright base, if the opponent is still low, you can use your legs to sweep them by extending one leg and scissor-ing the other.
    • Back take: If the opponent reacts poorly to your Stand In Base and exposes their back, you can transition to a back mount attempt by circling your hips.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Stand In Base from closed guard?

    Your lower back pain likely stems from arching your back excessively instead of using your hips and legs for power. To fix this, focus on engaging your core muscles and driving your hips forward with your legs, keeping your spine neutral. Think of it as pushing your opponent away with your entire body, not just your back.

    Q: How do I Stand In Base effectively against a much larger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Ensure your collar grips are deep and your wrist grips are secure to control their posture. Focus on creating a wide, stable base by pushing your knees out and keeping your feet flat. Drive your hips forward with maximum force, using their weight against them to create an unstable platform for them.

    Q: When is the best moment to try Stand In Base, and should I always go for it?

    The optimal moment is when you sense your opponent is committing to a forward pass, especially if their base is narrow or compromised. You don't always have to go for it; it's a strategic option. If your opponent has a very strong, stable base and is not actively passing, other guard retention or sweep options might be more effective. Look for their forward momentum.
