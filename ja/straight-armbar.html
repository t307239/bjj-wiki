BJJ Straight Armbar Technique for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Straight Armbar attacks the elbow joint from dominant positions like closed guard or mount, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's arm.

White belts often struggle by trying to force the submission with raw strength, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key is isolating the arm and creating a fulcrum with your hips to leverage their own strength against them.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From Closed Guard: Secure a deep grip on the opponent's gi sleeve (or wrist in no-gi) with your right hand. Simultaneously, your left hand secures their gi collar or wrist opposite the attacking arm.
    2. Create Space: Use your legs to create a small gap between your hips and their torso. Angle your hips slightly to your left.
    3. Isolate the Arm: Bring your right knee up and across their chest, trapping their attacking arm between your thighs. Your left leg remains anchored on their hip.
    4. Hip Elevation: Drive your hips upwards, perpendicular to the mat, creating upward pressure on their shoulder.
    5. Body Alignment: Lie back, aligning your spine with their attacking arm. Your body should form a straight line from your hips to your head, with their arm running along your torso.
    6. Grip Transition: Release the collar/wrist grip. Bring your left foot over their head, securing it behind their neck to prevent them from posturing up.
    7. Finishing Mechanics: Grip your own biceps (the arm that is underneath their shoulder). Maintain hip elevation and gently push your hips forward, creating a lever action that hyperextends their elbow.
    8. Controlled Release: If the opponent taps, immediately release all pressure and return to a neutral position.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Arm Isolation: Grabbing the opponent's arm loosely and trying to 'pull' their arm straight leads to elbow hyperextension injury. The correct action is to trap the arm firmly between your thighs, controlling its angle.
    • Premature Hip Elevation: Lifting your hips before the arm is properly isolated and controlled can cause their shoulder to dislocate or their arm to bend unnaturally. Always ensure the arm is secured first, then elevate hips.
    • Excessive Force without Control: Forcing the submission by pushing down on their elbow with your legs without proper hip elevation and fulcrum creation can damage their elbow ligaments. Focus on the lever action created by your hips.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Elevation Drill: From a supine position, practice the hip elevation and body alignment mechanics without a partner. (50 reps)
    2. Closed Guard Arm Isolation Drill: With a partner, practice opening guard, isolating one arm by trapping it between your thighs, and re-establishing guard. (20 reps per side)
    3. Controlled Armbar Entry Drill: Partner lies supine. You are in closed guard. Practice the steps of creating space, isolating the arm, and bringing your knee over their head. Partner offers no resistance. (15 reps per side)
    4. Static Armbar Finish Drill (0% Resistance): Partner remains supine and allows you to secure the armbar. You practice the grip transition, hip elevation, and gentle finishing pressure. Partner taps immediately. (10 reps per side)
    5. Controlled Resistance Drill (25% Resistance): Partner in closed guard offers very light resistance to arm isolation and posture. You practice the full sequence, focusing on smooth transitions. Partner provides minimal defense. (10 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling Application (50% Resistance): In live rolling, consciously look for opportunities to set up the armbar from closed guard. Focus on executing the mechanics correctly, not on forcing the submission. Partner actively defends but allows for technique practice.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent postures up significantly in your closed guard, creating an opportunity to isolate an arm.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and leaves an arm extended or vulnerable.
    • When you achieve a dominant position like mount and the opponent defends by reaching across their body.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Escape the Trap" Counter: If the opponent traps your attacking arm between their thighs, immediately drive your elbow back towards your own body to create space and prevent the isolation. Then, attempt to regain guard or posture up.
    • The "Spin Under" Defense: If the opponent starts to bring their leg over your head, immediately try to spin your body underneath their armpit, creating a scramble to escape the position or transition to a different attack.
    • The "Reguard" Counter: If you feel the armbar being applied but can't finish, focus on bringing your legs back to your chest, re-establishing a strong closed guard, and creating distance to reset.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my elbow hurt when I try to do a Straight Armbar from closed guard?

    Your elbow likely hurts because you are trying to 'pull' the opponent's arm straight or are not properly isolating it. This can cause hyperextension of the elbow joint, damaging ligaments. Ensure you trap the arm firmly between your thighs and use your hips to create a lever, rather than raw pulling force.

    Q: How can I do a Straight Armbar against a much bigger and stronger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, precision in isolation is paramount. Focus on creating a small space with your hips, trapping their arm tightly between your thighs, and then elevating your hips. Leverage is more important than strength; your hips create the fulcrum against their shoulder, not brute force against their elbow.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a Straight Armbar from closed guard, and what if they defend by tucking their arm?

    The best time is when they posture up and reach for your collar or sleeves, leaving an arm accessible. If they tuck their arm, this often means they are trying to prevent the isolation. You can use this to your advantage by switching to a grip on their other arm or leg to break their posture and create a new opening, or transition to a sweep.
