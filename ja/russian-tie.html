Russian Tie: A Precise BJJ Takedown Guide for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

The Russian Tie is a standing control position initiated from a collar tie, aiming to disrupt balance and set up a takedown. White belts often rush, neglecting the subtle weight shifts required for effectiveness.

The key is understanding how to use your body's leverage to create an unresolvable imbalance, not just brute force.

Mastering the hip angle and precise weight transfer is paramount to achieving a dominant position and executing the takedown safely.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Opponent is standing, you are facing them. Grab opponent's right collar with your left hand (Left Collar Grip).
    2. Immediately place your right forearm across the back of opponent's neck, trapping their head (Head Control).
    3. Your left elbow should be tight to your own body, creating a strong frame.
    4. Step your left foot slightly forward and to the outside of opponent's right foot, creating a small angle.
    5. Rotate your hips inward, towards your opponent's left side, while simultaneously leaning your upper body forward.
    6. Drive your right shoulder into opponent's upper back, just below the neck.
    7. Shift your weight from your left foot onto your right foot, then onto your left foot again, creating a subtle "hitchhiking" motion with your hips to off-balance them to their right.
    8. As opponent resists or falls, use your head control and collar grip to guide them down.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Attempting to "pull hard" with your collar grip while your body is square: This can hyperextend your own shoulder, damaging the rotator cuff and labrum.

    Correct: Maintain a stable frame and use hip rotation and weight transfer, not just arm strength.

    • Stepping directly forward with your lead leg without angling hips: This squares you up, allowing opponent to easily counter with a sprawl or grip fight, potentially twisting your knee if you force the movement.

    Correct: Step forward and slightly to the outside, creating a dynamic angle.

    • Overextending your neck or bending your spine to "lean" on opponent: This puts your cervical spine at risk of hyperextension or flexion, leading to neck pain or injury.

    Correct: Maintain a neutral spine and use your hips and core to drive the movement.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip sequence and hip rotation without a partner. Focus on feeling the weight transfer. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drilling (static): Partner stands still. You execute the Russian Tie and a controlled takedown (e.g., trip). Partner offers no resistance. (25 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to your initial grip and movement. You focus on breaking their posture and completing the takedown. (25 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    4. Flow drilling: Execute the Russian Tie as part of a sequence. Partner can attempt to defend or transition, but no aggressive counters. (10 rounds x 1 minute, 75% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Russian Tie during positional sparring, focusing on timing and execution. Partner can defend normally. (5 rounds x 2 minutes, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (full): Attempt the Russian Tie during live rolling. Focus on recognizing opportunities and executing the technique under full pressure. (10 rounds x 3 minutes, 100% resistance).

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is reaching for your collar or sleeves, creating an opening for a collar tie.
    • When opponent is posturing up aggressively, providing a stable target for the head control.
    • When you have established a strong collar grip and opponent is not actively defending their head.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent sprawls aggressively: As you drive forward, if opponent sprawls their hips back and down, immediately transition to a double leg takedown by lowering your level and securing their legs.
    • Grip release and base shift: If opponent breaks your collar grip and widens their base, step back and circle out to reset your position, avoiding being pulled off balance.
    • Snap down and transition: If opponent attempts to pull their head out, use the collar grip to snap their head down and transition to a front headlock or guillotine choke setup.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Russian Tie?

    Neck pain during the Russian Tie often stems from an incorrect head control and body alignment. If you are bending your neck to force the connection or using your head as a primary lever, you risk hyperextension or compression. Ensure your spine remains neutral and your upper body is driving the movement through your hips and core, with the forearm on the back of the neck acting as a control point, not a fulcrum for pain.

    Q: How do I Russian Tie against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing become even more critical. Focus on maintaining a tight frame with your arm across their neck and your elbow tucked. Instead of relying on pure driving force, emphasize hip rotation and a subtle, precise weight transfer that exploits their weight distribution. Aim to off-balance them to their side, making their size a disadvantage rather than an advantage.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Russian Tie in a BJJ match?

    The Russian Tie is most effective when your opponent is actively engaging in the upper body, such as when they are reaching for your collar or sleeves, or when they have just attempted a strike or a takedown themselves. It's also a good opportunity when your opponent is posturing up, as this creates a stable target for your head control and allows for a more effective disruption of their balance. Avoid attempting it when your opponent has a very strong, wide base and is actively resisting grip fighting.
