The Russian Tie is a fundamental standing technique initiated from a collar-and-sleeve or collar-and-collar grip, aiming to off-balance and take your opponent down.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of coordinated weight transfer and a tendency to rely on brute strength rather than leverage.

The key mechanical insight is using your head and shoulder pressure to break their posture and create an angle for a powerful hip-driven takedown.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Grip: Opponent's left lapel with your right hand (collar grip), and opponent's right sleeve with your left hand (sleeve grip). Maintain a fist grip, not an open palm.
    2. Head and Shoulder Pressure: Drive your right shoulder into the opponent's upper chest/collarbone area, simultaneously turning your head to their left side, making contact just below their chin.
    3. Weight Transfer (Initial): Shift your weight slightly onto your lead (left) leg, creating a small pivot point.
    4. Pulling Arm Action: With your left hand, pull down on their right sleeve, drawing their arm across their body towards your right side.
    5. Hips and Body Angle: As you pull the sleeve and apply head pressure, rotate your hips counter-clockwise, bringing your right hip closer to their left hip.
    6. Stepping Action: Step your right foot forward and slightly to the outside of their right foot.
    7. Takedown Drive: Simultaneously, drive your hips forward and execute a sweeping motion with your right leg, lowering your level and using your head/shoulder to push them over your right hip.
    8. Weight Distribution: Your weight should be transferred forward and onto your right leg, driving through to the mat as they fall.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Head Placement: Driving your head directly into their forehead or nose can cause concussions or facial fractures for both participants.
    • Correct: Drive your shoulder into their upper chest and your head against the side of their neck/upper shoulder, creating a stable wedge.
    • Jerking Arm Motion: Yanking their sleeve with excessive force without proper body rotation can strain your shoulder and elbow joints.
    • Correct: Use your pulling arm in conjunction with your hip rotation and body weight to guide their arm across.
    • Standing Too Tall: Attempting the takedown with your hips high and back straight exposes your base and makes you vulnerable to sweeps or throws, also risking lumbar spine hyperextension.
    • Correct: Lower your hips by bending your knees and driving your weight forward and down.

    ドリル段階

    1. Grip and Posture Practice (0% resistance): Stand opposite a partner, practice establishing the collar-and-sleeve grip and applying head/shoulder pressure without moving. (10 reps each side)
    2. Controlled Body Rotation Drill (25% resistance): With grips and pressure, practice rotating your hips and turning your body, guiding their arm across. Partner offers minimal resistance. (10 reps each side)
    3. Single Leg Step and Sweep (50% resistance): Practice the stepping motion and the initial hip drive and sweep with your leg. Partner provides light support. (10 reps each side)
    4. Russian Tie to Controlled Fall (75% resistance): Execute the full Russian Tie motion. Partner is instructed to fall safely with the movement, not resist. (5 reps each side)
    5. Live Takedown Attempts (90% resistance): Attempt the Russian Tie against a resisting opponent who is actively trying to defend. Focus on execution, not necessarily completion. (5 attempts each side)
    6. Live Rolling with Takedown Focus (100% resistance): Incorporate the Russian Tie into live sparring, aiming to use it as a primary takedown option. (3 minutes of rolling, focusing on takedowns)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is reaching for your collar or sleeves, creating an opening for grip fighting.
    • When your opponent is postured up and you have a strong collar grip, allowing you to break their posture.
    • When your opponent attempts a collar-and-sleeve grip on you, creating a mirror opportunity.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Sprawl and Circle: As they apply head pressure and begin to rotate, immediately sprawl your hips back and circle your hips to your right, breaking their angle and clearing their head pressure.
    • Grip Break and Posture Up: Focus on breaking their collar grip by prying their hand open with your thumb and forefinger, then immediately popping your head up and widening your base.
    • Counter-Rotation and Hip Bump: As they drive their hip in, counter-rotate your hips by bumping them with your left hip while simultaneously stepping your left foot back to widen your base and resist the takedown.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try the Russian Tie?

    Shoulder pain often stems from incorrect grip fighting and pulling mechanics. If you're yanking their sleeve with an extended arm, you're putting torque on your shoulder joint. The correct method involves using your body weight and hip rotation to pull their arm across, keeping your elbow bent and your pulling arm closer to your body. Imagine guiding their arm rather than ripping it.

    Q: How do I Russian Tie against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and head pressure are paramount. Focus on driving your shoulder deep into their chest and using your head to create a strong wedge, preventing them from posturing up. Your hip rotation needs to be explosive, and your step needs to be precise to get your hips in a position to lift or sweep them. Don't try to overpower them; use their size against them by creating an unstable base for them.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Russian Tie in BJJ?

    The Russian Tie is most effective when your opponent is reaching for your collar or sleeves, creating an opportunity for you to secure grips and initiate a grip fight. It's also a great option when your opponent is postured high, as you can use the head and shoulder pressure to break their posture down and then transition into the takedown. It's a proactive technique, best used when you see an opening rather than as a reactive response.
