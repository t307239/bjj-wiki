The Back Take is a transition from a dominant position, usually guard, to the opponent's back, achieving a highly advantageous attacking platform.

White belts often fail by lunging or using brute force, leading to loss of position or injury instead of controlled transition.

The key is using your hips to create space and your grips to control the opponent's posture, allowing your body to follow naturally.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Starting from Closed Guard: Establish two collar grips, one on the opponent's gi lapel near their neck, the other on the opposite lapel at chest level. Your forearms should be tight against their shoulders.
    2. Hip Elevation: Drive your hips up towards the ceiling, creating a slight gap between your hips and theirs. This is not a large movement, just enough to initiate separation.
    3. Frame Creation: As your hips lift, extend your arms, pushing their shoulders away slightly with your collar grips. This creates a small frame and breaks their posture.
    4. Leg Pummeling (Right side example): Peel your right leg off their hip, bringing your knee towards your chest. Simultaneously, swing your right foot to the outside of their right hip, planting it firmly on the mat.
    5. Hip Rotation and Seatbelt Grip: Rotate your hips 90 degrees towards your right, facing their right side. As you rotate, secure a "seatbelt grip" on their back: right arm over their shoulder, left arm under their armpit, with your hands clasped on your own right bicep.
    6. Body Connection: Drive your chest into their back, creating tight contact. Your hips should be lower than their hips.
    7. Leg Trap and Control: Bring your left leg up and over their right leg, trapping it between your thigh and calf. Your right leg should now be positioned to hook their left leg.
    8. Secure Back Mount: Continue to drive forward, bringing your hips to the mat on their right side. Your right leg hooks their left leg, and your left leg is now positioned to secure the "hooks" (feet on the inside of their thighs), completing the Back Mount.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Lunging forward with your head and shoulders: This can cause hyperextension or sprains in your neck and shoulders as the opponent may push back or fall unexpectedly.
    • Forcing leg over opponent's head during transition: This can lead to knee ligament tears (ACL/MCL) if their body shifts or you apply too much torque.
    • Gripping too loosely on the collar or arm: This allows the opponent to escape posture and break your grip, potentially leading to a sweep or reversal where your own limbs are exposed to hyperextension.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Lift and Frame Drill: Practice lifting your hips and extending your arms to create frames against an imaginary opponent. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Leg Pummel and Hip Rotation: Practice the leg peel, outside foot placement, and 90-degree hip rotation without resistance. (50 reps per side)
    3. Partner Drill - Controlled Transition (0% resistance): Partner stands, you are in closed guard. Practice the entire Back Take sequence, with your partner offering minimal to no resistance, allowing you to focus on body mechanics. (10 reps)
    4. Partner Drill - Light Resistance (25% resistance): Partner offers slight pressure to prevent easy hip escape, but still allows for controlled movement. Focus on maintaining frame and hip movement. (10 reps)
    5. Partner Drill - Posture Control (50% resistance): Partner attempts to maintain good posture. Focus on breaking posture and executing the leg pummel and seatbelt grip. (10 reps)
    6. Live Rolling (75-90% resistance): Attempt the Back Take during rolling. Focus on recognizing opportunities and executing the mechanics under pressure. (5-10 attempts per round)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Opponent leans too far forward in your guard, breaking their posture.
    • Opponent attempts a guard pass and their weight shifts to one side, creating an opening.
    • Opponent is turtled or on their hands and knees, exposing their back.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • When opponent tries to peel your leg off: Immediately re-establish your guard or shrimp your hips to create space and prevent them from securing the seatbelt. Maintain tight body connection.
    • When opponent secures a strong seatbelt grip: Focus on defending the neck by tucking your chin and making your neck as small as possible. Use your free arm to block their choking arm if possible. Try to turn into them to relieve pressure.
    • When opponent attempts to roll you to the other side: Keep your hips low and use your legs to maintain control of their hips and legs. Drive your chest into their back to prevent them from completing the roll.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to Back Take from Closed Guard?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are leading the transition with your head or neck instead of using your hips and core. When you lift your hips to create space, ensure your head stays neutral and your gaze is forward, not down. The movement should originate from your posterior chain and core, not your cervical spine.

    Q: How do I Back Take from Closed Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on leveraging their weight against them. Instead of a powerful hip lift, use a subtle hip escape to create a small space, then execute the leg pummel and hip rotation. Your seatbelt grip is crucial; ensure your arm is high over their shoulder and your hand is clasped securely. Drive your chest into their back to maintain connection and use your legs to control their hips.

    Q: I keep losing the Back Take when I get the seatbelt grip. What am I doing wrong?

    You are likely losing the Back Take because you are not maintaining sufficient body connection or are allowing your hips to get too far away. Once the seatbelt grip is secured, immediately drive your chest into their back, creating a "hugging" sensation. Use your legs to "climb" their body, bringing your hooks in tightly. Keep your hips low and drive forward to prevent them from turning or escaping.
