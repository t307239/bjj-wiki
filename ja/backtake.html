BJJ Back Take: The Essential White Belt Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

This technique initiates from a position where your opponent is on their hands and knees, or has fallen to their side.

The goal is to secure a dominant position with control of the back, leading to submission opportunities.

Many white belts struggle due to incorrect weight distribution and neglecting fundamental body positioning.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From your opponent's guard pass attempt (e.g., knee-slice), establish a strong "seatbelt" grip: right hand under their left armpit to their right collarbone, left hand on their right shoulder.
    2. Drive your chest into their back, aligning your spine parallel to theirs, creating a tight connection.
    3. With your hips low and slightly forward, drive your left knee across their body, aiming to place it inside their left hip crease.
    4. Simultaneously, secure a "hook" with your right foot, placing it on the outside of their left thigh, knee bent at 90 degrees.
    5. Shift your weight forward, using your chest to push their head down and away from your center line.
    6. Peel your left hand off their shoulder and reach for their left sleeve or wrist, securing a "control grip".
    7. Drive your right knee forward and in, sweeping their left leg out as you pivot your hips to face their side, securing the back mount.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Neck Strain: Grabbing your opponent's head with a "neck crank" grip (thumb wrapping around the back of the head) can cause cervical spine hyperextension or hyperflexion, damaging the neck ligaments and muscles.
    • Correct: Maintain a "seatbelt" grip on the collarbone and shoulder, keeping your head up and chest connected.
    • Knee Injury: Extending your knee forcefully against a resisting opponent's hip or leg can lead to ACL or MCL tears, especially if your foot is stuck.
    • Correct: Drive your knee *inside* the opponent's hip, creating a stable base and controlling their base.
    • Shoulder Dislocation: Attempting to "pull" your opponent's head down to your chest without proper body alignment can lead to your shoulder joint being forced out of its socket (anterior shoulder dislocation).
    • Correct: Use your chest and hip drive to control their posture, not brute arm strength.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Movement (0%): Practice the hip drive and knee-in motion without a partner, focusing on smooth transitions from seatbelt to hook. (10 reps)
    2. Partnered Drills - Static (25%): Your partner lies on their stomach. You establish the seatbelt grip and practice driving your hips and bringing your knee across, securing the hook. (10 reps)
    3. Partnered Drills - Resisted Hip (50%): Your partner offers light resistance with their hips. You practice the hip drive and knee-in motion to get to the hook. (10 reps)
    4. Partnered Drills - Resisted Transition (75%): Your partner resists the transition to back mount. You practice maintaining the hooks and controlling their posture as you pivot. (10 reps)
    5. Situational Sparring - Guard Pass Defense (90%): Start from a common guard pass scenario where the opponent is on hands and knees. Focus on securing the back take as they attempt to pass. (5 min round)
    6. Live Rolling (100%): Apply the back take during regular sparring, looking for opportunities when the opponent exposes their back. (1-2 rounds)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts a knee-slice or side-control pass and their hips are low.
    • When your opponent turns away from you after a failed sweep or takedown.
    • When you have successfully defended a takedown and are in a scramble on the ground.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Defending the Hooks: If your opponent secures hooks, immediately try to "post" your foot (extend your leg) on their hip or thigh to break their base and create space to turn into them.
    • "Uncle" Sweep: If the opponent has one hook and is driving in, attempt to "uncle" sweep by framing their knee with your forearm and bridging to create an angle and escape.
    • Turning to Face: If the opponent gets a strong seatbelt but fails to secure hooks, turn your hips towards their leg and try to face them, creating a scramble or guard recovery opportunity.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to take the back in BJJ?

    This often happens because you are using your lower back muscles to "pull" your opponent into position instead of using your hips and core. When you drive your hips forward and align your spine with theirs, you engage your glutes and hamstrings, which are stronger and can support the movement better. Ensure your chest is glued to their back and you are "humping" with your hips, not arching your back.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, precision in your grips and weight distribution is paramount. Focus on securing the "seatbelt" grip with your choking arm under their armpit and your other hand on their shoulder. Drive your hips forward to create a tight connection, then use your knee to get *inside* their hip, creating leverage. Don't try to muscle them; use your body weight and angles to disrupt their balance and secure the hooks.

    Q: The most opportune moment to transition to a back take from closed guard is when your opponent attempts to "stand up" or "pass" by posturing high and breaking your leg control. As they create space to stand, you can use your legs to "climb" their back, securing hooks as they try to disengage. This often involves a "hip bump" or "technical stand-up" motion to create the initial angle for the climb.
