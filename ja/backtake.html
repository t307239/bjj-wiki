BJJ Back Take: A Precise Guide for White Belts

This technique transitions from a position where your opponent is facing away from you, aiming to secure the back mount. White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage and timing.

The key is to control their hips and create a situation where their base is compromised, allowing you to secure the dominant back position.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. Start from a position where opponent's back is exposed (e.g., after a sweep or failed guard pass).
    2. Secure a "seatbelt grip" on the opponent's torso: right hand grips opponent's left collar, left hand grips opponent's right armpit from underneath.
    3. Place your right knee inside the opponent's right hip crease, angling your hips 45 degrees to their left.
    4. Drive your left shoulder into the opponent's left shoulder blade, creating a wedge.
    5. Shift your weight forward onto your right knee and left foot, pulling the opponent's head towards your chest.
    6. As the opponent's weight shifts forward, step your left foot across their body, aiming to place it on the mat behind their right hip.
    7. Immediately secure "hooks" with your feet: left foot hooks opponent's right inner thigh, right foot hooks opponent's left inner thigh, forming a "figure four" with your legs.
    8. Pull the opponent's torso down and into your chest, maintaining control of the seatbelt grip and your hooks.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrect Hip Angle: Attempting to step over with hips squared to the opponent can lead to knee hyperextension and ACL/MCL tears as the opponent resists.

    Correct Movement: Angle hips 45 degrees to opponent's side, creating a lever.

    • Over-Reliance on Strength: Pulling the opponent's torso with pure arm strength without hip leverage can cause shoulder impingement and rotator cuff strains.

    Correct Movement: Use your hips and chest to drive and control their posture.

    • Forced Head Position: Trying to force the opponent's head down with excessive neck pressure can result in cervical spine strain or whiplash injuries.

    Correct Movement: Guide the head towards your chest using your shoulder and seatbelt grip.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Hip Placement & Hook Setup: Practice planting your right knee and hooking with your left foot from a supine position, repeating 20 times.
    2. Solo Seatbelt Grip & Shoulder Drive: Mimic the seatbelt grip and shoulder drive motion without a partner, 20 repetitions.
    3. Partner Drill - Static Position: Partner lies prone. You practice securing seatbelt, knee placement, and shoulder drive, holding for 10 seconds, 10 repetitions.
    4. Partner Drill - Controlled Transition (25% resistance): Partner helps by lightly resisting. Focus on hip angle and stepping over, 10 repetitions each side.
    5. Partner Drill - Semi-Live (50% resistance): Partner offers moderate resistance. Execute the full back take, aiming for hooks and control, 5 repetitions each side.
    6. Live Rolling (75-90% resistance): Attempt the back take against a resisting opponent, focusing on the mechanics learned, 3-5 rounds.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent attempts a guard pass and their back is exposed.
    • After successfully sweeping an opponent who lands on their hands and knees.
    • From a scramble where you end up behind a turtled opponent.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Defending the Seatbelt Grip: Opponent tucks their chin tightly to their chest and turns their hips away, creating a "wall" with their shoulder.

    Counter Mechanic: If the seatbelt is secured, the opponent must immediately try to turn their hips *towards* you, making it difficult to establish hooks.

    • Preventing Hook Establishment: Opponent drives their knee to the mat on the side you are trying to step over, blocking your leg.

    Counter Mechanic: The opponent can also try to "frame" with their arm against your hip to prevent you from closing the distance.

    • Escaping the Hooks: Opponent turns their hips aggressively into your hooks, trying to "untangle" your legs.

    Counter Mechanic: You can counter by "flattening" your hooks (extending your legs) or "squeezing" your knees together if they attempt to turn.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to take the back in BJJ?

    Lower back pain often stems from excessive arching of your spine when trying to pull the opponent towards you. Instead of arching, focus on driving your hips forward and down, using your core muscles. Ensure your seatbelt grip is tight and your shoulder is driving into their back to maintain control, rather than relying solely on lumbar extension.

    Q: How can I take the back of a much larger and stronger BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, pure strength is not your ally. Focus on precise biomechanics: use their forward momentum against them. When they try to posture up, that's your opportunity to drive your knee into their hip and use your shoulder as a wedge. Secure the seatbelt grip tightly, and prioritize getting your hooks in *before* attempting to pull them fully down.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a Back Take in BJJ?

    The optimal time is when the opponent's structure is compromised and their hips are off the mat. This commonly occurs during guard pass attempts where they lean too far forward, or immediately after a sweep where they land on their hands and knees. Look for moments of instability; a strong, stable opponent is much harder to attack from behind.
