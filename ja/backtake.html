This technique transitions you to the opponent's back, offering a dominant position for submission. It typically starts from a guard or a scrambled position.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and leverage, leading to inefficient movements. They might over-rely on strength rather than technique.

The key mechanical insight is controlling the opponent's hips and base while simultaneously creating an angle to secure the back, turning their momentum against them.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From closed guard, establish a collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel, and a sleeve grip with your left hand on their left sleeve, palm facing inwards. Your hips should be at a 45-degree angle relative to their torso.
    2. Slice your right leg between their legs, aiming to place your knee on the mat as your hips lower. Your weight shifts forward, creating space.
    3. As your right knee hits the mat, use your left hand to pull their left arm across your body, breaking their posture. Simultaneously, your right leg begins to hook their right hip.
    4. Drive your right hip into their left side, creating a strong lever. Your left arm continues to control their upper body, preventing them from turning into you.
    5. Untuck your right leg and immediately swing it over their back, securing a "seatbelt" grip: right arm over their neck, left arm under their armpit, both hands clasped on their back.
    6. With the seatbelt secured, drive your hips into them, bringing your knees closer together to create a tight body triangle. Your weight should be distributed over their hips, not their shoulders.
    7. Maintain constant pressure with your hips and squeeze your legs to restrict their movement. Your head should be tight to their neck, preventing them from creating space to escape.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly rotating your hips during the leg-switching phase can cause a hyperextension or twisting injury to your knee ligaments (ACL/MCL). Always ensure your knee tracks over your toes and your hips remain controlled.
    • Grabbing the opponent's collar too high or with a bent wrist can lead to wrist sprains or carpal tunnel irritation due to excessive flexion. Maintain a neutral wrist and a firm grip lower on the lapel.
    • Trying to pull the opponent's arm across without controlling their hips can lead to a shoulder dislocation or rotator cuff tear if they resist by falling away. Focus on hip connection and weight transfer first.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg-switching and hip-driving motion without a partner. Focus on smooth transitions and hip angle changes. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a willing partner, walk through the steps, focusing on precise grips and weight distribution. (25 reps)
    3. Passive partner drill: Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice securing the seatbelt and body triangle. (10 reps per side)
    4. Light resistance drill: Partner actively defends by trying to prevent the leg switch or escape the position. Focus on maintaining control. (5 reps per side)
    5. Controlled sparring (25% resistance): Attempt the back take from a specific starting position (e.g., closed guard) with a partner who is aware of the attempt and offers typical defense. (5 attempts)
    6. Live rolling (50% resistance): Integrate the back take attempt into live sparring. Focus on timing and reacting to your opponent's movements. (5 minutes)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their hips are within reach.
    • When you secure a strong grip on their collar and sleeve from closed guard, creating an opportunity to break their posture.
    • During scrambles where you find yourself behind your opponent with an opportunity to secure hooks.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Elbow-Knee Connection Defense": If the opponent tries to switch legs, immediately bring your elbow to your knee on the same side, creating a tight frame to prevent the leg from coming over. This maintains your base and prevents hip control.
    • "Hip Escape and Base Reinforcement": If the opponent secures hooks, immediately hip escape to create space and bring your knees to your chest, reforming a guard or establishing a base to prevent them from sinking the body triangle. Reinforce your base by placing your feet flat on the mat.
    • "Turning Inward": If the opponent has hooks but is not yet controlling your hips tightly, turn your body inward towards their chest, making it difficult for them to maintain the back mount and creating an opportunity to regain guard or escape.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to take the back from guard?

    Lower back pain often occurs when you arch your back excessively to compensate for a lack of hip drive and proper weight transfer. Instead of arching, focus on driving your hips forward and connecting them to your opponent's hips as you transition. Imagine scooping them with your hips rather than pulling with your upper body.

    Q: How can I take the back against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise angles are paramount. Do not try to match their strength. Focus on controlling their hips with your legs and using your grips to break their posture and create off-balancing opportunities. The key is to get your hips in a position to drive into their side, using their own weight against them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Back Take from closed guard?

    The ideal moment is when your opponent's posture is broken, and their hips are close to yours, typically when they are trying to establish a strong guard pass. Look for opportunities when they lean forward, exposing their neck and shoulders. This allows you to control their upper body while driving your hips to initiate the transition.
