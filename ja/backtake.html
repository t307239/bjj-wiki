This technique begins from a dominant position, usually after a sweep or guard pass, aiming to establish control of the opponent's back. It's a critical transition for submission.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of hip and shoulder mechanics, leading to overreaching and poor weight distribution. They try to 'force' the position instead of guiding it.

The key insight is using your hips to drive forward and 'climb' your hooks, rather than pulling the opponent's shoulders back. This creates a stable, dominant base.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From Side Control or Mount, establish a seatbelt grip: left hand grips opponent's right collar, right hand grips opponent's left collar. Keep elbows tight.
    2. Shift your hips laterally, aligning your left hip with opponent's left shoulder. Your weight should be on your left hip.
    3. Drive your chest into opponent's sternum, creating pressure and preventing them from turning into you.
    4. Release your right collar grip and immediately secure your right knee behind opponent's right hip, creating a "hook."
    5. Transfer weight to your right hip, and simultaneously bring your left knee to the opponent's left hip, securing the second hook.
    6. Your torso should now be perpendicular to the opponent's, with your chest against their upper back. Your hips are deep.
    7. Ensure your hooks are active: press your heels towards your own glutes, squeezing your thighs, to maintain control and leverage.
    8. Secure a "body triangle" or reinforce seatbelt grip by placing your right arm under opponent's chin and left arm behind their head, connecting hands.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Overreaching for the collar: This can strain the shoulder joint's rotator cuff ligaments as the elbow flares out, leading to tears. Always keep elbows tucked and use hip movement to close distance.
    • Forcing the hip rotation: Applying excessive torque to the lumbar spine when rotating the hips can cause facet joint sprains or disc injury. Rotate hips smoothly and in conjunction with chest pressure.
    • Knee placement on hooks: Placing knees too wide or not deep enough can expose your own hips and knees to hyperextension or twisting injuries. Ensure knees are tight to the opponent's hips and driven forward.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip shift, hook placement, and torso alignment with no resistance. Focus on smooth transitions. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from side control to back mount. Focus on grip accuracy and weight transfer. (25 reps)
    3. Controlled drilling (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and hip movement. Focus on maintaining control as they try to shrimp. (15 reps)
    4. Controlled drilling (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend by shrimping and bridging. Focus on keeping hooks tight and body perpendicular. (10 reps)
    5. Live rolling (75% resistance): Attempt the back take during rolling. Focus on timing and reacting to the opponent's movements. Don't force it if the opportunity isn't there. (5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Integrate the back take as a natural transition during sparring. Focus on maintaining control and finishing the submission. (10 minutes)

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent turtles effectively from side control or mount, exposing their back.
    • After a successful sweep where the opponent lands on their stomach or in a vulnerable position.
    • When the opponent attempts to shrimp away from side control, creating an opening for you to "climb."

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Turtle defense: If opponent attempts to climb hooks, immediately drive your hips into the mat and turn your shoulders towards the attacker, creating a "wall" with your arms and chest. This prevents the hooks from getting deep.
    • "X" defense: If the attacker loses one hook, immediately bring your leg across their hip and attempt to "x-guard" their hips to create space and prevent them from securing the second hook. This requires quick leg work.
    • "Upa" (Bridge) escape: If caught with one hook, bridge your hips powerfully towards the attacker's head, attempting to dislodge them and create space to turn into them or escape to a safer position.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to take the back from side control?

    This often happens when you overreach with your arms to grab the collar, extending your elbow and flaring your shoulder joint. Instead, keep your elbows tucked and use your hip rotation to drive your chest into their sternum, closing the distance. Then, secure the hook with your knee before transitioning your arm.

    Q: How do I take the back against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Focus on using your hips to drive forward and 'climb' your hooks rather than pulling their upper body. Maintain a tight chest-to-back connection and ensure your hooks are deep by actively pressing your heels towards your own glutes. This creates a strong base that their size cannot easily overcome.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a back take in BJJ?

    The opportune moment to attempt a back take is when your opponent is momentarily unbalanced or has exposed their back. This includes when they turtle, when they are trying to escape your side control by shrimping, or immediately after you've swept them and they land on their stomach. Look for these brief windows of vulnerability.
