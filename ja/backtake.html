Precise Back Take Technique for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White帯テクニック
📖 完全ガイドを見る → 白帯ガイド

This technique initiates from a position where your opponent is on their hands and knees, often after a failed guard pass or sweep. The goal is to secure a dominant back-mount position, controlling their torso and limbs for submission opportunities.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength and incorrect body positioning, leading to loss of control and potential injury. They miss the crucial sequencing of weight transfer and limb control.

The key mechanical insight is to use your hips to create a fulcrum, driving your shoulders over their hips while simultaneously securing your hooks, effectively 'climbing' onto their back.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From opponent on hands and knees, establish collar grip (right hand on opponent's left collar, left hand on opponent's right collar) with thumbs inside.
    2. Secure opponent's hip with your left hand, palm flat against their hip bone, creating a frame.
    3. Step your right foot outside of opponent's right hip, aligning your knee with their hip joint.
    4. Initiate a hip bump by driving your hips upwards and slightly to your right, forcing opponent's weight to their left.
    5. As opponent's weight shifts, drive your right shoulder over their right hip, creating leverage.
    6. Simultaneously, reach your left arm under opponent's left armpit and secure their far collar grip (left hand on opponent's right collar, thumbs inside).
    7. Drive your hips forward and up, transitioning your body weight onto their upper back while your legs establish hooks (feet connected behind opponent's thighs).
    8. Maintain tight chest-to-back pressure and control both collar grips to prevent escape.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Neck Injury Risk: When attempting to climb, if the opponent turtles aggressively, a white belt might crane their neck backward to avoid pressure. This can strain the cervical spine ligaments and muscles, especially if the opponent drives forward.
    • Correct Movement: Keep head neutral, focusing on driving hips up and shoulders over their hips, maintaining a strong connection to their back.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Grabbing the collar too high and pulling across the body, while simultaneously trying to drive forward, can cause the shoulder joint to impinge. The humerus can jam against the acromion process.
    • Correct Movement: Maintain a neutral shoulder position and focus on driving the shoulder *over* the opponent's hip, not across their body.
    • Knee and Hip Strain: When stepping the foot outside the hip, if the opponent does not shift weight, a white belt might forcefully try to drive past, twisting their own knee or hip joint as the opponent's base remains solid.
    • Correct Movement: Wait for the opponent's weight shift initiated by the hip bump before driving the shoulder over; if no shift occurs, reset.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo Movement (0% resistance): Practice the hip bump and shoulder drive motion without a partner, focusing on hip elevation and weight transfer. 10 reps.
    2. Partnered Drills (25% resistance): Partner on hands and knees. You practice the hip bump and shoulder drive, partner makes minimal weight shift. Focus on feel. 5 reps each side.
    3. Controlled Transition (50% resistance): Partner on hands and knees. You execute the full sequence, partner resists lightly by maintaining base but allows the climb. Focus on grip and hook establishment. 5 reps.
    4. Back Take Entry (75% resistance): Start from a common guard pass scenario where opponent is on knees. You initiate the back take attempt, partner defends passively. Focus on securing hooks and chest-to-back. 3 reps.
    5. Live Rolling (90% resistance): During rolling, actively look for opportunities to initiate the back take from the specified position. Partner provides active, but not expert, defense. Focus on completing the take. 2 rounds.
    6. Full Live Rolling (100% resistance): Integrate the back take attempt naturally within the flow of live rolling against a resisting opponent. Focus on timing and execution. 3 rounds.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT
    • When opponent attempts to stand up from your guard, creating a brief moment of instability on their knees.
    • After a failed guard pass where the opponent ends up on their hands and knees.
    • When opponent attempts to shrimp away from your pressure, exposing their back momentarily.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS
    • Turtle and Drive: If the opponent turtles and drives forward, immediately bring your knees to your chest, creating a tight frame and preventing them from driving into you. Maintain collar grips to control their head.
    • Frame and Escape: If the opponent fails to secure hooks and maintains a loose connection, they can frame with their forearm against your hip and shrimp away, creating space to escape.
    • Counter-Climb: If the opponent attempts to climb, but their base is unstable, you can attempt to drive your hips into their chest and push them off, then transition to a guard pass.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to take the back from the turtle position?

    This often happens when you grip the collar too high and try to pull the opponent's head down while simultaneously driving your body forward. This can cause shoulder impingement as your humerus jams against your shoulder joint. Instead, focus on driving your shoulder *over* the opponent's hip and maintaining a neutral spine, using your hips to create the leverage for the climb.

    Q: How can I take the back of a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Focus on the hip bump to shift their weight *before* attempting the shoulder drive and climb. Ensure your hooks are deeply embedded behind their thighs to control their base. Do not rely on strength; use your hips and core to create the upward momentum.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a back take from the knees-on-belly position?

    The knees-on-belly position is typically a transitional position for advancing to mount. Attempting a back take directly from here is less common and biomechanically challenging. It's best to transition to a position where the opponent is more likely to be on their hands and knees, such as after a failed sweep or guard pass, to set up the back take.
