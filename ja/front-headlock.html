The Front Headlock is a dominant positional control, primarily used to stifle an opponent's offense and set up submissions like chokes or transitions to better control positions.

White belts often struggle by treating it as a wrestling pin instead of a dynamic BJJ position, leading to poor posture and energy expenditure.

The key is to use your body weight to create spinal flexion in the opponent, limiting their movement and creating opportunities for submission or positional advancement.

目次

    グリップ・生体力学

    1. From a neutral or scrambling situation, secure a grip around the opponent's head and neck with one arm (e.g., bicep on the neck, hand gripping the opponent's far shoulder or tricep).
    2. Place your other hand on the opponent's hip or lower back to establish a strong frame and prevent them from posturing up.
    3. Step your lead leg inside the opponent's hips, ensuring your knee is pointing towards their hip joint, not their stomach.
    4. Drive your chest into the opponent's head and neck, creating spinal flexion and forcing their chin towards their chest.
    5. Shift your weight forward and down, aiming to drive your hips towards your own heels, not straight down.
    6. Maintain a wide base with your feet, approximately shoulder-width apart, to ensure stability.
    7. Keep your back straight and your core engaged, avoiding rounding your own spine.

    ⚠️ 白帯の注意点

    • Incorrectly driving your head into the opponent's head: This can cause neck injuries for both parties, specifically hyperextension or hyperflexion of the cervical spine, potentially damaging ligaments and discs. Always aim to control their head with your arm and chest, not skull-to-skull contact.
    • Letting the opponent's hips create space by not framing properly: If you don't control their hips, they can bridge or shrimp, leading to a loss of position and potential knee or ankle strain for you as you try to recover. Maintain a solid frame on their hip with your free hand.
    • Overextending your own neck to 'look' for submissions: This exposes your own neck to attacks and can lead to cervical strain. Keep your chin tucked and focus on maintaining positional control first.

    ドリル段階

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the footwork, hip angle, and weight transfer from a standing position into a simulated Front Headlock with no resistance. 10 reps.
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner stands, you practice entering the Front Headlock position and establishing the grip and frame. 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance 25%): Your partner offers minimal resistance to posture or movement as you secure the Front Headlock. Focus on maintaining control. 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drilling (moderate resistance 50%): Your partner actively tries to posture up or escape as you work to maintain the Front Headlock and hip control. 5 reps per side.
    5. Flow drilling (75%): Move through a sequence of securing the Front Headlock, maintaining it, and transitioning to a basic submission attempt (e.g., a guillotine setup) while your partner defends. 3 rounds, 2 minutes each.
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Integrate the Front Headlock into sparring. Focus on recognizing opportunities to enter the position and maintain control, understanding when to disengage if control is lost.

    使うタイミング・カウンター

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent attempts a takedown and you can sprawl effectively, transitioning to a Front Headlock as they try to recover.
    • When an opponent attempts to pass your guard and you can secure their head and neck as they drive forward.
    • During scrambles where both parties are on their hands and knees, creating an opportunity to establish head and neck control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip escape and frame: The opponent bridges their hips away and uses their free arm to frame against your shoulder or bicep, creating space to recover guard or posture up.
    • "Whizzer" or Overhook: The opponent secures an overhook on your arm that is controlling their head, and uses it to drive their shoulder into your armpit, creating a strong base to turn into you or escape.
    • Standing up and driving forward: If the Front Headlock is not fully secured, the opponent can stand up and drive forward, attempting to break your grip and use their weight to pass.

    よくある質問・トラブル

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to get a Front Headlock?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are craning your own neck to look for the submission or are not tucking your chin. The Front Headlock should be controlled by driving your chest into their head and using your arm to secure their neck, not by straining your cervical spine. Ensure your own head is in a neutral, safe position and focus on spinal flexion of your opponent.

    Q: How do I maintain a Front Headlock against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and weight distribution are paramount. Instead of trying to lift them, focus on driving your weight *through* them and into the mat. Use your hips to create a strong base, and keep your chest glued to their head to prevent them from posturing up. A strong frame on their hip is crucial to stop them from generating power through bridging.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Front Headlock to a submission?

    Transition from Front Headlock when you feel your opponent's posture is broken and they are struggling to defend. Look for opportunities when they try to drive forward or turn into you, as this can create openings for a guillotine choke or an arm-triangle choke. If they are completely flattened and unable to move, you have a stable platform to set up a darce or anaconda choke.
