The Stack Pass: A Biomechanically Precise BJJ Guide for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Stack Pass initiates from a position where your opponent is attempting to establish Closed Guard or has achieved a strong guard retention. It aims to break their leg structure and establish side control or knee-on-belly.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or poor body mechanics, leading to ineffective passes and potential injury. They may not understand the critical angles of pressure needed.

The single most important mechanical insight is to utilize your body weight and hip pressure to compress their legs, creating an opening for your knee to penetrate and secure the pass.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From your opponent's closed guard, secure two strong grips on the outside of their thighs, just above the knees, using your palms and fingers. Ensure your knuckles are pointed upwards.
    2. Internally rotate your shoulders and pull their knees slightly wider apart, creating a small gap.
    3. Lower your hips by bending your knees, bringing your torso closer to their legs. Your back should be relatively flat, not rounded.
    4. Shift your weight forward, driving your chest into their hips. Your base should be wide, with your feet shoulder-width apart.
    5. Begin to drive your right knee (or left, depending on your dominant side) into the space between their legs, aiming to slide it along the mat towards their hip.
    6. As your knee penetrates, simultaneously drive your left foot forward, establishing a solid base. Your hips should be lower than your opponent's.
    7. Use your thigh grips to continue to compress their legs inwards as your knee slides through. Your goal is to break their structural connection.
    8. Once your knee is past their hip line, transition your grips from the thighs to securing their hips or pants, and drive your chest into their side to establish side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to rip the legs apart with straight arms and rounded back: This can cause severe strain to your shoulder joints and lumbar spine due to hyper-extension and excessive shear forces.
    • Driving forward with your head instead of your hips: This leads to whiplash-like neck injuries and a loss of structural integrity, as your head can be easily controlled or used as a lever.
    • Allowing your knees to splay outwards while driving forward: This compromises your base and can lead to knee valgus collapse, risking ACL or meniscus tears.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip acquisition and hip lowering motion without a partner. Focus on the pelvic tilt and chest drive. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from guard to side control. Focus on the correct footwork and weight transfer. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Partner provides minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and hip movement. Focus on penetrating with the knee. (15 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance - 50%): Partner actively tries to maintain guard, but allows you to progress through the steps. Focus on breaking their structure. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Partner actively defends the pass, simulating a real scenario but allowing for instruction. Focus on reacting to their defense. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Apply the Stack Pass in sparring. Focus on understanding when to attempt it and reacting to common defenses. (3-5 attempts per roll)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent has established a tight closed guard and is attempting to break your posture.
    • When opponent is using a butterfly guard and you can control their hips and feet.
    • When opponent is attempting to shrimp or create space from a half guard situation.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): As the passer drives forward, the defender shrimpes their hips away to create space and re-establish guard or transition to a single leg.
    • Leg Pummeling: The defender actively works to pummel their legs between the passer's legs to prevent knee penetration and maintain guard.
    • Underhook/Overhook Control: The defender secures an underhook or overhook to prevent the passer from flattening them out and uses it to create angles for sweeps or escapes.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Stack Pass?

    Your knee pain likely stems from your knee being forced into an unnatural angle as you drive forward. This happens when you fail to create sufficient space between your opponent's legs before penetrating, or when your weight isn't properly distributed. Ensure you are widening their knees slightly with your grips and driving your hips *forward and down*, not just forward. This allows your knee to slide along the mat rather than being jammed.

    Q: How can I Stack Pass against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision become even more critical. Focus on using your grips to subtly widen their legs and create a small gap. Instead of a direct forward drive, think of driving your hips *under* their hips and then using your leg as a wedge. Your weight distribution should be such that you are compressing their legs inwards, making it difficult for them to maintain their structure. Aim to get your knee *past* their hip line before they can effectively use their size to push you away.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Stack Pass in BJJ?

    The optimal time to attempt a Stack Pass is when your opponent has committed to establishing a strong, closed guard and your posture is somewhat compromised. This often occurs when they are actively trying to break your posture down or set up sweeps. Another prime opportunity is when they are using a less rigid guard, like an open guard where their legs are extended, allowing you to more easily penetrate their structure. Avoid attempting it when they have a very dominant grip on your arms or collar, as this limits your ability to create the necessary angles.
