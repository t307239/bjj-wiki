The Stack Pass is a guard pass initiated from open guard or Half Guard, aiming to collapse your opponent's legs and achieve side control.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and framing, leading to ineffective pressure and potential injury.

The core principle is driving your hips forward and down, using your chest and forehead to create a "stacking" pressure that breaks their base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a closed guard broken position, secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a left sleeve grip with your left hand.
    2. Step your right foot outside your opponent's left hip, placing your knee on the mat.
    3. Drive your left knee forward, aiming to place it inside your opponent's right thigh (knee-on-ground).
    4. Initiate a forward hip drive, pushing your pelvis towards their hips, while simultaneously shrugging your right shoulder forward.
    5. Apply downward pressure with your chest and forehead onto their upper thigh/hip area, creating a "stacking" force.
    6. Shift your weight onto your right knee and left foot, creating a base.
    7. As their base collapses, your left hand transitions to a grip on their left hip or pants, and your right arm secures a far-side underhook or wrist control.
    8. Drive your right shoulder into their chest, clearing their legs and moving to side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect forward lean: Leaning forward from the waist without hip drive can hyperextend the lumbar spine, risking a disc injury. The correct movement is a forward hip thrust combined with a chest-down pressure.
    • Knee-on-throat pressure: Driving your knee directly onto the opponent's throat or Adam's apple can cause airway obstruction and injury. The pressure should be on their upper thigh or hip bone.
    • Head placement: Slamming your head into the opponent's face or neck can cause concussions for both parties. Your head should be used as a stable point of contact, driving pressure down through your shoulders and hips, not as a blunt instrument.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip drive and weight transfer motion without a partner, focusing on hip angle and chest-down posture (50 reps).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, execute the grip-taking, foot placement, and hip drive sequence, focusing on perfect form (25 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and weight transfer, focusing on breaking their base (50 reps).
    4. Dynamic drilling: Partner actively tries to recover guard, allowing you to practice maintaining pressure and adjusting your base (50 reps).
    5. Flow rolling (50% resistance): Roll with a partner, attempting the Stack Pass when the opportunity arises, focusing on timing and pressure application.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Execute the Stack Pass during live rolling, focusing on completing the pass and securing side control.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent's guard is broken or has large openings.
    • When opponent is trying to shrimp away, creating space.
    • When opponent attempts a sweep and overextends their base.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Frame: If the stacker's pressure is insufficient, the defender can shrimp their hips away and create a frame with their forearm between their hips and the stacker's chest, preventing further compression.
    • Knee Shield and Re-guard: The defender can bring their knee up between their body and the stacker, creating a "knee shield" to re-establish guard and prevent side control.
    • Leg Pummeling and Sweep: The defender can actively pummel their legs, specifically their far-side leg, to create space and potentially transition to a sweep or another guard position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Stack Pass?

    This usually happens because you're bending at the waist instead of driving with your hips. Your lower back is not designed for this kind of direct forward flexion under load. Focus on initiating the movement from your glutes and pelvis, driving your hips forward and down like a powerful hinge. Ensure your core is engaged to protect your spine.

    Q: How do I Stack Pass against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise weight distribution are even more critical. Focus on getting your knee inside their thigh to break their posture and create a strong base. Use your forehead and shoulder to drive *down* into their hip, not just forward. Imagine you are trying to pin their hip to the mat, rather than just pushing them over.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Stack Pass instead of a Knee Slice?

    The Stack Pass is most effective when your opponent's legs are relatively close together and they are trying to maintain a tight guard or are in the process of shrimping. If their legs are spread wide or they are actively trying to create space with their knees, a Knee Slice Pass might be more appropriate as it excels at cutting through wider stances.
