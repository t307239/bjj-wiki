Stack Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Stack Pass is initiated from a seated or kneeling guard position, aiming to break the opponent's guard and establish Side Control.

White belts often struggle by focusing on brute force over proper weight distribution and leverage, leading to ineffective passes and potential injury.

The core principle is to create a strong, stable base and drive your weight forward to compress the opponent's hips, forcing them to open their guard.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure both of your opponent's knees with your hands, fingers interlaced or palms flat against the outside of their knees.
    2. Base: Kneel on one knee, with the other knee on the mat, forming a stable tripod base. Your hips should be lower than your opponent's hips.
    3. Weight Transfer: Begin to shift your weight forward, driving your chest towards your opponent's thighs.
    4. Compression: Simultaneously, drive your knees inward and upward, compressing their thighs against their torso.
    5. Head Position: Keep your head up and drive it into their hip or thigh to help maintain forward pressure.
    6. Step-Up: As their guard opens, step your non-kneeling leg forward to establish a base for passing.
    7. Pass: Drive your hips forward and lower your chest to the mat, moving your hips past their legs to secure side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to pry open the legs with straight arms: This can lead to elbow hyperextension or ligament tears in the elbow joint. The correct alternative is to use your knees and hip drive to compress, not just pull.
    • Driving forward without a stable base: This can cause you to fall forward onto your face or into the opponent's guard, risking neck or shoulder injury. Maintain a wide, stable kneeling base.
    • Arching your back excessively to create pressure: This puts undue stress on the lumbar spine and can lead to back strains. Keep your core engaged and drive with your hips and chest.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Compression Drill (10 reps per side): From a kneeling position, practice driving your knees inward and upward, simulating the compression motion without a partner (0% resistance).
    2. Partnered Knee Compression (10 reps per side): With a partner, practice the knee compression on their knees, focusing on creating pressure without moving your base (25% resistance).
    3. Base and Drive Drill (10 reps): From your kneeling base, practice driving your weight forward and stepping your leg through, focusing on hip movement and base stability (50% resistance).
    4. Controlled Stack Pass (5 reps per side): Execute the full Stack Pass against a passive partner, focusing on grip, base, and weight transfer (75% resistance).
    5. Light Resistance Stack Pass (5 reps per side): Perform the Stack Pass against a partner who offers light resistance by trying to maintain their guard (90% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling (1 minute rounds): Integrate the Stack Pass into live rolling situations, attempting it when the opportunity arises (100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's knees are close together, making it difficult for them to maintain guard.
    • When you have established a strong base and can control their hips.
    • When the opponent attempts to close guard after a failed pass attempt.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention: The opponent can maintain a tight guard by actively pressing their knees inward and keeping their heels close to their hips. This prevents the compression.
    • Shrimping: If the opponent feels the pressure, they can shrimp their hips away to create space and reset their guard. You must anticipate this and adjust your base.
    • Knee Shield/Push Away: The opponent can use their knees to push your chest away, creating distance and disrupting your forward pressure. Maintain your frame and drive through their knees.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to Stack Pass against a smaller opponent?

    Your back is likely hurting because you are arching your lumbar spine to create pressure, rather than driving with your hips and core. Engage your core muscles to maintain a neutral spine and focus on driving your chest and hips forward. This biomechanical shift utilizes your larger muscle groups for power and protects your back.

    Q: How can I effectively Stack Pass against a much larger and stronger opponent who has a tight closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, raw strength won't work. Focus on precise leverage and timing. Instead of trying to force their knees apart, aim to compress their hips firmly against their own torso. This biomechanical principle of leverage, combined with a stable base and forward drive, can make them uncomfortable and force an opening, even with their size advantage.

    Q: When should I stop trying to Stack Pass and switch to a different guard pass?

    You should consider switching passes if the opponent consistently defends by creating a strong frame with their forearms, pushing your chest away, or shrimping effectively to maintain distance. If you cannot achieve hip compression and forward drive due to their active defense, persistency with the Stack Pass can lead to you being swept or ending up in a compromised position. Look for opportunities to transition to a knee-slice or leg-drag pass.
