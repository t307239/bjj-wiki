Spider Guard is an open guard position where you control your opponent's sleeves with your feet, creating distance and offensive opportunities.

White belts often struggle by collapsing their base or allowing their opponent to close the distance without establishing frames.

The core principle is using your feet as levers to break posture and create angles for sweeps and submissions, while maintaining a strong connection.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or supine position, establish control over both of your opponent's sleeves with your hands, using a standard two-on-one grip (one hand controlling the wrist, the other the forearm).
    2. Extend one leg, placing the sole of your foot directly onto the opponent's bicep, approximately 2-3 inches above the elbow crease. Your knee should be slightly bent, creating a spring-like tension.
    3. Simultaneously, extend your other leg and place the sole of your foot onto the opponent's opposite bicep, again 2-3 inches above the elbow. Maintain a similar knee bend.
    4. Your hips should be angled slightly away from the opponent's dominant side, creating a lateral frame. Your base should be wide enough to prevent easy penetration.
    5. To break posture, actively push the opponent's arm that corresponds to your foot on their bicep, while simultaneously pulling their opposite sleeve inwards towards your body.
    6. Rotate your hips to create an angle. As you break their posture, your foot on the bicep of their extended arm will drive them forward, while the foot on their other bicep will help control their hip.
    7. Maintain constant tension on the sleeves and a slight pressure through your feet on their biceps to prevent them from closing the distance or establishing a strong base.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing feet too high on the opponent's shoulders or neck: This risks hyperextension or compression of the cervical spine, leading to neck injuries. Always aim for the bicep area.
    • Bending knees too deeply or collapsing ankles: This can lead to ankle sprains or ligament tears due to excessive internal/external rotation under pressure. Keep ankles dorsiflexed and knees slightly bent, not hyper-flexed.
    • Allowing opponent to step inside your leg frames: This leads to your opponent achieving a dominant passing position, often resulting in knee injuries from awkward impacts or leg entanglement. Maintain frame integrity by actively pushing and pulling.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice extending legs, placing feet on a padded surface (like a yoga block), and mimicking the push-pull on imaginary sleeves for 5 minutes, 0% resistance.
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Opponent stands, you are in Spider Guard. Practice establishing sleeve grips and bicep control, then breaking posture with the push-pull motion. 20 repetitions per side, 25% resistance.
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Opponent attempts to close the distance. You practice maintaining frames and breaking posture. 10 repetitions, 50% resistance.
    4. Spider Guard to Sweep Drills: Practice a specific sweep from Spider Guard (e.g., Scissor Sweep). Opponent provides minimal resistance to the sweep. 10 repetitions, 60% resistance.
    5. Transition Drills: Practice transitioning from Spider Guard to another guard (e.g., Closed Guard) when the opponent successfully closes the distance. 15 repetitions, 75% resistance.
    6. Live Rolling (positional sparring): Start from Spider Guard. Opponent attempts to pass. You attempt to maintain guard and sweep. 3 rounds of 3 minutes, 90% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up and you have established sleeve control.
    • When your opponent is attempting to establish a knee-slide or pressure pass, creating space to insert your feet.
    • When you are on your back and need to create distance to prevent them from smashing your guard or establishing side control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Drag Pass: The opponent steps their leg outside your Spider Guard frame, then drives their hip forward to drag your leg across their body. To defend, maintain frame integrity by pushing their hip away and simultaneously pulling their sleeve to disrupt their base.
    • Knee Slice Pass: The opponent drives their knee across your body, aiming to cut through your guard. To defend, actively push their knee away with your foot on their bicep and pull their sleeve to create an angle for a sweep or to reset to another guard.
    • Torreando Pass: The opponent attempts to circle around your legs. To defend, maintain tension on the sleeves and use your feet on their biceps to push them away and pivot your hips to follow their movement, keeping them at bay.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I do Spider Guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping too tightly on the opponent's sleeve and not using your forearm to control their arm. Instead of just gripping the fabric, try to get your forearm underneath their elbow or upper arm to create a stronger frame. Also, ensure your wrists are not bent backwards; keep them neutral or slightly flexed for better leverage and less strain.

    Q: How do I use Spider Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary goal is to use your feet as long levers to prevent them from closing the distance and using their weight. Focus on maintaining a strong frame on their biceps, constantly breaking their posture by pushing one arm away while pulling the other sleeve towards you. This disrupts their base and makes it harder for them to drive forward effectively.

    Q: Spider Guard is most effective when you can maintain distance and control posture. If your opponent successfully closes the distance and smashes your frames, it's often beneficial to transition. Look to transition when they've passed your leg frames but haven't yet secured a dominant passing position, allowing you to potentially enter Closed Guard or Half Guard.
