Spider Guard is an open guard position where you control your opponent's arms with your legs, typically by gripping their sleeves.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise limb control and an overreliance on generalized pushing or pulling.

The core principle is creating a stable, extended framework with your legs to control distance and off-balance your opponent's base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Sit up, place your feet on your opponent's hips, toes pointed towards their shoulders. Maintain a neutral pelvis angle.
    2. Secure a sleeve grip with both hands, ensuring your thumbs are inside the collar or your pinky finger is at the cuff's end.
    3. Extend one leg, keeping it straight and pressing into the opponent's hip with the sole of your foot.
    4. Simultaneously, bend the opposite knee, bringing your foot higher on the opponent's hip, creating a 'spider' leg configuration.
    5. Shift your weight slightly onto your hips, maintaining a broad base with your glutes on the mat.
    6. Use your extended leg to create a frame, pushing their hip away to control distance.
    7. Use your bent leg to 'pull' their arm across your body or to set up sweeps by altering the angle of their hips.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too low on the sleeve (e.g., mid-forearm): This allows the opponent to easily pull their arm out and break your grip, leading to a loss of control and potential for them to pass.
    • Incorrect alternative: Grip the sleeve firmly at the wrist cuff, ensuring your knuckles are aligned with the cuff's edge.
    • Extending legs too wide or at an unstable angle: This can strain your adductor muscles (groin) and lower back, especially if the opponent drives forward forcefully.
    • Incorrect alternative: Maintain a controlled leg extension, keeping your knees slightly bent to absorb pressure and your feet positioned directly on the hips.
    • Bending your elbows and collapsing your frames when the opponent pushes: This transfers their weight directly into your body, risking shoulder or elbow hyperextension.
    • Incorrect alternative: Keep your arms straight and actively push back against their hips, using your legs as the primary control point.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice entering Spider Guard from a seated position, focusing on smooth leg extension and grip acquisition. 10 reps each side.
    2. Partner drill - static grips (25% resistance): Opponent stands passively. Focus on establishing sleeve grips and leg frames, maintaining control. 5 reps each side.
    3. Partner drill - controlled movement (50% resistance): Opponent gently pushes and pulls. Practice maintaining frames and adjusting leg position. 5 reps each side.
    4. Partner drill - basic sweeps (75% resistance): Introduce one simple sweep (e.g., scissor sweep) from Spider Guard. Opponent offers light resistance. 3 reps each side.
    5. Live rolling - positional sparring (90% resistance): Start in Spider Guard. Focus on maintaining the guard and attempting sweeps or transitions. 2 minute rounds.
    6. Live rolling - full rolling (100% resistance): Integrate Spider Guard into your rolling. Prioritize control and safety over submission success. 5 minute rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up, creating space between your hips and theirs.
    • When your opponent is reaching for your collar or upper body, exposing their sleeves.
    • When you want to control the distance and prevent your opponent from establishing a strong base to pass.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Knee Slice Pass: Opponent drives forward, placing their knee inside your leg frame. They will then attempt to slice their knee across your body to pass.
    • Toreando Pass: Opponent circles their hips away, pulling both sleeves free and stepping around your legs to pass to the opposite side.
    • Stack Pass: Opponent drives forward with significant weight, attempting to 'stack' you by bringing your knees towards your head.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I do Spider Guard?

    Wrist pain in Spider Guard often stems from gripping the sleeve too tightly with a flexed wrist, especially when the opponent pulls. This places excessive strain on the carpal bones and ligaments. To fix this, ensure your grip is firm but relaxed, with your wrist in a neutral or slightly extended position, and focus on using your forearm and elbow to control the sleeve rather than just your hand.

    Q: How do I use Spider Guard against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, Spider Guard relies heavily on superior leverage and frame control. Focus on keeping your legs extended to create distance, preventing them from collapsing your guard. Use precise foot placement on their hips to off-balance them, and grip their sleeves at the wrist to break their posture and control their arms. Your goal is to make them feel unstable and unable to generate their natural weight advantage.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Spider Guard to another guard or submission?

    The ideal transition from Spider Guard occurs when you've successfully off-balanced your opponent or created an opening. For example, if you pull their arm across your body and their posture breaks significantly, it's a prime moment to attack a Triangle Choke. If you successfully sweep them and they try to recover guard quickly, you can transition to mount or side control. Always look for the opponent's reaction to your control to dictate the next move.
