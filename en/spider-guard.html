Spider Guard: A White Belt's Guide to BJJ Control

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Spider Guard is an open guard position where you control your opponent's sleeves with your feet, creating distance and attacking angles.

White belts often struggle by collapsing their frames or allowing the opponent to close the distance, losing the guard's primary function.

The core principle is maintaining frames with your legs to prevent the opponent from establishing a strong base and attacking your hips.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or shrimped position, establish two sleeve grips: your right hand controls their left sleeve at the wrist, and your left hand controls their right sleeve at the wrist.
    2. Place the soles of your feet on the inside of their biceps, with your knees bent to 90 degrees and your hips slightly elevated off the mat.
    3. Maintain tension on the sleeve grips, pulling their arms slightly forward and towards your midline.
    4. Simultaneously, extend one leg (e.g., your right leg) to create a frame against their corresponding bicep, keeping your ankle flexed.
    5. Your left leg will remain in a controlling position, ready to switch frames or apply pressure.
    6. Your hips should be angled at approximately 45 degrees away from the opponent, creating space for leg movement.
    7. Ensure your back is relatively flat on the mat, avoiding excessive arching, and keep your core engaged to maintain posture.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing feet on the opponent's shoulders: This can lead to their head driving into your face and neck, risking cervical hyperextension or whiplash injuries.
    • Correct: Always place feet on the biceps, maintaining a strong frame.
    • Allowing the opponent to step inside your leg frames: This removes your distance and control, often leading to a pass or the opponent crushing your guard, risking knee or hip impingement.
    • Correct: Actively extend your legs to maintain the bicep frame, pushing their weight away.
    • Gripping too low on the sleeve (forearm/elbow): This provides insufficient leverage and control, allowing the opponent to easily break grips and advance, risking wrist sprains from uncontrolled pulling.
    • Correct: Grip firmly at the opponent's wrist.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice shrimping and establishing sleeve grips, then extending one leg to the bicep while holding the grip. Repeat 10 times per side. (0% resistance)
    2. Solo drill: Practice shrimping, establishing sleeve grips, and transitioning the foot from one bicep to the other while maintaining sleeve control. Repeat 10 times per side. (0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (no resistance): Opponent stands passively. You practice entering Spider Guard, establishing sleeve grips, and creating frames. Focus on hip angle and leg extension. 5 reps per side. (25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (light resistance): Opponent attempts to close the distance. You practice maintaining frames and preventing them from passing. Focus on leg extension and hip movement. 5 reps per side. (50% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring: Start from Spider Guard. Opponent attempts to pass. You attempt to maintain guard and create opportunities for sweeps. 3 rounds, 2 minutes each. (75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Incorporate Spider Guard attempts when the opportunity arises. Focus on maintaining the position and understanding when to transition to sweeps or submissions. 1 round, 5 minutes. (100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is posturing up and maintaining distance, creating an opening for leg control.
    • When the opponent attempts a standing guard pass, allowing you to intercept their arms.
    • When you are in your open guard and wish to transition to a more offensive control position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Toreando Pass: If the opponent attempts to circle out, extend your leg furthest from them to create a frame and use your sleeve grips to pull them back into your guard.
    • Knee Slice Pass: If the opponent drives forward and tries to smash your guard, extend both legs to create space and simultaneously pull one sleeve to disrupt their base.
    • Stack Pass: If the opponent tries to stack you, hip escape away and use your legs to push their hips away, maintaining the bicep frames.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to Spider Guard?

    Wrist pain in Spider Guard often stems from gripping too high on the sleeve or not maintaining a neutral wrist angle. Ensure you grip firmly at the opponent's wrist. Keep your wrist straight and avoid excessive flexion or extension. Practice gripping drills with a partner to build forearm and wrist strength and endurance.

    Q: How do I Spider Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your frames are paraMount. Focus on maintaining strong bicep control with your feet, actively pushing their weight away. Use your hips to create space and angle yourself. Don't let them settle their weight directly on you; constantly use your legs to manage distance and disrupt their base.

    Q: When should I stop trying to Spider Guard and switch positions?

    You should consider switching if the opponent consistently closes the distance and smashes your frames, or if they have established a strong grip on your pants or hips that negates your leg control. If they are able to consistently get their knee inside your leg frame, it's often a sign to transition to a different guard or escape.
