Spider Guard: A Precise BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Spider Guard is an open guard position where you control your opponent's arms with your legs, typically by hooking their biceps or sleeves.

White belts often struggle by overextending their hips or not establishing a strong, stable base, leading to passes and injuries.

The core principle is creating a dynamic framework with your legs to control distance and off-balance, allowing for sweeps and submissions.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or shrimping position, place the soles of your feet on your opponent's biceps, directly above the elbow joint. Your knees should be bent at approximately 90 degrees, forming a stable frame.
    2. Grip your opponent's sleeves with your hands, thumbs inside the cuff, fingers gripping the fabric. Your elbows should be tucked close to your body, creating a strong structural connection.
    3. Maintain a slight posterior pelvic tilt (tucking your tailbone) to keep your hips close to your opponent and prevent them from easily stacking you.
    4. Your weight should be distributed evenly between your hips on the mat and the pressure of your feet on their biceps. Avoid leaning too far forward or backward.
    5. To initiate a sweep or attack, extend one leg while simultaneously retracting the other, creating a pulling and pushing motion.
    6. Use your hands to pull their sleeve towards your chest as you extend the opposite leg, disrupting their base and balance.
    7. Maintain constant tension and control on both sleeves and biceps to prevent them from posturing up or passing your guard.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your legs too straight and stiffly can hyperextend your knee joint, leading to ACL or MCL tears. Always maintain a slight bend in your knees.
    • Grabbing the opponent's gi too loosely on the sleeves, without securing thumbs inside the cuff, can lead to the grip slipping, causing you to lose control and potentially exposing your arm for a pass.
    • Trying to 'pull' the opponent down with excessive upper body force instead of using your legs to create leverage can strain your lower back and shoulders, and offers poor control.
    • Allowing your hips to sag or your back to round excessively creates a space for the opponent to 'stack' you, compressing your spine and potentially causing disc injury.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg placement and grip on a dummy or mat. Focus on the hip angle and knee bend. (50 reps)
    2. Mirror drilling: With a partner, practice entering Spider Guard and establishing grips without resistance. Focus on maintaining frames. (25 reps each side)
    3. Light resistance entry: Partner offers minimal resistance to your entry. Focus on securing grips and controlling biceps/sleeves. (20 reps each side, 25% resistance)
    4. Guard retention drill: Partner attempts to pass, you focus on maintaining Spider Guard by adjusting frames and leg tension. (30 seconds rounds, 50% resistance)
    5. Sweep attempt drill: From Spider Guard, attempt a basic sweep (e.g., pendulum sweep) with partner offering light resistance. (15 reps each side, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Apply Spider Guard from your back, focusing on maintaining the position and attempting sweeps/submissions. (2-minute rounds, 75-90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is postured up and trying to apply pressure from their knees or standing.
    • When you have established a strong connection and can control both of their sleeves.
    • When your opponent is trying to pass your guard and you need to create distance and control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stacking Pass: Opponent drives forward, attempting to 'stack' your hips. Counter by widening your leg base and pushing their hips away with your feet while framing with your hands on their shoulders.
    • Knee Slice Pass: Opponent attempts to bring their knee across your body. Counter by using your Spider Guard legs to push their knee away and framing their upper body with your arms to create space.
    • Toreando Pass: Opponent attempts to circle their legs around yours. Counter by using your Spider Guard legs to hook their hips or legs and pull them back into your guard, or by transitioning to a different guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I do Spider Guard?

    Wrist pain in Spider Guard often stems from gripping the opponent's gi sleeves too tightly with your fingers extended straight, creating excessive strain. Ensure you are gripping with your thumbs inside the cuff and your fingers curled naturally around the fabric, maintaining a neutral wrist position. Also, avoid pulling directly down; use your arm to guide and create angles rather than just brute force.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paramount in Spider Guard. Focus on using your legs to create sharp angles and off-balancing movements rather than relying on pure strength. Keep your feet glued to their biceps and actively push and pull to disrupt their base. Prioritize controlling their posture and preventing them from establishing their weight on you.

    Q: You should consider switching guards when your opponent effectively neutralizes your Spider Guard grips or starts to pass with a strong posture. If they are too heavy and static, it's hard to generate sweep opportunities. If they are too mobile and you can't maintain grips, it's also a sign to transition. Look for openings to move to a guard that better suits their reaction.
