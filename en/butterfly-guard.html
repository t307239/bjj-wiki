The Butterfly Guard is a dynamic guard position where you sit up, hook your opponent's legs with your shins, and use your hips to create off-balancing opportunities.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength or incorrect body positioning, leading to immediate Guard Passes or injury.

The key is understanding how to use your hooks to elevate and off-balance your opponent, creating space for sweeps and submissions.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from your back, bring your knees towards your chest, feet slightly wider than hip-width.
    2. Place your shins across the front of your opponent's thighs, just below the knees, creating your "hooks".
    3. Grip your opponent's left arm with your right hand, securing a "collar grip" at the lapel, and your opponent's right arm with your left hand on the "sleeve grip" at the wrist.
    4. Sit up by driving your hips off the mat, creating an angle where your torso is not directly facing your opponent's torso.
    5. Keep your back straight and chest up, maintaining a strong posture to prevent your opponent from stacking you.
    6. Apply pressure by gently pulling the collar grip towards your chest and pushing the sleeve grip away, creating a pulling and pushing force.
    7. Shift your weight to your hips, rocking your opponent slightly forward onto the balls of their feet, preparing for a sweep.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly placing hooks too high on the thighs (above the knee): This can hyperextend the opponent's knees if they fall, damaging their ACL or PCL, and allows them to easily step over your guard.
    • Trying to lift the opponent solely with your arms: This strains your shoulders and biceps, leading to potential rotator cuff tears or bicep strains, and is inefficient for generating sweep power.
    • Allowing your back to round while sitting up: This compresses your lumbar spine, risking disc injuries and making you vulnerable to stack passes, whereas maintaining a straight back protects your spine.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice sitting up and establishing the hooks with no opponent. Focus on hip elevation and spinal posture. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Opponent stands passively. Practice establishing hooks, sitting up, and creating the angle. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Opponent offers minimal resistance to hook placement. Focus on grip acquisition and hip drive. (25% resistance, 15 reps per side)
    4. Sweep progression: From Butterfly Guard, attempt a basic sweep (e.g., pendulum sweep) against a compliant partner. (50% resistance, 10 reps per side)
    5. Guard retention drill: Opponent attempts to pass. Focus on maintaining hooks and hip angle to prevent the pass. (75% resistance, 5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling: Implement Butterfly Guard during sparring, prioritizing sweep attempts over submission for this phase. (90% resistance, 3 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is trying to pass your guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • When you are on your back and have an opportunity to establish hooks before they secure a dominant position.
    • When your opponent is postured up, creating a space to insert your shins.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass: If your opponent aggressively drives their weight forward and down, immediately tuck your chin to your chest and drive your hips backward, creating space and potentially transitioning to a single-leg takedown or escape.
    • Knee Slice Pass: If your opponent attempts to drive a knee across your body, use your hooks to elevate their hips and shrimp your hips away, creating distance and re-establishing your guard.
    • Underhook Control: If your opponent secures an underhook, use your free arm to control their opposite collar or sleeve, and use your legs to maintain distance and off-balance them to prevent them from collapsing your guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to get into Butterfly Guard?

    This often happens when you're trying to sit up by rounding your spine instead of hinging at your hips. To fix this, focus on keeping your back straight and driving your hips off the mat. Imagine you're trying to lift your pelvis towards the ceiling, using your glutes and core, rather than just bending your upper back.

    Q: How can I effectively use Butterfly Guard against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Focus on getting your hooks deep and tight around their thighs, then use your hips to create off-balancing angles. Elevate their weight by driving your hips up and away, making them feel lighter and more unstable, which allows you to initiate sweeps with less physical strength.

    Q: When should I transition from Butterfly Guard to another position, and how do I do it safely?

    You should transition when your opponent successfully defends your sweep attempt and establishes a strong counter-pass or when you see an opening for a submission like an Armbar or triangle. To transition safely, always maintain your hooks or re-establish them if lost, and use your hip movement to create space before committing to the new position.
