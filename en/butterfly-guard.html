Butterfly Guard: A White Belt's Guide to BJJ

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Butterfly Guard is a dynamic open guard position initiated from the bottom, aiming to off-balance and sweep an opponent. It's a fundamental position for controlling distance and creating attacking angles.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of core engagement and over-reliance on arm strength, leading to poor posture and easy passes. They also tend to keep their hips too flat, negating the leverage.

The key mechanical insight is using your hooked legs to create an upward lifting and turning force, combined with precise weight shifts to disrupt the opponent's base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat. Hook each of your opponent's ankles with the instep of your foot, ensuring your shins are angled towards the ceiling.
    2. Place your feet inside your opponent's legs, close to their hips, with your knees pointing outwards and slightly upwards.
    3. Grip your opponent's pants just above the knees, or their collars with your hands, keeping your elbows tucked in.
    4. Drive your hips upwards and slightly towards one side, creating a strong base and lifting your opponent's weight off their feet.
    5. Arch your back slightly, engaging your glutes and core to maintain the upward pressure.
    6. Use your hooked feet to subtly pull your opponent's legs towards you while simultaneously pushing your hips towards them.
    7. Shift your weight and hips to one side, initiating a turning motion that leverages your opponent's unbalanced weight to create a sweep.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Ankle Hooking: Placing your feet too far down the opponent's shins or outside their legs. This can lead to knee ligament tears (MCL/LCL) as the opponent's weight drives into your knee joint at an incorrect angle. Always hook the instep of your foot around their ankle and keep your shins angled upwards.
    • Over-Extending the Back: Arching your back too aggressively without core engagement. This places immense strain on the lumbar spine, potentially causing disc compression or muscle strains. Maintain a slight arch and engage your glutes and abs for support.
    • Allowing Opponent to Stack: Remaining flat on your back with hips too low. This allows the opponent to stack your weight, potentially leading to shoulder or neck injuries from the pressure. Drive your hips up and away to create space and lift.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Lift and Hook Drill: Practice lifting your hips off the mat and hooking your feet into an imaginary opponent's ankles. Focus on the feeling of the lift and maintaining the hook. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner Assisted Hip Lift: With a partner lying on their back, practice entering Butterfly Guard and lifting their hips slightly. Focus on hook placement and hip drive. (20 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Static Butterfly Guard Control: Your partner lies on their back. You enter Butterfly Guard and practice maintaining the hooks and hip lift without attempting sweeps. Partner provides minimal resistance. (30 seconds hold, 25% resistance)
    4. Butterfly Guard Sweep - Controlled Resistance: Your partner lies on their back. You enter Butterfly Guard and practice your preferred sweep. Your partner provides light, predictable resistance, focusing on allowing the sweep to happen. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Butterfly Guard Sweep - Dynamic Resistance: Your partner actively tries to defend the sweep, but still allows you to work your technique. Focus on timing and hip movement. (15 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling - Limited Butterfly Guard: In a rolling session, explicitly state you will be working Butterfly Guard. Your partner is aware and defends normally. Focus on applying the technique under live pressure. (5 minute rounds, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing down, attempting to pass your guard from a kneeling or low stance.
    • When your opponent is establishing a dominant top position, and you need to create space and disrupt their base.
    • As a transitional guard from other open guard positions when the opponent is close and their weight is accessible.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • The Stack Pass: When your opponent tries to 'stack' you by driving their weight straight down onto your hips, immediately drive your hips up and away, and use your hooks to pull one of their legs towards you, creating space to shrimp out or transition.
    • The Knee Slice Pass: If your opponent tries to drive their knee down along your hip, use your hooks to pull their knee towards your chest and simultaneously shrimp your hips in the opposite direction, creating a strong frame to prevent the pass.
    • The Toreando Pass: If your opponent tries to spin around you, use your hooks to lightly pull their closest leg towards you while posting your free foot on their hip, forcing them to face you and preventing them from circling past.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to do Butterfly Guard?

    Your back is likely hurting because you are arching excessively without engaging your core muscles. This puts undue stress on your lumbar spine. Instead of just arching, focus on driving your hips upwards and slightly towards your opponent, while simultaneously engaging your glutes and abs. This creates a stable base and uses your core to support the movement, reducing strain on your lower back.

    Q: How can I use Butterfly Guard effectively against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on their base by hooking their ankles with your insteps and keeping your shins angled upwards. Drive your hips up and *into* them, not just straight up, to disrupt their balance. Use your hooked legs to subtly pull their legs towards you while pushing your hips into them, creating an unstable platform they cannot easily maintain.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a sweep from Butterfly Guard?

    The optimal time to attempt a sweep is when your opponent is slightly off-balance or committed to a forward or sideways movement. Look for moments when they have weight on one leg, or when they are leaning heavily forward. This is when your hip lift and sweep motion will be most effective in disrupting their base and sending them to the mat.
