Anaconda Choke: A Precise BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Anaconda Choke is a powerful strangulation technique typically initiated from the front of an opponent, often when they are postured up or attempting to pass your guard.

It aims to constrict blood flow to the brain by compressing the carotid arteries.

Many white belts struggle due to a lack of understanding of spinal alignment and hip pressure, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The single most crucial mechanical insight is using your hips to drive forward and upward into the opponent's neck, creating a fulcrum with your shoulder.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a front-facing position (e.g., opponent's head is near your chest, arms are relatively loose), establish a "figure-four" grip: your right arm goes under their left armpit, and your left hand grips your own right bicep.
    2. Your left shoulder should be tucked into the side of their neck, creating a tight seal.
    3. Lower your hips to create space for your left leg to step over their head or shoulder, depending on the angle.
    4. Drive your hips forward and upward, creating pressure against their carotid artery with your right bicep and shoulder.
    5. Simultaneously, begin to arch your back and drive your chest into their upper back, further tightening the choke.
    6. Use your head to push against their head, preventing them from posturing up or creating space.
    7. Maintain a strong base by keeping your feet planted, distributing your weight evenly over your hips.
    8. Squeeze your arms together, pulling your right bicep towards your left forearm, increasing the constriction.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly tucking your chin: This can cause hyperextension of the cervical spine, leading to neck pain or potential nerve damage if the opponent continues to drive forward.

    The correct movement involves keeping your chin tucked slightly inward, protecting your airway and neck alignment.

    • Over-extending your back without hip drive: This places excessive stress on the lumbar spine, potentially causing lower back strains.

    Instead, focus on driving your hips forward and upward to generate the primary choking pressure.

    • Allowing your opponent to create space by posturing up: This often happens when the initial grip is loose or the hip drive is insufficient, leading to a "choked" arm being pulled out.

    Maintain tight control of the arm and neck, and use your head to pin their head to create a solid structure.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drill: Practice the grip setup and hip drive motion without a partner, focusing on the sequence and feel of the body mechanics (10 reps).
    2. Partner Drill (0% Resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the grip setup and hip drive, focusing on achieving the correct body position without any choking pressure (5 reps per side).
    3. Partner Drill (25% Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to focus on the hip drive and shoulder pressure to initiate the choke. Focus on the initial compression phase (5 reps per side).
    4. Partner Drill (50% Resistance): Partner begins to defend slightly, forcing you to adjust your hip and shoulder angles to maintain the choke. Focus on tightening the figure-four grip and driving into the neck (5 reps per side).
    5. Partner Drill (75% Resistance): Partner actively defends, requiring you to use more precise hip movement and body weight to secure the choke. Focus on the full sequence and maintaining control (3 reps per side).
    6. Live Rolling (90% Resistance): Apply the Anaconda Choke during rolling, but tap early if you feel the submission is not secure or if your partner is in a dangerous position. Focus on recognizing opportunities (3 attempts per roll).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent attempts a guard pass and their head is low, creating an opening for the "figure-four" grip.
    • When you have secured a front headlock position and can isolate one of their arms.
    • During scrambles where you can quickly secure the initial grip before they can establish defensive posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shoulder Post and Elbow Escape: If the choke is being applied from the side, the defender can post their shoulder into the attacker's hip and drive their elbow into the attacker's bicep to create space and break the grip.
    • "Sprawling" hips: The defender can aggressively drive their hips back and down, away from the attacker's forward pressure, to relieve the constriction on the neck.
    • "Stacking" the attacker: The defender can attempt to drive their weight forward and upward, essentially "stacking" the attacker on top of them, which can break the angle and loosen the choke.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Anaconda Choke?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are not driving your hips forward and upward sufficiently. Instead, you might be relying too much on pulling with your arms or arching your back, which puts direct strain on your cervical spine. Focus on initiating the choke by driving your pelvis into your opponent's neck and using your shoulder as a fulcrum, keeping your chin tucked to protect your own neck.

    Q: How do I finish the Anaconda Choke against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, you must prioritize achieving a superior angle and maximizing your body weight. Instead of just a hip drive, try to "climb" your hips higher up their body and use your legs to help anchor yourself. Your goal is to create a tight seal with your shoulder and bicep, then use your entire body weight by leaning back and driving your hips forward, effectively using their size against them by pinning them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Anaconda Choke from closed guard?

    The Anaconda Choke is rarely initiated directly from a standard Closed Guard. It's more effective when your opponent is attempting to pass your guard and their head is within reach, or if you have transitioned to a position like a front headlock from a failed sweep. Trying to force it from a static closed guard often leaves you vulnerable to counter-passes or leg attacks.
