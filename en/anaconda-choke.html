Anaconda Choke: A White Belt's Precise Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Anaconda Choke is a powerful submission applied from positions like Side Control or when the opponent is turtled, aiming to constrict blood flow to the brain.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of body awareness and a tendency to rely on brute strength rather than leverage.

The key is to create a "figure four" grip around the neck and arm, using your shoulder to drive into their carotid artery.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, secure a collar grip with your right hand on the opponent's left lapel, thumb inside.
    2. With your left arm, reach under their chin and grip your own right wrist, creating a "figure four" around their neck and left arm.
    3. Drive your right shoulder into their carotid artery on their left side, pressing your chest into their body.
    4. Lower your hips, angling your pelvis towards their feet, creating a perpendicular line to their body.
    5. Squeeze your biceps and triceps simultaneously, tightening the "figure four" grip.
    6. Shift your weight forward, driving your head into their shoulder to prevent them from posturing up.
    7. Maintain a strong base with your left knee on the mat and your right leg extended for stability, ensuring your hips are lower than theirs.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly squeezing your own biceps: This can cause excessive strain on the elbow joint, potentially leading to a medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow) injury due to repetitive hyperextension or overuse. The correct movement is to squeeze your arms together, not forcefully bend your elbows.
    • Over-extending the neck when applying the choke: This can strain the cervical spine ligaments or muscles, risking a neck sprain or whiplash-like injury. Maintain a neutral neck alignment and let the choke pressure do the work.
    • Driving your shoulder too aggressively into the opponent's head: This can cause shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strain by compressing the bursa or tendons within the shoulder joint. Focus on driving your shoulder into the carotid artery, not the side of their head.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling the "figure four" grip and shoulder drive: 10 reps per side, 0% resistance.
    2. Partner drilling the grip and shoulder drive with no resistance: 5 reps per side, 25% resistance.
    3. Partner drilling the full Anaconda Choke setup without finishing: 5 reps per side, 50% resistance.
    4. Partner drilling the Anaconda Choke with light resistance, focusing on maintaining the position: 5 reps per side, 75% resistance.
    5. Controlled drilling of the Anaconda Choke with a resisting partner, focusing on finishing mechanics: 3 reps per side, 90% resistance.
    6. Live rolling application of the Anaconda Choke from appropriate positions: 2-3 attempts per round, 100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent turtles up significantly, presenting their neck and arm.
    • During a guard pass attempt where the opponent exposes their neck and arm to defend.
    • From side control when the opponent attempts to bridge or shrimp out, creating an opening.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Grapevine and Hip Escape: If the choke is being applied from side control, the defender can grapevine the attacker's legs and hip escape to relieve pressure on the neck and create space.
    • Posture Up and Straighten: The defender can attempt to stand up and straighten their body, breaking the "figure four" grip and reducing the leverage of the choke.
    • Grip Release and Frame: The defender can use their free arm to frame against the attacker's shoulder or bicep, creating space to peel off the choking arm and release the grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Anaconda Choke?

    Your wrist might hurt because you are gripping your own wrist too tightly or in an awkward angle, leading to hyperextension or excessive pronation. Ensure your grip is firm but relaxed, focusing on squeezing your biceps together to create the choke pressure. Keep your wrist in a neutral, anatomical position to avoid strain.

    Q: How do I Anaconda Choke a much bigger opponent when they are defending?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a tighter "figure four" grip and driving your shoulder more forcefully into their carotid artery. Use your hips to angle your body perpendicular to theirs, effectively using their weight against them. Prioritize sinking your shoulder deep into their neck to cut off blood flow, rather than just pulling.

    Q: When is the best time to switch from a Darce Choke to an Anaconda Choke?

    You would typically switch from a Darce to an Anaconda if your opponent defends the Darce by turning into you and tucking their chin. This defensive movement often exposes the arm and neck in a way that is more conducive to the Anaconda setup. The key is recognizing the opponent's defensive reaction and adapting your choke accordingly.

    In-Depth: Anaconda Choke: A White Belt's Precise Guide

    Biomechanics & Physics

    The Anaconda choke leverages principles of applied anatomy and physics to create a submission. Anatomically, it targets the carotid arteries, responsible for blood flow to the brain, and the jugular veins, returning blood to the heart. By creating a figure-four grip, the practitioner effectively compresses these vessels. The specific leverage points are crucial. The practitioner's shoulder acts as a fulcrum, pressing into the opponent's carotid artery on the side of the trapped arm. The gripping arm, locked around the opponent's neck and the practitioner's own wrist, provides the constricting force. The body positioning—specifically, lowering the hips and angling the pelvis perpendicular to the opponent—maximizes the force vector. This allows the practitioner to use their body weight and spinal erectors to drive into the opponent, creating a superior mechanical advantage over simply squeezing with the arms. The goal is to collapse the opponent's posture and create a tight seal, preventing any arterial flow while allowing venous flow to continue, which can lead to a quicker tap.

    Common Mistakes (Specific to This Technique)

    • Insufficient Hip Angle: Failing to lower the hips and create a perpendicular body alignment often results in the Anaconda becoming a "neck crank" rather than a blood choke. This happens when the practitioner's body remains too parallel to the opponent, leading to pressure on the cervical spine instead of the carotids.
    • Over-Reliance on Grip Strength: White belts frequently try to muscle the choke closed by squeezing their biceps and triceps intensely. This is inefficient and leads to fatigue. The power should come from body weight and hip drive, not isolated arm strength.
    • Poor Head Position: Allowing the opponent to establish head and arm control, or failing to drive your head into their shoulder, negates the choke. Your head should be pressed firmly against their trapezius or shoulder on the side of the choke to prevent them from posturing up and relieving pressure.

    Variations & Counters

    The Anaconda choke serves as a gateway to understanding positional pressure and submission chains. From a failed Anaconda, particularly if the opponent escapes by posturing up, a transition to a Guillotine choke is often possible if the opponent's head is still trapped. Conversely, if the opponent tries to roll out to the side of the choke, this can create opportunities for a rear-naked choke if the practitioner can secure the back. An opponent defending the Anaconda might try to "stack" the practitioner, driving their weight down. This defense can be countered by shifting weight backward and using the grip to pull the opponent's head down, potentially transitioning into a triangle choke setup. Another common defense is the "turtle-up" to relieve pressure, which can be exploited to secure a Sode Guruma Jime (same-side arm triangle) if the opponent commits to that posture.

    Drilling Recommendations

    For white belts, drilling the Anaconda choke should focus on correct body mechanics and grip setting. Begin by drilling the setup from side control with a compliant partner. Focus on achieving the figure-four grip and driving the shoulder into the neck for 10-15 repetitions, ensuring proper hip angle and weight distribution. Progress to drilling the squeeze, but emphasize body movement over arm strength, aiming for 5-8 successful "taps" per side. Introduce mild resistance from the partner, where they attempt to posture up or frame, for 8-10 repetitions, focusing on maintaining control and finishing the choke. Finally, drill the transition from a missed Anaconda to a basic Guillotine attempt with 5-7 repetitions per side to build flow.
