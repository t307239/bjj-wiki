This technique initiates from a standing or clinch position, aiming to destabilize your opponent and bring them to the mat by controlling one of their legs.

White belts often fail by lunging with their head up, exposing their neck and failing to create a solid base.

The key is to drop your level, secure a deep grip, and use your hips and driving leg to power through.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Stance and Entry: Begin in a staggered stance, left foot forward. Your opponent is also standing. Assume a neutral or slightly pressing posture.
    2. Grip Acquisition: As your opponent shifts weight or offers an opening, step your right foot forward and outside their left foot. Simultaneously, shoot your left arm deep between their legs, securing a "collar grip" on their left ankle or lower shin, palm facing upwards and gripping the fabric firmly.
    3. Head Position: Drive your head to the opponent's right hip, ensuring your forehead makes contact and your head is tight against their body. This prevents them from easily posturing up.
    4. Weight Transfer and Drive: With your left arm secured on the ankle/shin, drive your right knee towards the mat behind your left foot. Your hips should be angled slightly forward, creating a "cutting" motion.
    5. Hip Drive: Initiate a powerful forward drive with your hips, pushing them into the opponent's torso. This unbalances them and initiates the takedown.
    6. Leg Drive and Sweep: Use your right leg (the one with the knee driving towards the mat) to simultaneously sweep their grounded leg backwards and upwards, assisting in the takedown.
    7. Finishing: As the opponent falls, maintain your head and arm control, and drive your body forward and slightly to the side, ensuring you land in a dominant position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Head Diving: Lunging forward with your head first and neck exposed, trying to grab the leg. This can lead to a "whiplash" injury or a dangerous "guillotine choke" setup because the cervical spine is hyperextended.
    • Correct: Drive your head to the opponent's hip, maintaining a strong neck and keeping your head up relative to your opponent's torso.
    • Knee Buckling: Shooting in with your lead knee bent at a sharp angle and then collapsing it to the mat. This can cause a "ligamentous knee sprain" (e.g., MCL tear) from the twisting force or direct impact.
    • Correct: Drive your knee behind your lead foot, aiming for a controlled drop, not a collapse.
    • Standing Up Too Early: Attempting to pull the opponent up from the leg grip without driving your hips forward. This can strain your lower back due to improper leverage and excessive spinal flexion.
    • Correct: Focus on hip drive and forward momentum before attempting to elevate the opponent.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Footwork and Grip Practice (0% resistance): Practice the staggered stance, step, and arm-shoot motion 20 times per side. Focus on hip angle and head position. No partner.
    2. Shadow Takedown (25% resistance): Imagine a stationary opponent. Perform the entire single leg takedown motion, focusing on the sequence and weight transfer. 10 reps per side.
    3. Stationary Partner Drill (50% resistance): Your partner stands passively. You practice the grip acquisition and the hip/leg drive, bringing them down gently. Focus on control. 10 reps per side, 2 minutes rest.
    4. Controlled Entry and Drive (75% resistance): Partner offers light resistance to your entry. You must work to secure the grip and execute the drive. Focus on breaking their posture slightly. 8 reps per side, 3 minutes rest.
    5. Live Entry (90% resistance): Partner actively defends but allows you to attempt the takedown. Focus on reacting to their defenses and maintaining your structure. 5 reps per side, 5 minutes rest.
    6. Live Rolling (100% resistance): Integrate the single leg takedown into sparring. Look for opportunities and execute with full intent, but prioritize safety and technique over winning.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up from "closed-guard" or "half-guard".
    • During a "clinch" exchange where you can step in and secure a deep grip.
    • When your opponent attempts a "guard-pass" and exposes their legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Sprawl" Counter: If the opponent shoots for your leg, immediately "sprawl" your hips back and down, extending your legs behind you. Drive your chest into their head and push their hips away to break the grip and establish a "pressure-pass" posture.
    • The "Switch Hips" Counter: As the opponent drives forward, quickly "switch" your hips to face the direction they are trying to take you. Use your free leg to "hook" their hip or thigh and pivot away, attempting to "sweep" or "reverse" the position.
    • The "Guillotine Choke" Counter: If the opponent shoots too high and leaves their head exposed, you can "counter" by "snapping" their head down and "locking" your arms around their neck for a "guillotine-choke" attempt.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a single leg takedown in BJJ?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are collapsing your lead knee directly to the mat without proper support or driving force. This can cause ligamentous strain or direct impact injury. To fix this, ensure your lead knee drives down *behind* your lead foot, and that your hips are driving forward powerfully to absorb the impact and facilitate the sweep, rather than taking the full force of the fall on the knee itself.

    Q: How do I do a single leg takedown against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage and timing are crucial. Focus on getting *underneath* their center of gravity by dropping your level significantly. Secure a deep grip on their ankle or shin, and use your head and shoulder pressure to drive into their hip. The key is to use their own weight and momentum against them by driving forward with your hips and sweeping their leg, rather than trying to lift them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a single leg takedown in BJJ if I'm a white belt?

    As a white belt, the best times to attempt a single leg takedown are when your opponent is actively posturing up from a "closed-guard" or "half-guard" position, or when they are moving forward aggressively and expose their legs. Avoid attempting it when they are stable and have good "base". Look for moments of imbalance or when they are reaching for you, creating an opening for your entry.
