Single Leg Takedown Technique for BJJ Beginners

This technique starts from a standing or clinch position, aiming to control one of your opponent's legs and bring them to the mat.

White belts often fail by lunging forward without proper base or off-balancing, leaving them vulnerable to sweeps or submissions.

The key is to use your opponent's forward momentum against them while securing a solid grip and driving through with your hips.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start with a staggered stance, opponent facing you. Secure a collar grip with your left hand and a sleeve grip on their right arm with your right hand.
    2. Step your left foot forward and slightly to the outside of their right foot, creating a base width of approximately shoulder-width.
    3. As you step, lower your level by bending your knees and hips, keeping your back straight and head up.
    4. Simultaneously, pull down on their collar and sleeve with your arms, creating a slight angle of their upper body away from you.
    5. Drive your right hip forward and slightly inward, past their right hip, while keeping your left knee bent and close to the mat.
    6. Secure a deep grip on their right ankle or lower shin with your right hand, ensuring your forearm is tight against their leg.
    7. Drive forward and slightly upward with your chest and head, using your hips to elevate and control their leg, leading to the takedown.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Lunging forward with straight legs: This can hyperextend the knees, causing ACL or MCL tears due to excessive anterior/posterior shear forces.
    • Gripping too low on the leg without controlling the upper body: This allows the opponent to easily post their free leg and counter with a sweep or escape.
    • Dropping your head to the mat: This can lead to neck injuries or allow the opponent to establish a dominant position like side control.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo bodyweight practice: Mimic the leg drive and hip movement without a partner. 10 reps per side.
    2. Partner assisted static drill (0% resistance): Partner stands passively. Focus on grip acquisition and hip drive. 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner assisted dynamic drill (25% resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to movement. Focus on maintaining balance and driving through. 5 reps per side.
    4. Controlled entry drill (50% resistance): Partner offers resistance to the initial entry. Focus on reacting and completing the takedown. 5 reps per side.
    5. Live entry drilling (75% resistance): Partner actively defends. Focus on timing and commitment to the takedown. 3 reps per side.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Attempt the single leg takedown during sparring. Focus on execution and learning from defenses. 3-5 attempts per round.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is too close in a clinch and leaning forward.
    • When your opponent overcommits to a forward movement or strike.
    • When you have secured a strong grip and can break their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Whizzer (overhook): When the opponent attempts the single leg, secure an overhook on their far side (your right arm over their left shoulder) and drive your weight into them, preventing them from elevating your leg.
    • Sprawl: As they shoot for your leg, quickly widen your base and drive your hips back and down, placing your weight on their upper back.
    • Footwork escape: If they secure a weak grip, step your free leg back and pivot your hips away, creating space to disengage.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Single Leg Takedown?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are lunging forward with straight legs or not driving your hips through effectively. This puts excessive hyperextension or shear force on the knee joint. To fix this, ensure you bend your knees and hips deeply, keeping your back straight, and focus on driving your hips forward and upward to lift the opponent's leg, rather than just pushing your body forward.

    Q: How do I do a Single Leg Takedown against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, focus on timing and leverage. Secure a strong collar and sleeve grip to break their posture and create an angle. Instead of trying to overpower them, use their forward momentum against them by ducking under their weight and driving your hips in low. A deep ankle grip combined with a powerful hip drive will be more effective than brute strength.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Single Leg Takedown if my opponent is defending well?

    The best time is when your opponent overcommits to a forward motion, like a push or a poorly timed strike. You can also create opportunities by feinting a shot or by using your grips to pull them slightly off-balance. Look for moments when their base is compromised or when they are not fully squared up to you.
