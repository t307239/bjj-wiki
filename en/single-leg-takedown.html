This technique starts from a standing position, aiming to off-balance and bring your opponent to the mat by controlling one of their legs. White belts often rush this, neglecting foundational body mechanics and grip integrity. The key mechanical insight is to use your head to drive forward and your hips to drop and rotate, not to simply pull with your arms.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Initiate by securing a collar grip with your non-driving arm and a wrist grip on your opponent's sleeve with your driving arm. Your hips should be angled slightly towards your opponent's side.
    2. Step forward with your foot on the same side as the leg you are attacking, placing it slightly outside your opponent's foot. Your driving knee should bend deeply, aiming for a position below your opponent's knee.
    3. Simultaneously, lower your hips by bending your knees and slightly rotating your torso away from your opponent, creating a 'shooting' motion. Your head should be driving into your opponent's chest or shoulder.
    4. Secure a 'crotch grab' or 'over-hook' grip on your opponent's thigh with your non-driving arm, ensuring your forearm is tight against their hamstring.
    5. Drive forward with your head and chest, maintaining a low base. Your driving leg's knee should remain on the mat or very close to it, acting as an anchor.
    6. As you drive, elevate your opponent's leg by extending your hips and straightening your driving leg. Simultaneously, pull their leg towards your chest with your arms.
    7. Continue to drive forward and slightly to the side, forcing your opponent to fall. Your body should remain in a stable, low base throughout the movement.
    8. Once your opponent is on the mat, transition to controlling the leg and securing a dominant position like side control or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Lunging forward with straight legs: This places extreme stress on the lumbar spine, risking a herniated disc as the posterior chain is overextended. The correct movement involves bending knees and dropping hips before driving.
    • Gripping only the leg without a solid base: This allows your opponent to easily circle out or sweep you, potentially causing knee ligament tears (ACL/MCL) if your knee buckles under uneven pressure. Always establish a low, stable base and drive with your body, not just your arms.
    • Tucking your chin or looking up: This compromises your head position and balance, making you susceptible to concussions or neck injuries from uncontrolled falls. Maintain head control by driving your forehead into your opponent's chest or shoulder.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo stance and shot drill: Practice the shooting motion and body positioning without a partner. Focus on hip drop and knee bend. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partnered entry drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice securing grips and taking the shot. Partner offers no resistance to the takedown. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    3. Partnered entry with grip defense: Partner resists grips slightly but allows the shot to be initiated. Focus on breaking grips and completing the entry. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    4. Live entry drill: Practice the takedown from a standing clinch. Partner offers moderate resistance to the shot. Focus on completing the entry and initial leg control. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    5. Live single leg takedown drill: Attempt the full single leg takedown from a standing clinch. Partner actively defends the takedown. Focus on finishing the takedown and maintaining control. (5 reps per side, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the single leg takedown into sparring rounds. Focus on timing and situational awareness. (3 rounds, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is in a staggered stance, offering an easy target.
    • When your opponent attempts a tie-up or clinch, creating an opening for a shot.
    • When your opponent is posturing up, creating a space to shoot between their legs.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Sprawl: As your opponent shoots, quickly drop your hips back and to the side, placing your weight on their shoulders and chest. This prevents them from securing the leg and drives their head down.
    • Whizzer (overhook): While they are shooting, secure an overhook on their arm and drive your shoulder into their head, forcing them to break their posture and making the takedown difficult.
    • Footwork and Circle Out: If the opponent's grip is weak or the shot is poorly timed, quickly step back and circle your hips away, breaking their control and creating distance.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do a single leg takedown?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are landing with your driving leg's knee directly on the mat without proper hip flexion and forward drive. This can hyperextend or twist the knee. Instead, focus on bending your hips and dropping your weight *before* your knee makes contact, keeping your knee aligned with your foot and driving forward through your hips.

    Q: How can I do a single leg takedown against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are crucial. Focus on attacking their base when they are off-balance or reaching. Secure a deep grip on their leg and drive your forehead into their chest or shoulder, using your entire body weight to create forward momentum. Don't try to lift them; instead, drive them forward and to the side to disrupt their balance.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a single leg takedown in BJJ if I'm a white belt?

    As a white belt, the best times to attempt a single leg takedown are when your opponent is in a staggered stance, their weight is forward, or they are reaching for you. Avoid attempting it when they are perfectly balanced or have a strong, tight base. Look for opportunities when they create a slight opening by stepping or leaning.
