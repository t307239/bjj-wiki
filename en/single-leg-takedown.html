Single Leg Takedown: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The single leg takedown is initiated from a standing position, aiming to off-balance and bring your opponent to the mat by controlling one of their legs. White belts often struggle due to a lack of fundamental body control and fear of falling. The key mechanical insight is using your head and shoulder to drive *through* your opponent, not just *at* them, creating a stable base and forward momentum.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a collar grip with your lead hand (e.g., right hand on opponent's left collar) and a sleeve grip with your trail hand (e.g., left hand on opponent's left sleeve).
    2. Step forward with your non-takedown leg (e.g., right leg) to a position slightly wider than shoulder-width, creating a stable base.
    3. Simultaneously, lower your level by bending both knees, bringing your hips below your opponent's hips.
    4. Drive your lead shoulder (e.g., right shoulder) into your opponent's torso, aiming for their sternum or upper abdomen, while keeping your head up and driving forward.
    5. Secure a "hook" grip around your opponent's lead ankle with your trail arm (e.g., left arm), ensuring your forearm is tight against their shin.
    6. Drive your hips forward and slightly downward, simultaneously pulling their ankle upwards and slightly backward with your hooked arm.
    7. Continue forward pressure with your head and shoulder, using your legs to drive your opponent off balance and to the mat, finishing in a dominant position like side-control or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Injury: Attempting to "shoot" too low without proper hip control and driving with the head can cause the lead knee to hyperextend or buckle under impact, leading to ACL or MCL tears. The correct movement involves bending both knees and driving with the torso.
    • Back Injury: Trying to "pull" the opponent down using only arm strength without driving your body weight forward can lead to lumbar spine hyperextension and disc strain. The force must come from your legs and core, driving your body forward.
    • Ankle/Foot Injury: Grabbing the ankle loosely or improperly can cause your hand to slip, leading to a loss of balance and a potential awkward fall. Secure a firm hook grip with your forearm against their shin, and drive with your entire body.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling (0% Resistance): Practice the footwork, level change, and head/shoulder drive motion without a partner. Perform 10 repetitions.
    2. Partnered Gripping and Level Change (0% Resistance): With a compliant partner, practice establishing grips and performing the level change and head/shoulder drive. Perform 10 repetitions.
    3. Controlled Single Leg Entry (25% Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance. Practice the full sequence from grip to driving your opponent backwards slightly. Perform 5 repetitions per side.
    4. Assisted Takedown (50% Resistance): Partner takes a controlled fall once the takedown is initiated. Focus on hip drive and finishing to the mat. Perform 5 repetitions per pair.
    5. Light Sparring (75% Resistance): Attempt the single leg takedown during live rolling, focusing on timing and execution against a resisting opponent who is also trying to defend. Perform 3 rounds.
    6. Full Sparring (100% Resistance): Integrate the single leg takedown into your regular sparring sessions, aiming for successful execution against a fully resisting opponent. Perform 5 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is leaning forward, creating an open lane for your shot.
    • When your opponent has a weak base or is off-balance, making them susceptible to a takedown.
    • When you have established strong collar and sleeve grips, allowing you to control their posture and create opportunities.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Sprawl: When you feel the opponent shooting, quickly drive your hips back and down, placing your shins on the mat and your chest on their upper back. This breaks their grip and base.
    • Whizzer: As the opponent secures the leg, reach over their back with your arm and hook their armpit, applying downward pressure. This hinders their ability to drive forward and allows you to potentially circle out or transition.
    • Circle Out: If the opponent has a weak grip or is not driving effectively, use your free leg to step around their hips and circle out to the side, breaking their control and resetting your base.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a single leg takedown?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are dropping your lead knee to the mat too early or too hard without proper hip control. This puts excessive shear force on the knee joint. Ensure you bend both knees, keep your head driving forward into your opponent's torso, and maintain a strong base with your hips below their hips before committing to the pull.

    Q: How do I perform a single leg takedown against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on timing and leverage rather than pure strength. Wait for them to step forward or lean, creating an opening. Use your head and shoulder to drive *through* their mass, aiming to off-balance them. Secure the ankle grip and drive your hips forward, using their size against them as you pull them down.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a single leg takedown in a BJJ match?

    The ideal time is when your opponent is posturing up, creating space between your guards, or when they are attempting to pass your guard and are leaning forward. You can also initiate it from standing if they are not maintaining a tight, balanced stance. Look for moments of instability or forward momentum from your opponent.
