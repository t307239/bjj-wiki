The Pressure Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position when the opponent has established a guard, typically closed or open guard. Its primary goal is to break the opponent's guard and establish Side Control or mount.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute force or incorrect weight distribution, leading to stalled passes and potential injury. They may not understand the fundamental mechanics of driving weight downwards and forwards.

The key mechanical insight is to use your body's mass and skeletal structure to apply constant downward and forward pressure, collapsing the opponent's structure and limiting their mobility. This is achieved through precise body positioning and weight transfer.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a staggered, kneeling stance, one knee forward (e.g., right knee down) and the other back (e.g., left foot forward). Your chest should be over your opponent's hips.
    2. Secure a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand, palm facing your face, and your left hand on their opposite hip or waist. Alternatively, use a double-collar grip.
    3. Drive your hips forward and down, aiming to sink your chest into their sternum. Your forehead should be close to their chest, creating a "smash" effect.
    4. Your right knee should be angled slightly inward, pointed towards their left hip, to prevent them from easily turning into you.
    5. Your left foot, planted firmly, acts as a base and allows for small forward shuffles as you advance.
    6. Maintain a straight spine and tuck your chin to protect your neck. Your weight should be distributed over their hips and lower torso.
    7. As you feel their structure begin to break, shuffle your left foot forward and drive your hips further, aiming to bring your right knee closer to their side.
    8. Transition to side control by releasing the collar grip and securing an underhook with your right arm, keeping your chest heavy on their chest.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Extension Injury: Trying to "push off" with the back leg (e.g., left leg) while keeping the front knee (e.g., right knee) planted can hyperextend the front knee, damaging the ACL or PCL. The correct movement is to drive forward with your hips, not push with your leg.
    • Lower Back Strain: Arching your back excessively to create pressure, instead of using hip drive, puts immense strain on the lumbar spine. Maintain a neutral spine and use your glutes and core to generate force.
    • Neck Compression: Allowing your head to drop too low or at an awkward angle, especially if the opponent attempts a guillotine, can lead to cervical spine compression or nerve impingement. Keep your chin tucked and maintain awareness of your head position relative to their arm.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Drive Drill: From your staggered kneeling position, practice driving your hips forward and down repeatedly, focusing on the sensation of weight transfer. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    2. Grip and Hip Drive: Practice securing your collar and hip grips, then driving your hips forward and down. Hold the position for 3 seconds. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Opponent Static Hold: Have a partner hold a closed guard with no resistance. Practice your staggered stance, grips, and hip drive, maintaining pressure for 5 seconds before releasing. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Opponent Minimal Resistance Guard Break: Partner maintains a loose closed guard. Practice breaking the guard with your hip drive and transitioning to side control. Partner offers minimal resistance to hip movement. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Controlled Pressure Pass: Partner actively tries to recompose guard or shrimp away minimally. Focus on maintaining constant pressure and forward hip drive. (5 rounds, 2 minutes each, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling Pressure Pass Attempt: In live rolling, actively look for opportunities to implement the Pressure Pass against a resisting opponent. Focus on executing the mechanics correctly, not necessarily on finishing the pass. (5 rounds, 3 minutes each, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent has established a closed guard and is not actively threatening with submissions.
    • When your opponent attempts to open their guard and create space for sweeps or submissions.
    • When you have a stable base and are in a kneeling or staggered position facing your opponent's hips.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Shrimping and Re-guarding: If the passer drives too far forward without breaking your base, actively shrimp your hips away and bring your knee between you and the passer to re-establish guard. The key is to create space and angular movement.
    • Hips to the Mat and Framing: If the passer is smashing down with excessive weight, flatten your hips to the mat and use your forearms to create frames between your chest and their chest, preventing further forward drive. This allows you to create space to escape or transition.
    • Leg Pummeling and Guard Retention: As the passer attempts to advance their knee, actively pummel your legs to keep them on the outside of your hips, preventing them from penetrating your guard and establishing side control. This requires active hip and leg movement.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Pressure Pass?

    Your knee pain likely stems from hyperextending your front knee or jamming it into the mat. Instead of pushing with your legs, focus on driving your hips forward and down using your glutes and core. Keep your front knee slightly bent and angled inward to create a stable base and prevent direct impact.

    Q: How can I Pressure Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes critical. Focus on sinking your chest low into their sternum to disrupt their posture and weight distribution. Secure a strong cross-collar grip to control their upper body and use your hips to drive forward, collapsing their structure rather than trying to muscle through them. Small, controlled forward shuffles are more effective than large movements.

    Q: When is the best time to start the Pressure Pass?

    The ideal time to initiate the Pressure Pass is when your opponent has committed to establishing a guard, particularly a Closed Guard, and is not actively threatening with submissions. Look for moments when they are slightly off-balance or have created a small opening by attempting to open their guard. Avoid attempting it when they have strong frames or are actively attacking.
