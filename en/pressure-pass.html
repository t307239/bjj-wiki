Mastering the Pressure Pass in BJJ for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Pressure Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a Closed Guard, aiming to break the opponent's guard and achieve side control.

White belts often struggle by trying to force the pass with raw strength instead of controlled weight distribution and leverage.

The core principle is to drive your body weight forward and downward, creating a heavy, immovable base that crushes the opponent's legs.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish Cross-Collar Grip with your right hand and Left Sleeve Grip with your left hand.
    2. Position your right knee inside the opponent's right thigh, creating a strong base with your left leg extended behind you.
    3. Drive your hips forward and down, aiming your sternum towards the opponent's chest, reducing space.
    4. Maintain a wide base with your feet, roughly shoulder-width apart, ensuring stability.
    5. Use your cross-collar grip to pull the opponent's head towards your chest, breaking their posture.
    6. With your left hand on the sleeve, slightly lift and pull their arm to create an angle for your body.
    7. Step your right foot forward, bringing your left knee closer to your right hip, maintaining constant forward pressure.
    8. Rotate your hips to the right, driving your right shoulder into the opponent's hip to clear their legs.
    9. As their legs begin to open, drive your chest through their guard and establish side control, keeping your weight heavy.
    10. Secure a Cross-Face grip with your right arm and a far-side Hip Grip with your left hand to maintain control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly attempting to "smash" through the guard with straight knees can hyperextend the knee joint, leading to ACL or MCL tears.
    • Gripping too tightly on the collar or sleeve and twisting your wrist unnaturally can cause wrist sprains or tendonitis.
    • Allowing your base to become too narrow while driving forward can lead to a loss of balance, making you vulnerable to sweeps and potentially injuring your ankle or knee on the fall.
    • Failing to keep your hips low and driving your weight down can result in your opponent bridging or shrimping effectively, putting strain on your lower back.
    • Overextending your neck while trying to maintain head control can lead to cervical strain.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drilling: Practice the step-by-step body mechanics without a partner, focusing on hip angles and weight transfer. 20 reps.
    2. Partner Drilling (Static): Partner lies on their back, you practice the grip setup and initial forward drive without resistance. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partner Drilling (Light Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice breaking posture and clearing their legs. 5 reps per side.
    4. Guard Break and Transition Drill: Partner maintains a loose closed guard. You practice breaking the guard and moving to side control. 5 reps per side.
    5. Flow Rolling (Controlled): Roll with a partner who is actively but gently defending, focusing on maintaining pressure and correct body mechanics. 5 minutes, 75% resistance.
    6. Live Rolling (Pressure Pass Focus): Roll with a partner, specifically attempting the Pressure Pass from closed guard. 5 minutes, 90% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent has established a tight closed guard and you are struggling to create space.
    • When your opponent has a weaker posture and you have an opportunity to drive forward.
    • When you are in a kneeling position and your opponent is trying to maintain a strong base.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): As the pressure passer drives forward, actively push off their hip with your foot and shrimp your hips away to create space and re-establish guard.
    • Bridge and Roll: When the pressure passer commits their weight forward, use your legs to hook their hips and bridge your hips upwards, then roll to your side to sweep them.
    • Re-Guard: If the pressure passer's base is too narrow or they lose their balance, use your feet to push their hips away and quickly bring your knees back between you to re-establish guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Pressure Pass?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are not maintaining a proper base or are driving forward with locked knees. When you drive forward, your lead knee should be bent and angled inwards, supporting your weight, while your rear leg provides a stable base. Avoid hyperextending your lead knee or allowing it to collapse inwards.

    Q: How can I Pressure Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on biomechanical leverage over brute strength. Use your grips to break their posture by pulling their head down and away. Drive your chest and sternum into their chest, reducing their ability to use their size effectively. Keep your hips low and your base wide to prevent them from overpowering you.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Pressure Pass in BJJ?

    The optimal time to attempt a Pressure Pass is when your opponent has a relatively tight guard and their posture is compromised, meaning their head is closer to their chest. It's also effective when you can establish dominant grips and begin to drive your weight forward before they can fully solidify their defensive frame.
