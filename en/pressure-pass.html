The Pressure Pass is a fundamental guard passing technique originating from standing or top half-guard, aiming to flatten the opponent and establish Side Control or mount.

White belts often struggle by trying to force their way through, neglecting hip pressure and proper weight distribution, leading to ineffective passes and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is to use your body's mass and structure to create a "smash" effect, collapsing the opponent's base and preventing them from reguarding.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From standing, secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a cross-sleeve grip on your opponent's left arm with your left hand. Your hips should be angled slightly to your right, creating a base width of approximately shoulder-width.
    2. Step your left foot forward, placing it outside your opponent's left knee. Your right knee should be angled towards the mat, directly behind your left foot.
    3. Drive your left shoulder into your opponent's chest, ensuring your head is to their left side, creating a strong frame.
    4. Initiate a forward hip drive, pushing your pelvis towards your opponent's hips. This transfers your weight directly downwards and forwards.
    5. As you drive, begin to "smash" their legs by continuing to angle your left knee towards the mat and driving your right knee behind their hip.
    6. Maintain your collar and sleeve grips to control their upper body, preventing them from shrimping or creating frames.
    7. Continue to drive forward and downward, using your chest and shoulder to flatten their hips to the mat, then advance to side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to "muscle" the pass by pulling hard on grips while standing upright: This can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears as the glenohumeral joint is forced into an unstable position.
    • Bending your knees excessively and dropping your hips too low without forward pressure: This exposes your knees to hyperextension injuries, especially if the opponent attempts a leg entanglement.
    • Allowing your opponent to establish a strong frame with their hands on your hips or shoulders without driving your own weight forward: This can result in elbow hyperextension if you are pushed back suddenly.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the footwork and hip drive motion 20 times on each side, focusing on hip angle and knee placement, with 0% resistance.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): From the starting position, have a partner stand passively. Execute the full sequence 10 times, focusing on grip security and weight transfer, with 0% resistance.
    3. Controlled progression: From top half-guard, have a partner resist slightly by keeping their legs closed. Practice the drive and smash for 1 minute, then switch roles, with 25% resistance.
    4. Introduction to pressure: From closed guard, have a partner attempt to maintain guard. Execute the pressure pass sequence for 2 minutes, focusing on breaking posture and driving forward, with 50% resistance.
    5. Live sparring (controlled): In a rolling scenario, focus exclusively on applying the pressure pass for 3-minute rounds, with 75% resistance.
    6. Full sparring: Incorporate the pressure pass into your regular sparring sessions, attempting it whenever the opportunity arises, with 90-100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is playing a tight closed guard and you have established good posture control.
    • When you have secured a dominant half-guard position and the opponent is struggling to create space.
    • When the opponent attempts to sit up or shrimp, creating an opening for a forward drive.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimp): If the opponent drives forward, the defender should immediately hip escape to their left, creating space to re-establish guard or transition to a different position. This involves pushing off the mat with the right foot and driving the hips away.
    • Underhook/Frame Creation: The defender can attempt to secure an underhook on the passer's arm or create a strong frame with their forearm against the passer's neck or shoulder to prevent the forward drive and maintain guard.
    • Leg Pummeling: The defender can actively pummel their legs to try and re-establish guard, such as by bringing their top leg between the passer's legs to create a closed guard or to transition to a butterfly guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Pressure Pass from half guard?

    Your knee pain likely stems from your knee driving directly into the opponent's hip or thigh without proper angling. Instead of a direct forward drive, focus on angling your knee *behind* their hip, creating a "smash" effect. Ensure your own hip is driving forward and down, collapsing their base rather than jamming your knee joint.

    Q: How can I effectively Pressure Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is to use structure and leverage, not brute strength. Focus on getting your head and shoulder to their chest, creating a strong frame to prevent them from using their mass. Drive your hips forward and down, aiming to collapse their base and flatten them. Control their posture with your grips to neutralize their size advantage.

    Q: I keep getting stuck in the opponent's guard when I try the Pressure Pass, what am I doing wrong?

    You are likely not committing enough weight and forward pressure. White belts often hesitate, creating a gap where the opponent can re-establish guard. Once you commit to the forward hip drive and shoulder pressure, do not stop. Think of it as a "smash" that collapses their legs and hips, making it impossible for them to insert their legs effectively.
