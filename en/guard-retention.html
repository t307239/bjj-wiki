Guard Retention: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Guard retention starts from your back, aiming to prevent your opponent from passing your legs and achieving a dominant position like Side Control or mount.

White belts often fail by statically holding their legs or pushing with their arms, creating openings for passes.

The key is active, dynamic framing with your forearms and shins, creating space and redirecting pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a closed guard: Knees are tucked, ankles crossed, hips slightly elevated off the mat.
    2. Opponent attempts a pass: They will likely try to break your closed guard by posturing up or stepping their legs to the side.
    3. Create frames: Place your forearms (ulnar bone to elbow) between your hips and their hips, creating a "frame" to push against.
    4. Maintain leg connection: Keep your knees tight enough to prevent easy leg separation, but not so tight that you cannot move.
    5. Hip movement: Use your hips to shrimp (move laterally) away from their pressure, creating space.
    6. Redirect their weight: As they push into you, use your frames and hip movement to guide their weight away from your center line.
    7. Re-establish guard: If they create space, immediately re-close your guard or transition to an open guard position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Statically pushing with open palms against the opponent's hips: This can lead to wrist hyperextension or ligament tears (e.g., TFCC tear) if the opponent applies significant pressure or shifts their weight unexpectedly. The correct alternative is to use a "frame" with your forearms, distributing pressure along the bone.
    • Letting your knees splay wide open when the opponent tries to pass: This creates a large opening for their legs to enter, potentially leading to knee joint hyperextension or ligament damage (e.g., MCL sprain) if they force a pass. The correct alternative is to keep your knees relatively close, using your shins to block their hips.
    • Arching your back excessively to create distance: This puts immense strain on your lumbar spine, risking disc compression or muscle strains. The correct alternative is to use your hips to shrimp away, maintaining a neutral spine position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice shrimping and framing against an imaginary opponent for 2 minutes.
    2. Partner drilling - static position (25% resistance): Partner lies on their back, you are in closed guard. Partner gently tries to posture up. You practice creating frames and shrimping. 5 reps.
    3. Partner drilling - basic pass attempt (50% resistance): Partner attempts a simple closed guard pass (e.g., standing up and stepping legs out). You focus on framing and hip escape to maintain guard. 5 reps.
    4. Partner drilling - varied pass attempts (75% resistance): Partner attempts multiple pass variations (e.g., knee slice, toreando). You focus on transition and frame maintenance. 5 reps.
    5. Light rolling - specific focus (90% resistance): Roll with a partner, with the explicit goal of keeping your guard closed for 3 minutes. They are allowed to pass, but not with maximum intensity. Focus on retention.
    6. Live rolling (100% resistance): Implement guard retention techniques during regular rolling. Be mindful of your frames and hip movement throughout the roll.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is attempting to break your closed guard by posturing up.
    • Opponent is trying to step their legs to the side to initiate a pass.
    • Opponent has achieved a dominant position (e.g., knee slice pressure) and you need to re-establish your guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Forearm/Shin Framing: Place forearms between your hips and their hips, or shins across their hips, to create space and prevent them from collapsing your guard. Maintain a solid, unyielding frame.
    • Hip Escapes (Shrimping): Actively move your hips away from the direction of their pressure. This creates space to re-angle your legs and prevent them from flattening you out.
    • Leg Re-angling/Closure: As space is created, use your legs to re-establish control (e.g., re-closing the guard) or transition to a more advantageous open guard position (e.g., half guard).

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to keep my guard closed against a bigger person?

    Your wrist pain is likely due to hyperextension caused by pushing with an open palm against your opponent's hips. They are leveraging their weight to drive into your unsupported hands. Instead of pushing with your palms, create a "frame" by using your forearms from elbow to wrist, or even your bicep against their hip. This distributes the pressure more effectively along your bones and protects your wrist joint.

    Q: How do I keep my guard against a much heavier opponent who keeps smashing me?

    Against a heavier opponent, static grips and pushing are ineffective. Focus on dynamic "framing" with your forearms and shins to create space and "bridge" their weight away from your center line. Use your hips to "shrimp" laterally, constantly moving to avoid being flattened. The goal is to make them work to maintain their pressure, rather than fighting their weight directly.

    Q: When is the best time to try and "re-close" my guard if it gets opened?

    The ideal time to re-close your guard is the moment you create even a small aMount of space. This usually happens when the opponent shifts their weight, misses a grip, or when you successfully execute a hip escape. As soon as you feel that slight separation, immediately bring your knees together and ankles crossed, using your shins to block their hips to prevent them from passing into the newly created space.
