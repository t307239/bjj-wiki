Guard retention begins when your opponent attempts to pass your guard, whether from closed, open, or half-guard. It aims to re-establish or maintain your guard position, preventing them from achieving dominant control.

White belts often fail by panicking, flailing limbs, or relying on strength instead of structure. This leads to predictable movements and easy passes.

The core mechanical insight is creating frames and using your body's levers to wedge space, rather than simply holding on. This makes your structure strong against pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent attempts to pass, step outside your leg base. Activate your hips, driving them towards the opponent's hips to create space.
    2. Place your forearms (ulnar border) firmly against the opponent's biceps or shoulders, creating rigid frames. Your elbows should be slightly bent, not locked out.
    3. Simultaneously, use your feet to push against the opponent's hips or thighs, depending on the distance, to further create space and control their base.
    4. If they are close, bring your knees together, tucking them towards your chest while maintaining forearm frames, creating a tight 'shell' or closed guard posture.
    5. If they are slightly further, use a "shrimping" motion (lateral hip escape) to move your hips away, increasing the distance and re-establishing your leg entanglement.
    6. Maintain a wide base with your feet on the mat or on their hips, ensuring your center of gravity is low and stable.
    7. Continuously adjust your frames and grips, always seeking to wedge space between your body and theirs, preventing them from "flattening" you out.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to hold opponent's sleeves with a death grip: This overloads your wrist joints, risking hyperextension injuries. The correct action is to use your forearms to frame against their biceps/shoulders.
    • Arching your back excessively to create space: This puts immense strain on your lumbar spine, risking disc herniation or muscle pulls. Instead, use controlled hip shrimping and forearm frames.
    • Locking elbows straight when framing: This makes your frames brittle and easily broken, while also risking elbow hyperextension. Keep a slight bend in your elbows for shock absorption and frame integrity.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Escapes and Frame Placement: Practice shrimping and placing forearm frames against an imaginary opponent. 100 reps each.
    2. Partner Drill: Opponent applies light pressure, you practice shrimping and framing to maintain guard. 5 rounds x 1 minute (25% resistance).
    3. Partner Drill: Opponent attempts a simple pass (e.g., knee slice). You focus solely on framing and shrimping to regain guard. 5 rounds x 1 minute (50% resistance).
    4. Partner Drill: Opponent attempts a pass with moderate resistance. Focus on combining frames, hip escapes, and leg repositioning. 5 rounds x 1 minute (75% resistance).
    5. Live Rolling (Guard Retention Focus): Start rolling with a partner. Your primary objective is to prevent them from passing your guard for the entire round. 3 rounds x 3 minutes (90% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling: Full rolling session, applying guard retention principles as needed against varied passing attempts. 5 rounds x 3 minutes (100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent steps to the side of your closed guard, attempting to break it open.
    • Opponent drives forward with chest pressure into your open or half-guard.
    • Opponent begins to circle their hips to your side, looking for a pass.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Forearm Frames and Hip Escape: When opponent steps outside your legs, immediately drive hips towards them and place forearms on their biceps. Shrimp hips away to create space and re-establish guard.
    • Knee Shield Creation: If opponent is pushing into you, bring one knee up to create a "shield" against their chest/hips. Use your shin and foot to push their hips away while keeping the other leg ready to re-entangle.
    • Base Control and Framing: Maintain a strong base by keeping your feet wide or on their hips. Use your hands to frame on their hips/shoulders, preventing them from collapsing your posture and driving through.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to keep my guard closed against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Your wrist is likely hurting because you're trying to use pure grip strength to hold your opponent's sleeves or pants. Bigger opponents have more mass, making this ineffective and dangerous. Instead, focus on creating a strong frame with your forearms against their biceps and using your hips to create space. This biomechanically distributes the force, protecting your wrists and making your guard much harder to break.

    Q: How do I maintain my guard against someone who keeps pushing their chest into me and flattening me out?

    When an opponent drives their chest into you, they are trying to collapse your posture and break your guard structure. To counter this, immediately create a "knee shield" by bringing one knee up between your body and theirs, using your shin and foot to actively push their hips away. Simultaneously, use your other foot or hand to frame on their hip or shoulder, preventing them from driving forward and maintaining the necessary space to keep your guard intact.

    Q: I keep losing my guard when I try to switch between open and closed guard. What's the key mechanical principle?

    The key principle is maintaining continuous contact and framing. When transitioning, don't let your legs go completely slack or your arms drop. Keep your feet actively engaged on their hips or use your forearms to frame against their shoulders. The moment you release contact with one limb to reposition it, ensure another limb is already establishing a new frame or control point to prevent your opponent from exploiting that brief moment of vulnerability and passing your guard.
