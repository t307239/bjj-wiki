Guard retention starts from a compromised position, aiming to re-establish a strong defensive guard before a pass is completed.

White belts often fail by panicking and giving up space, leading to easily exploitable openings and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is maintaining frames and connection to prevent the opponent from creating angles and reducing your base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent attempts a guard pass from standing or kneeling, driving forward.
    2. Immediately establish "forearm frames" by placing your forearms between your hips and the opponent's torso, elbows bent at 90 degrees.
    3. Extend your legs to create distance, pushing against the opponent's hips or thighs with your feet, maintaining a 3-point base (both feet, one hip).
    4. If the opponent is using a "grip fighting" approach, secure "double sleeve grips" with your hands on their wrists, palms facing inwards.
    5. When the opponent drives forward, use your frames to push their weight back, simultaneously "shrimping" (moving your hips away) to create space.
    6. If they attempt a "torreano pass" (stepping around), pivot your hips and bring your knee across their body to re-establish a "closed guard" or "half guard" position.
    7. Maintain a "hip angle" of approximately 45 degrees to your opponent, preventing them from achieving a flat, perpendicular base against you.
    8. If they secure a "collar grip", use your "sleeve grips" to control their arms and prevent them from pulling you into them.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "stiff-arming" to create frames: This places excessive hyperextension stress on the elbow joint's ligaments, risking sprains or tears. The correct movement is to maintain a slight elbow bend and use hip and body movement to generate force.
    • "Squeezing knees together" in closed guard when pressure is applied: This can lead to knee valgus collapse, stressing the MCL and ACL, especially if the opponent drives forward or attempts a "stack pass". Keep knees slightly apart, maintaining hip flexibility.
    • "Flailing arms" in panic: This leaves your shoulders vulnerable to "armbar" or "kimura" attacks and can cause rotator cuff impingement or tears from uncontrolled, rapid movements. Focus on controlled frame creation and hip movement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo "frame and shrimp" drill: Practice setting forearm frames and shrimping hips away from an imaginary opponent for 30 seconds, 10 reps.
    2. Partner drill: Opponent applies light pressure (25% resistance). Practice establishing frames and shrimping, focusing on re-establishing guard (e.g., closed guard or half guard). 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill: Opponent increases pressure to 50%. Focus on maintaining frames and preventing the opponent from flattening you out. Aim for 5 successful guard re-establishments per minute.
    4. "Guard retention flow" drill: Opponent attempts basic passes (e.g., standing pass, knee-slide). You focus solely on retaining guard using frames and hip movement. 5 minutes, 75% resistance.
    5. "Controlled sparring" (rolling): Focus entirely on guard retention. If guard is passed, reset. 5 minutes, 90% resistance.
    6. Live sparring: Apply guard retention techniques as part of your overall game. 6 minutes, 100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is actively driving forward to pass your guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • Opponent has established a strong grip and is attempting to break your guard open or flatten you.
    • You feel your hips are being pressured and your base is compromised, risking a guard pass.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Forearm Frames and Hip Shrimp: Place forearms between your hips and opponent's torso, bend elbows, then explosively push your hips away while extending legs. This creates immediate space to re-establish guard.
    • Guard Pull and Re-Grip: If opponent is close, perform a "guard pull" to establish "closed guard" or "half guard", immediately securing "sleeve grips" to control their posture and prevent them from driving forward.
    • "Knee Shield" Establishment: If opponent is driving past your legs, use one knee as a "knee shield" by placing it between your hips and their torso, while the other leg hooks behind their knee or hip to create a strong barrier.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to keep my opponent away in BJJ guard retention?

    Wrist pain often occurs from "stiff-arming" or pushing directly with an extended wrist. Instead, create "forearm frames" by bending your elbows 90 degrees and pushing with the entire forearm and bicep. This distributes the force across a larger muscle group and protects the delicate wrist joint. Always maintain a slight bend in your elbows when framing.

    Q: How do I maintain guard retention against a much bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and angles are critical. Focus on "hip movement" (shrimping) to create space and prevent them from flattening you out. Use "forearm frames" to control their posture and "push-pull" dynamics. Think about moving your hips to their side, creating a 45-degree angle to prevent them from generating full pressure.

    Q: When should I focus on guard retention instead of trying to sweep or submit?

    Guard retention is paraMount when your opponent has successfully initiated a "guard pass" and is actively threatening to advance their position. If you feel your guard is about to be passed, or they have broken your posture and are driving pressure, your immediate priority is to reset and re-establish a defensive guard. Submissions or sweeps are secondary to survival and positional control.
