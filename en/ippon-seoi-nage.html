This technique initiates from a standing clinch, aiming to throw your opponent over your shoulder. White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength instead of proper leverage.

The key is to create a strong, stable base and use your body's rotation to generate the throwing force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel fabric, fingers gripping firmly. Your left hand secures a sleeve grip at the opponent's wrist, thumb inside.
    2. Step your left foot forward, angling your body 45 degrees to your right, bringing your right hip close to the opponent's left hip.
    3. Drop your level by bending your knees, ensuring your base is wider than shoulder-width for stability.
    4. Initiate a pulling motion with your left arm on the sleeve, drawing the opponent forward and slightly off-balance.
    5. Simultaneously, rotate your hips and torso to the right, driving your right shoulder into the opponent's chest, just below their collarbone.
    6. As your right shoulder makes contact, slide your right arm under their armpit, securing a grip on their far side collar or gi.
    7. Drive forward and upward with your right shoulder while continuing to pull with your left hand on the sleeve, using your hips to lift and rotate.
    8. Extend your legs and pivot on your left foot, completing the rotation to drive the opponent forward and over your right shoulder, landing them on their back.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Rushing the entry: Incorrectly stepping too far forward or not dropping your level can lead to your knee ligaments (ACL/MCL) being hyperextended or torn if the opponent resists or shifts weight unexpectedly.
    • Gripping too high on the sleeve: A grip too close to the elbow reduces your control and leverage, forcing you to pull with your arms instead of rotating your body, potentially straining your shoulder capsule.
    • Not committing the shoulder: Failing to drive your right shoulder firmly into the opponent's chest means you are not using their body as a lever, leading to ineffective throws and potential back strain from trying to lift without proper support.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the footwork, hip rotation, and shoulder entry without a partner. Focus on a wide, stable base and controlled movement. (10 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a cooperative partner, practice the entire sequence of grips, entries, and rotation. Partner remains passive. (10 reps, 0% resistance).
    3. Light resistance drill: Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the weight transfer and rotation. Focus on maintaining balance. (10 reps, 25% resistance).
    4. Controlled resistance drill: Partner actively tries to resist by pushing back or shifting weight slightly. You must adjust your entry and rotation to overcome this. (15 reps, 50% resistance).
    5. Dynamic resistance drill: Partner attempts to defend the throw by posturing up or stepping away. You must adapt your timing and commitment. (20 reps, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the technique during live rolling, but with the understanding that it may not be successful. Focus on applying the mechanics learned. (5 attempts per round, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is posturing up from your guard, creating a gap to enter.
    • During a standing clinch when the opponent leans forward, offering a shoulder.
    • If the opponent attempts a grip fight where you can isolate a sleeve and collar.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posture up and step back: If the opponent attempts to enter, immediately straighten your posture and take a large step backward, breaking the connection and grip.
    • Grip break and sprawl: As the opponent drives their shoulder in, use your free hand to break their collar grip and sprawl your hips back, creating distance.
    • Underhook and resist rotation: If the opponent gets a shoulder in, immediately secure an underhook with your opposite arm and resist their rotational pull by driving your hips forward.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try Ippon Seoi Nage?

    Shoulder pain often occurs when you're not properly driving your shoulder into the opponent's chest and instead try to 'hook' their arm. This forces your shoulder joint to bear the entire weight of the throw. Ensure your right shoulder makes firm contact and acts as a pivot point, driving through their body, not just around it.

    Q: How can I Ippon Seoi Nage against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on dropping your level significantly more than they expect, creating a wider, more stable base. Use your hips to drive into their center of gravity, and ensure your collar grip is deep and secure to control their head and upper body. The rotation and hip drive are more important than raw strength.

    Q: Ippon Seoi Nage is primarily a standing technique and is generally not attempted from open guard. The mechanics require a stable, upright base and the opponent's engagement in a clinch. Trying it from open guard would require a significant stand-up first, making it inefficient and exposing you to other attacks.
