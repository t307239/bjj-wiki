Mastering the Scissor Sweep in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Scissor Sweep is a fundamental sweep initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to invert your opponent's base and transition to a superior position. It's a cornerstone for controlling the match.

White belts often struggle with the Scissor Sweep due to a lack of precise body mechanics and reliance on brute strength instead of leverage. This leads to wasted energy and failed attempts.

The key mechanical insight is using your legs as levers to create rotational force, combined with precise hip elevation and weight transfer to off-balance your opponent.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish closed guard with both feet locked around the opponent's hips. Secure a double sleeve grip on your opponent's uniform, pulling their arms slightly forward and down.
    2. Place your right foot on the opponent's left hip crease, using it as a pivot point. Your left foot remains on the opponent's right hip crease.
    3. Simultaneously, elevate your hips off the mat by contracting your glutes and lower back, creating space. Your body should form an L-shape with your torso and legs.
    4. Begin to swing your right leg outwards and upwards, creating a sweeping arc. This leg acts as the primary lever.
    5. As your right leg sweeps, drive your left foot into the opponent's right hip to create opposition. This leg acts as the secondary pivot and brace.
    6. Simultaneously, pull down and towards you with your sleeve grips, driving your opponent's upper body down and to their left.
    7. As the opponent begins to fall to their left, release your right foot from their hip and pivot your hips to face the direction of their fall. Bring your right knee to the mat.
    8. Immediately transition your hips to their side and establish a knee-on-belly or side control position, maintaining control of their arms.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your legs without hip elevation can strain your knee ligaments. The collateral ligaments (MCL/LCL) are at risk when the knee is hyperextended sideways. The correct movement involves controlled leg extension driven by hip elevation.
    • Trying to 'push' your opponent over with your feet without proper hip rotation can cause shoulder impingement. Forceful outward pushing can jam the glenohumeral joint. Instead, use your feet as levers and your hips to create rotation.
    • Releasing grips too early or too late disrupts the timing and can lead to your opponent regaining balance or escaping. Premature release means no leverage; late release can cause over-rotation of your own body. Maintain consistent pulling pressure until the sweep is complete.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the leg movement and hip elevation without a partner. Focus on the arc of the sweeping leg and the hip lift. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the grips, hip elevation, and leg sweep motion. Partner remains static. (15 reps)
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to your grips and hip elevation. Focus on maintaining your frame. (20 reps)
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively defends by trying to post their feet or square up. Focus on timing your sweep with their weight shifts. (25 reps)
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the sweep, but allows the sweep to be completed occasionally. Focus on adapting to their defense. (30 reps)
    6. Live rolling (90% effort): Attempt the Scissor Sweep in a rolling situation, with your partner defending realistically. Focus on execution and follow-up position. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up and their weight is forward, creating an opportunity to break their base.
    • When your opponent tries to pass your guard and their weight is distributed unevenly, making them vulnerable to a sweep.
    • When your opponent is leaning heavily on one side, creating an imbalance that the sweep can exploit.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Opponent posts their foot: If the opponent posts their free foot (the one not being swept) on your hip, maintain your sleeve grips and use your hips to drive that posted foot further away, preventing them from creating a stable base.
    • Opponent squares up: If the opponent manages to square their hips and base out, maintain your closed guard and immediately transition to a different sweep or submission attempt, like a hip bump sweep or an armbar.
    • Opponent stacks you: If the opponent attempts to stack you by driving their weight down, release your grips and immediately shrimp your hips out to create space and recover your guard or transition to a sweep from open guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Scissor Sweep?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are relying on extending your legs to push your opponent over, rather than using your hips to generate the sweeping motion. This hyperextends your knee, stressing the ligaments. Focus on elevating your hips first, then using your legs as levers to rotate your opponent, keeping your knee in a more stable, slightly bent position throughout the movement.

    Q: How do I Scissor Sweep against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are even more critical. Focus on breaking their posture down by pulling their sleeves towards your chest, creating an angle. Elevate your hips significantly to create space, then use a sharp, decisive leg sweep with a strong hip rotation. Do not try to overpower them; instead, use their weight against them by timing the sweep when they are slightly off-balance.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Scissor Sweep in BJJ?

    The optimal time to attempt the Scissor Sweep is when your opponent is posturing up and leaning forward, or when they are actively trying to pass your guard and their weight is shifted. Look for moments of imbalance. If they are too low and flat, or too upright and stable, the sweep will be much harder to execute effectively. Patience and observation are key.
