This sweep originates from Closed Guard, aiming to unbalance your opponent and transition to a superior position. It's a fundamental technique for escaping bottom.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage and timing, relying on brute force instead of coordinated body movement. This leads to wasted energy and ineffective attempts.

The key insight is using your legs as levers, creating a fulcrum with your hips, and generating momentum through hip extension and shoulder pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong closed guard grip: Grip opponent's left collar with your right hand and their right sleeve with your left hand. Keep your hips tight to their torso.
    2. Create space by shrimping: Extend your hips away from your opponent while maintaining your grips, creating a small gap.
    3. Insert your right leg: Swiftly bring your right knee across your opponent's chest, aiming for their left shoulder blade area.
    4. Secure your left leg's position: Place the sole of your left foot on the mat, roughly shoulder-width apart from your right foot.
    5. Initiate the sweep motion: Arch your lower back, lift your hips off the mat, and simultaneously drive your right knee forward and across.
    6. Apply downward pressure with your right arm: As you sweep, use your right hand on their collar to pull their head and upper body downwards and towards your right.
    7. Rotate your hips and bring your right leg over: Continue the hip rotation, allowing your right leg to pivot over their shoulder as you drive your hips up and to the right.
    8. Follow through with hip extension: As their weight shifts, extend your hips fully to elevate their base and complete the sweep.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect leg extension: Extending your right leg too straight and forcefully can hyperextend your knee, potentially tearing the ACL or PCL. The correct movement involves a controlled drive and pivot, not a straight leg push.
    • Neck strain from pulling: Pulling too hard on the collar with your right arm without coordinating hip movement can strain your neck or shoulder, or injure your opponent's cervical spine. Focus on pulling the head *down* as your hips rise and rotate.
    • Hip stress from improper framing: Attempting to lift your hips by simply arching your back without proper leg leverage can lead to lower back strain. Ensure your left foot is firmly planted to create a stable base for hip elevation.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0%): Practice the entire sequence of leg movements and hip escapes without a partner, focusing on the feel of each step. (20 reps)
    2. Static partner drill (25%): Partner lies on their back. You are in closed guard. Practice grip acquisition, shrimping, and leg insertion. Partner offers no resistance. (15 reps)
    3. Controlled sweep attempt (50%): Partner lies on their back. You perform the sweep. Partner offers slight resistance by keeping their base stable. (10 reps per side)
    4. Assisted sweep (75%): Partner starts in closed guard. You initiate the sweep. Partner offers moderate resistance, allowing you to feel the leverage points. (10 reps per side)
    5. Limited resistance sweep (90%): Partner actively defends but allows the sweep to be completed. Focus on applying the mechanics against active, but not overwhelming, defense. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Attempt the scissor sweep during rolling. Focus on timing and adapting the mechanics to your opponent's reactions. (5 attempts during roll)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is postured up high in your closed guard, creating a gap between your hips and theirs.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you, as this often leaves an arm available for a collar grip.
    • When your opponent is leaning heavily forward, making it difficult for them to maintain balance.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape and Base: If the sweep is initiated, immediately hip escape to your right and widen your base by placing your right foot on the mat. This disrupts the lever action.
    • Grip Break and Posture: If your opponent has a strong collar grip, focus on breaking that grip with both hands and regaining your posture. This negates the pulling force.
    • Leg Posting: If the sweeping leg comes across, the defender can post their foot on the sweeping leg's thigh to prevent the rotation and maintain balance.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Scissor Sweep?

    Knee pain usually occurs from hyperextension or twisting forces. If you are pushing with a straight leg or your hip rotation is not coordinated with your leg movement, your knee joint can be stressed. Focus on a controlled 'driving' motion with your sweeping knee, aiming it across their body, and ensure your hips are actively lifting and rotating *with* the leg motion, not just pushing.

    Q: How can I Scissor Sweep against a much bigger and heavier opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. You must create a significant gap with your shrimping and ensure your collar grip is secure to pull their upper body down. Focus on generating momentum by driving your hips upwards and rotating them forcefully, using your body weight as much as possible to initiate the imbalance. Don't rely on pushing their weight; instead, pull their structure down and over.

    Q: When should I NOT attempt the Scissor Sweep in BJJ?

    Avoid attempting the Scissor Sweep when your opponent has successfully broken your closed guard and is postured very low with their weight on your hips, or when they have established a strong cross-face and are driving their weight into you. These positions make it very difficult to generate the necessary leverage and space, and attempting it can lead to you being easily passed or put in a worse position.
