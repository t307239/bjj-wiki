The Scissor Sweep is a fundamental sweep initiated from the Closed Guard, aiming to invert the opponent's base and sweep them to the mat.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage and proper weight distribution, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The core principle is to create a strong fulcrum with your hips and use your legs as levers to disrupt their balance and generate upward momentum.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish 'Double Sleeve Grip' on the opponent's gi sleeves, ensuring your knuckles face outwards.
    2. Maintain a tight 'Closed Guard' by hooking your feet around their hips, shins touching your own heels.
    3. Tilt your hips upwards and to your left, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and the opponent's body.
    4. Simultaneously, drive your right knee towards the opponent's left armpit, creating a wedge.
    5. With your left leg, extend it horizontally across the opponent's hips, acting as a pivot point.
    6. Pull down sharply on the right sleeve grip while simultaneously pushing up with your hips and driving your right knee.
    7. As the opponent's base falters, scissor your legs, driving your left heel towards your right buttock to complete the sweep, bringing them to your right side.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to sweep by simply pushing the opponent's shoulders with your hands: This can strain your shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) by forcing it into hyperextension or dislocation if the opponent resists strongly. The correct action is to use your legs and hips for leverage, not just upper body pushing.
    • Sweeping with straight legs and no hip elevation: This can lead to hyperextension of the knee joint (tibiofemoral joint) and damage to the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) if the opponent falls awkwardly or resists with forward pressure. Instead, keep knees slightly bent and focus on hip elevation and torso rotation.
    • Gripping too loosely on the sleeves or without proper wrist control: This allows the opponent to easily break your grip, rendering the sweep ineffective and potentially leading to a wrist sprain (radiocarpal joint) if they yank suddenly. Always maintain a firm, controlled grip with your thumbs wrapped around their sleeves.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip tilt, leg extension, and scissor motion without a partner. Focus on the sequence and feel of the movement. (10 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (passive): Your partner lies on their back in your closed guard. Practice establishing grips, hip tilt, and leg placement. They offer no resistance. (10 reps, 10% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (controlled sweep): Your partner in closed guard allows you to set up the sweep but offers minimal, controlled resistance to the sweep action. Focus on executing the full motion. (10 reps, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (timing): Your partner in closed guard attempts to posture up slightly. You react by initiating the sweep. Focus on the timing of your hip drive and leg scissor. (10 reps, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (limited): Attempt the scissor sweep during live rolling, but with the understanding that you can reset if the sweep is not effective. Focus on identifying opportunities. (5 attempts, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (full intent): Apply the scissor sweep with full intent during live rolling, aiming to complete the sweep and transition to a dominant position. (10 attempts, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Opponent's base is too high, making it difficult to control them from the bottom of closed guard.
    • Opponent is postured up, creating space to insert your knee and hips.
    • Opponent is leaning heavily forward, making their weight easily transferable.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • 'Stack Pass' Defense: If the opponent attempts to stack you, widen your base by extending your legs slightly and driving your hips into their chest, preventing them from collapsing your guard. Maintain your sleeve grips and prepare to transition to another sweep or guard recovery.
    • 'Knee Slice Pass' Setup: If you feel the opponent preparing to pass your guard, use the scissor motion to create space and then immediately transition to a 'De La Riva Guard' or 'Spider Guard' by placing your foot on their hip and extending your leg.
    • 'Hip Bump Sweep' Counter: If the opponent is too low and trying to pressure into you, the scissor sweep is less effective. In this scenario, consider switching to a 'Hip Bump Sweep' by creating an angle with your hips and bridging forcefully to their side.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Scissor Sweep from closed guard?

    Lower back pain often occurs when you try to generate all the sweep's power from your spine rather than your hips. Ensure your hips are actively tilting upwards and away from your opponent, creating a fulcrum. Also, make sure your knees are bent and not locked out straight, which puts excessive strain on your lumbar spine.

    Q: How can I do the Scissor Sweep effectively against a much bigger and heavier opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a stronger fulcrum with your hips and using your legs as precise levers rather than brute force. Ensure your 'Closed Guard' is extremely tight, preventing them from posturing. Prioritize controlling their posture with your sleeve grips and driving your knee into their armpit to break their base before initiating the scissor motion.

    Q: The optimal time is when the opponent is postured up or leaning forward, making their base unstable. You don't always 'pull' their arms; rather, you use your sleeve grips to direct their fall and control their balance as you execute the sweep. The downward pull on the sleeve, combined with your hip drive and leg scissor, creates the imbalance needed.
