The Biomechanically Sound Scissor Sweep for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This sweep originates from Closed Guard and aims to invert your opponent's base, allowing you to come up to a dominant position.

Many white belts fail because they rely on brute strength and lack precise control of angles and weight distribution.

The key mechanical insight is using your legs as levers to create off-balancing, not just to hold.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish strong Collar Grips: Both hands grip the opponent's collar, approximately at the opponent's sternoclavicular joints (where collarbone meets sternum).
    2. Secure Sleeve Grips: Simultaneously, secure one Wrist Grip on the opponent's sleeve on the same side as your dominant leg (e.g., right sleeve with right leg).
    3. Hip Angle: Scoot your hips to the side, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's center line. Your hips should be slightly elevated off the mat.
    4. Leg Placement: Bring your right knee across the opponent's chest, aiming to place it near their left shoulder. Your left foot hooks behind your opponent's right thigh.
    5. Initiate the Pull: Using your collar grips, pull your opponent's upper body towards you, driving their weight onto their base.
    6. Sweep Action: Extend your right leg forcefully in the direction of your opponent's head while simultaneously driving your left hip into their right thigh.
    7. Weight Transfer: As your right leg extends, your left leg drives their thigh away, causing their weight to shift and their base to collapse.
    8. Come Up: As they fall, immediately bring your right knee to the mat, framing their hip, and bring your left leg to the inside of their leg to establish a dominant top position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect leg extension: Violently extending the sweeping leg straight up can hyperextend the knee, damaging the ACL and PCL ligaments. The correct movement is a sweeping motion, not a direct push.
    • Neck strain from pulling: Gripping too high on the collar and pulling directly backward can strain the cervical spine and trapezius muscles. The pull should be controlled and angled towards your body.
    • Hip impaction injury: Driving your hip directly into the opponent's thigh with excessive force can cause bruising or joint pain in your own hip. Focus on creating a lifting and pushing motion with the hip, not a direct impact.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Scoot and Leg Extension: Practice scooting hips to 45 degrees and extending one leg, then switching. 10 reps per side.
    2. Partner Assisted Sweep (No Resistance): Partner stands, you perform the sweep motion from guard, partner gently falls. 5 reps per side.
    3. Controlled Partner Sweep (25% Resistance): Partner maintains a light base. Execute the sweep, focusing on grip and hip angle. 5 reps per side.
    4. Controlled Partner Sweep (50% Resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to the pull and sweep. Focus on timing and body mechanics. 5 reps per side.
    5. Controlled Partner Sweep (75% Resistance): Partner actively tries to defend but allows the sweep to be completed. Focus on maintaining control. 3 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (90% Resistance): Attempt the sweep in a rolling scenario with a partner who understands you are practicing. Focus on clean execution. 3 attempts per round.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is postured up with their weight forward, creating a stable base.
    • When your opponent is attempting a guard pass, and you can catch them off balance.
    • When you have secured strong collar and sleeve grips with good control of their posture.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass Defense: If the opponent stacks heavily, drive your hips back to create space and bring your knees up to defend. Your right knee should drive forward to create a frame.
    • Leg Drag Defense: If the opponent attempts to drag your leg across, maintain a strong hook with your left foot behind their thigh and use your right foot to push their hip away.
    • Kneeling Base Recovery: If the sweep is failing, immediately bring your knees back to the mat to re-establish your guard and prevent them from passing.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Scissor Sweep?

    Your knee pain likely stems from hyperextension. Instead of a direct push, think of your sweeping leg (e.g., right leg) as a lever that pivots your opponent's body. Extend it in a sweeping arc towards their head, not straight up. Ensure your hips are angled correctly to facilitate this rotational movement.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Focus on breaking their posture with your collar grips before initiating the sweep. Use your hips to create a sharp angle, and drive your sweeping leg towards their head with maximum velocity. A strong hook with your other leg behind their thigh is crucial to prevent them from easily recovering their base.

    Q: I keep getting my guard passed after attempting the Scissor Sweep. What's wrong?

    This usually means you are not completing the sweep or transitioning quickly enough. After initiating the sweep, you must immediately drive your hips forward and up, bringing your knee to the mat to establish a dominant top position. Do not linger in the sweep motion; transition directly to a Guard Pass or control position.
