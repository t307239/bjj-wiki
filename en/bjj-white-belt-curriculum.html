    Frequently Asked Questions

    How long does it take to learn White Belt Curriculum?

    Most practitioners develop functional competency with White Belt Curriculum within 3–6 months of consistent drilling. Mastery — the ability to execute reliably in live rolling against resisting opponents — typically takes 1–2 years.

    Is White Belt Curriculum effective for beginners?

    Yes. White Belt Curriculum is part of the core BJJ curriculum and taught at all belt levels. Beginners should focus on the fundamental mechanics and concepts before refining advanced entries.

    How often should I drill White Belt Curriculum?

    3–5 times per week is ideal for rapid skill acquisition. Even 10 focused repetitions per session compounds over time — consistency matters more than volume.

    What positions connect to White Belt Curriculum?

    BJJ is a linked system. White Belt Curriculum flows naturally to and from related positions. Study transitions in both directions to build a complete positional game.

    More Questions

    What are the most important things to focus on as a white belt?

    As a white belt, prioritize understanding fundamental concepts like base, posture, and maintaining control. Focus on learning the basic positions and the transitions between them, rather than complex submissions.

    How do I avoid getting submitted as a beginner?

    The best way to avoid being submitted is to learn how to defend properly and maintain good positional control. Always think about your opponent's goals and how to prevent them from achieving dominant positions.

    What are the essential techniques every white belt should know?

    Every white belt should learn basic escapes from common bad positions like side control and mount, as well as fundamental sweeps from the guard. Understanding how to pass the guard is also crucial for progression.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I'm trying to learn the basic BJJ guard retention techniques like the shrimping and bridging from the White Belt Curriculum?

    Neck pain often stems from using your neck muscles to push off the mat or your opponent. Instead, focus on driving through your hips and shoulders. For shrimping, initiate the movement by contracting your glutes and driving your hips away, keeping your neck neutral. For bridging, explosively extend your hips upward by tightening your core and glutes, avoiding any cervical hyperextension.

    Q: How can I effectively defend against a much larger opponent when they are trying to pass my guard using the fundamental techniques taught in the White Belt Curriculum?

    Against a larger opponent, prioritize maintaining a strong base and creating frames with your forearms. Keep your hips active to prevent them from flattening you out, using the shrimp motion to create space and re-establish distance. Your goal is to use your opponent's weight against them by constantly shifting your hips and using your legs to control their base, rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: When I'm practicing the basic mount escape techniques from the White Belt Curriculum, why do I feel like my opponent can easily keep me stuck and how can I improve my hip movement?

    Feeling stuck on mount often means your hips are too flat and your shoulder is glued to the mat, preventing hip escape. To improve, focus on actively 'gluing' your shoulder to your opponent's hip and driving your hips *away* from that shoulder, creating a wedge. Then, use your free leg to hook their leg and drive your hips back towards your opponent's body to create the space needed to turn.
