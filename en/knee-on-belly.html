Knee on Belly: Your Essential BJJ Position Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant control position, typically achieved after a successful Guard Pass, aiming to pin the opponent's torso. It allows for transitions to submissions or further positional advancement.

White belts often struggle by placing their knee too high or too low, or by neglecting their base, leading to loss of control and potential injury. The key is understanding weight distribution and frame.

The single most important mechanical principle is creating a stable base with your supporting leg while driving your knee and hip into the opponent's solar plexus, using your chest and head for further pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a side-control position, establish a strong cross-face grip on the opponent's collar with your right hand, and a grip on their left sleeve with your left hand.
    2. Drive your right knee (the one closer to their head) forward, aiming to place it directly on their solar plexus, approximately 4-6 inches below their sternum.
    3. Simultaneously, extend your left leg backwards, placing your left foot flat on the mat, creating a wide, stable base.
    4. Your right hip should be low, driving down into the opponent's torso, creating a tight connection.
    5. Your chest should be pressed firmly against the opponent's upper chest, maintaining head pressure by tucking your chin slightly.
    6. Your weight should be distributed primarily onto your right knee and hip, with your left leg acting as a strong stabilizer.
    7. Maintain your grips: the cross-face grip prevents them from turning into you, and the sleeve grip controls their posture.
    8. Ensure your right knee is perpendicular to the opponent's spine and your left foot is aligned with your left hip, forming a stable tripod.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing your knee directly on the opponent's hip bone: This can cause direct pressure and potential fracture of the iliac crest or pubic bone, and offers no control. Instead, aim your knee towards their solar plexus.
    • Extending your supporting leg too far back or not far enough: If too far back, you lose connection and base; if too close, you become unstable. Keep your supporting leg at approximately a 45-degree angle from your torso, with your foot flat.
    • Allowing your chest to lift off the opponent: This creates space for them to bridge or shrimp. Keep your chest glued to their upper torso and drive your hips down.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side control to Knee on Belly, focusing on the exact placement of knee and foot, and hip drive. (50 reps each side, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Have your partner lie flat. Practice achieving Knee on Belly, holding for 5 seconds, and returning to side control. (25 reps each side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drilling (light control): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to focus on maintaining pressure and base. (25 reps each side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drilling (controlled movement): Practice transitioning Knee on Belly to other positions (e.g., mount) while your partner offers light defense. (25 reps each side, 50% resistance)
    5. Flow rolling (controlled): Apply Knee on Belly during flow rolling, focusing on maintaining the position and making transitions. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Utilize Knee on Belly as a primary control position during live rolling, aiming to hold it for extended periods and transition to submissions. (3 rounds, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Immediately after successfully passing the opponent's guard and establishing side control.
    • When the opponent attempts to shrimp or bridge, and you can quickly shift your weight and knee.
    • As a transition from a failed submission attempt from side control, to re-establish dominance.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimping: If the opponent attempts to shrimp, maintain your cross-face grip and drive your knee deeper. As they create space, use your left leg to hook their hip and push them away, then re-establish.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly?

    Knee pain in Knee on Belly often stems from incorrect weight distribution. If you're placing too much direct pressure on your kneecap or driving your knee into a hard surface like the opponent's hip bone, it can cause discomfort. Ensure your weight is primarily on your shin and the side of your knee, and that your knee is positioned on the softer part of their torso, like the solar plexus, not directly on their bones.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a strong base and driving your hips down to break their posture. Use your cross-face grip to control their head and prevent them from posturing up, and your sleeve grip to pull them towards your knee. Your supporting leg needs to be planted firmly, providing a solid anchor for your weight.

    Q: When should I switch my knees in Knee on Belly?

    Switching knees, often called 'switching hips,' is a dynamic transition. You'd typically switch when the opponent starts to defend by turning into the side you're on, or when you want to set up a submission from the opposite side. To switch, bring your supporting leg forward and step your other leg back, always maintaining a strong base and control of their upper body.
