Knee on Belly: The Foundational Position in BJJ

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant control position achieved after a successful Guard Pass, aiming to pin your opponent's torso and restrict their movement.

White belts often struggle by placing too much weight on their knee or failing to establish a strong base, leading to instability and missed opportunities.

The key insight is to create a stable tripod of support using your shin, knee, and foot, distributing weight through your hips, not just your knee.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a side-control position, secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left arm) with your left hand.
    2. Drive your right shin across your opponent's sternum, angling your hips towards their head.
    3. Place your right knee firmly on the mat, directly beneath your right hip.
    4. Shift your weight forward, driving your right hip down towards your opponent's torso.
    5. Extend your left leg back, placing your left foot flat on the mat for a stable base, wider than shoulder-width.
    6. Keep your chest close to your opponent's upper body, creating upper body pressure.
    7. Maintain a slight bend in your right knee, acting as a pivot point, not a pressure point.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing your knee directly on the opponent's hip bone and pressing down: This can cause severe hyperextension of the knee joint, leading to ACL or MCL tears. Correct: Place your knee on the sternum/upper torso and drive weight through your hip.
    • Leaning back and placing weight on your foot: This compromises your base and allows the opponent to easily shrimp or bridge. Correct: Maintain forward pressure with your hips and a wide base with your back leg.
    • Lifting your hips too high off the opponent: This creates space for them to recover guard or escape. Correct: Keep your hips low and connected to their torso, driving weight down.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side control to Knee on Belly, focusing on hip angle and knee placement (10 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Achieve side control, then transition to Knee on Belly, holding for 5 seconds, then returning to side control (10 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): From side control, your partner offers minimal resistance as you transition to Knee on Belly. Focus on maintaining control (10 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (controlled movement): Transition to Knee on Belly, and your partner attempts to create slight discomfort without escaping. You practice maintaining the position and adjusting pressure (10 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Scenario drilling: Start from a simulated guard pass completion, immediately transitioning to Knee on Belly. Your partner attempts to defend (5 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Apply Knee on Belly as a transition during rolling, focusing on maintaining the position and transitioning to other dominant positions (5-minute rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Immediately after successfully passing the opponent's guard.
    • When the opponent attempts to bridge or shrimp, and you can adjust your hips to land on their torso.
    • When you have established a strong side-control position and see an opening to shift weight forward.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimp and recover guard: The opponent shrimpes their hips away to create space and bring their knee between you, aiming to re-establish guard.
    • Bridge and roll: The opponent drives their hips into you and bridges, attempting to roll you over or create an escape angle.
    • Post foot and spin: The opponent posts their free foot on your hip and spins away, creating space to stand or recover guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly in BJJ?

    Your knee likely hurts because you're placing direct pressure on the kneecap or the joint itself, especially if you have a bony hip. This is biomechanically unsound. Instead, aim to place your knee on the opponent's sternum or upper rib cage and drive your weight through your hip, using your shin as the anchor point. This distributes the force across your tibia and your opponent's torso, not directly onto your knee joint.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise weight distribution are critical. Focus on driving your hips down low and staying tight to their upper body, preventing them from creating space. Use your cross-collar grip to control their head and shoulder, and your free hand on their hip or far side to maintain base. Ensure your supporting foot is wide and firmly planted to resist their bridging attempts.

    Q: When should I transition from Knee on Belly to something else in BJJ?

    You should transition from Knee on Belly when you feel the opponent is successfully defending your pressure or creating an escape opportunity. If they are consistently bridging or shrimping effectively, it's time to consider transitioning to a different position like mount, or using the momentum to secure a submission like an Armbar. Don't linger in a position where your control is compromised.
