Knee on Belly: Your Essential BJJ Position Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant control position achieved from side-control or Mount, aiming to pin the opponent's torso and set up submissions or sweeps.

White belts often struggle by placing too much weight on their own knee or losing balance, leading to ineffective pressure and potential injury.

The key is to transfer your weight onto your opponent's sternum, creating a strong fulcrum with your shin and hip.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start from side-control, with your chest connected to your opponent's side, your hips low and your knee close to their hip.
    2. Drive your knee closest to their head forward, aiming to place it just below their sternum, perpendicular to their body.
    3. As your knee lands, immediately tuck your trailing foot back, creating a stable tripod base with your foot and the knee on belly.
    4. Simultaneously, lower your hip towards the mat, ensuring your weight is distributed over your opponent's sternum, not your own knee.
    5. Your supporting knee should be positioned on the mat, slightly behind your opponent's hip, providing a stable base.
    6. Maintain a strong frame with your arms: one hand controlling their far arm (e.g., gable grip on their bicep) and the other controlling their near arm (e.g., collar grip or wrist grip).
    7. Keep your chest pressed into their sternum, driving your weight downwards and slightly forward, creating a pinning effect.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing the knee directly onto the opponent's hip bone: This can cause direct pressure and potential bruising or knee cap injury to your opponent, and it shifts your weight off their center of mass, compromising control.
    • Letting the knee on belly knee drift too far forward or sideways: This compromises your base and can lead to your knee getting caught in a sweep or submission attempt, potentially hyperextending your knee joint.
    • Supporting weight on your own knee instead of your foot and hip: This creates an unstable base, limits your ability to apply downward pressure, and puts excessive strain on your knee joint, risking ligamentous injury.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice transitioning from side-control to knee on belly, focusing on hip and weight transfer. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo drill: Hold knee on belly, focusing on maintaining pressure and balance. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill: Start in side-control, move to knee on belly, hold for 5 seconds, then return to side-control. (10 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill: Transition to knee on belly, apply light pressure, partner resists passively. (10 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Partner drill: Practice transitioning and holding knee on belly, allowing partner to attempt basic escapes. (10 reps, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate knee on belly as a positional goal during rolling. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is turtled or trying to bridge out of side-control.
    • When you have established a dominant side-control position with good hip connection.
    • As a transitional position to move from side-control to mount or to set up a submission.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Framing: The opponent hips out and creates space with their arms, framing against your chest to prevent weight transfer.
    • Turtle/Bridge: The opponent tucks their chin and bridges their hips, creating an angle to escape or sweep.
    • Guard Recovery: The opponent uses their legs to re-establish their guard by bringing their knees to their chest and creating a frame.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly in BJJ?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are placing too much direct pressure on your own knee joint, especially if you're not keeping your shin perpendicular to your opponent's body. Ensure your weight is primarily supported by your foot and your hip, not solely by your bent knee. Try to keep your shin bone vertical and your hip low, driving forward into your opponent's sternum.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on leverage and weight distribution rather than brute strength. Ensure your hip is very low, almost touching the mat, and drive your weight directly into their sternum. Use your arms to maintain frames on their hips and shoulders to prevent them from bridging or shrimping effectively. Your trailing foot should be planted firmly for stability.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to Knee on Belly from Side Control?

    The optimal time to transition to Knee on Belly from Side Control is when you feel your opponent attempting to create space or bridge. If they are pushing into you, it's a good cue to shift your weight forward and drive your knee into their torso. This transition can often catch them off guard and solidify your control before they can fully execute their escape.
