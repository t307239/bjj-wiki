Knee on Belly: The Essential BJJ Position for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant positional control achieved after a successful Guard Pass, aiming to pin the opponent's torso while maintaining superior leverage.

White belts often fail by placing their weight incorrectly or allowing the opponent to bridge effectively, losing the control.

The key is to create a stable base with your hips low and your weight distributed over your opponent's sternum and opposite hip.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, secure a cross-face grip with your left hand on the opponent's right collar and your right hand on their right sleeve.
    2. Drive your right knee (the one closest to their head) towards their chest, aiming to place it just below their armpit.
    3. Simultaneously, extend your left leg back, creating a wide, stable base with your left foot flat on the mat, heel to toe alignment.
    4. Lower your hips until your left hip bone is directly above your opponent's sternum, creating a fulcrum.
    5. Shift your weight forward, driving your right knee into their chest and your left hip down onto their sternum.
    6. Ensure your right knee is perpendicular to your opponent's torso and your left foot is positioned for maximum base width, about shoulder-width apart.
    7. Maintain a tight cross-face grip and use your left hand to control their right sleeve, preventing them from framing or shrimping.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing your knee directly on the opponent's hip bone or thigh: This can lead to a knee-on-pelvis injury, specifically to the patellofemoral joint or collateral ligaments, due to direct pressure and potential twisting. Correct this by aiming your knee for the sternum/upper chest area.
    • Standing too upright with weight on your toes: This makes you unstable and vulnerable to sweeps, risking a fall that can cause ankle sprains or knee ligament damage. Correct this by lowering your hips and keeping your weight distributed over your base.
    • Allowing your opponent to bridge with their hips too close: This can trap your knee and lead to knee hyperextension or ligament tears if you cannot recover your base. Correct this by maintaining hip separation and using your left foot base to drive forward.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the weight transfer and hip lowering motion without a partner. Focus on hip angle and knee placement. (10 reps)
    2. Static partner drill: Partner lies on their back, you practice entering Knee on Belly and holding the position with no resistance. (20 reps each side)
    3. Light pressure drill: Partner offers minimal resistance to your entry and holding. Focus on maintaining control against slight pushes. (15 reps each side)
    4. Controlled entry drill: Partner starts from side control, you practice transitioning to Knee on Belly with light resistance. (20 reps)
    5. Positional sparring (limited): Start from Knee on Belly, opponent can only defend by shrimping or bridging. You aim to maintain control. (5 rounds, 1 minute each)
    6. Live rolling (focus on position): During sparring, actively seek to achieve Knee on Belly. Opponent can defend fully. (10 minutes, focus on achieving and maintaining control)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully passing the guard and establishing side control.
    • When your opponent attempts to shrimp away from side control, creating space to advance.
    • When you have secured a dominant grip that allows you to transition to a higher control position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): When the opponent attempts Knee on Belly, immediately drive your hips away from their knee, creating space to bring your guard back up.
    • Bridging: From Knee on Belly, if the opponent is not controlling your hips tightly, bridge your hips upwards to unbalance them and potentially escape or reverse the position.
    • Recovering Guard: If the opponent's knee is too far forward, use your hands to frame against their hip or knee and bring your legs back between you to re-establish guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly?

    Knee pain in Knee on Belly often stems from improper weight distribution or pressure on the kneecap. Ensure your knee is placed on the soft tissue of the torso, not directly on the hip bone or thigh. Lower your hips significantly so your weight is distributed over your opponent's sternum and your own base.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a wider base with your non-posting leg and keeping your hips extremely low. Use your cross-face and sleeve grips to prevent them from creating space. Drive your weight down through your hips and knee, using your body weight effectively.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Knee on Belly to another position?

    The optimal time to transition is when your opponent reacts defensively by turning into you or attempting to bridge. This reaction often creates an opening for you to advance to mount, take their back, or transition to a submission like an Armbar or kimura.
