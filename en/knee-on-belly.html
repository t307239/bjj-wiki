Knee on Belly: Your Essential BJJ Position Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant control position achieved after a successful Guard Pass, aiming to pin the opponent and set up submissions or further positional advancement.

Many white belts struggle because they treat it as a static pinning position rather than an active pressure point.

The key is to create a strong, stable base with your knee and foot while actively driving your hip into the opponent's torso.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a passed guard position, establish a strong cross-face grip with your left hand on the opponent's right collar, and your right hand on their right sleeve.
    2. Drive your right knee (the one closer to their head) down onto their sternum, aiming for the center of their chest.
    3. Place your right foot flat on the mat, directly behind your right knee, creating a stable base with your shin perpendicular to the mat.
    4. Your left leg should extend back, with your left foot planted firmly on the mat, providing balance and base width.
    5. Your hips should be angled towards their head, creating a "T" shape with your body and their torso.
    6. Transfer your weight forward through your right knee and the ball of your right foot, pressing your hip bone into their chest.
    7. Maintain head control by keeping your head up and driving forward, preventing them from bridging or turning into you.
    8. Keep your opponent's arms controlled with your grips; never let them frame effectively.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing your knee directly on the opponent's hip bone or ribs: This can cause sharp pain and potential rib fractures. Always aim for the sternum/upper torso.
    • Extending your supporting leg too far back: This compromises your base and allows the opponent to easily roll or shrimp away, risking knee ligament strain.
    • Allowing your hips to sag or drift away from the opponent: This relieves pressure and enables them to escape, potentially leading to shoulder impingement from uncontrolled movements.
    • Gripping too loosely on the collar or sleeve: This allows the opponent to create space and defend, increasing the risk of shoulder dislocations if they pull hard or you overextend.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip-to-knee-to-foot placement sequence. Focus on the body angle and weight transfer without a partner (50 reps).
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): From a passed guard, have a partner lie flat. Practice entering Knee on Belly, holding for 5 seconds, and releasing. Focus on correct base and hip pressure (20 reps each side).
    3. Partner drilling (light pressure): Partner offers minimal resistance to hip escapes. Focus on maintaining pressure and base as they try to move away (30 reps each side).
    4. Partner drilling (simulated escape): Partner actively tries to shrimp or bridge. You practice maintaining Knee on Belly by adjusting your base and hip pressure (40 reps each side).
    5. Flow rolling: Integrate Knee on Belly into positional sparring, aiming to achieve it after passes and hold for 10 seconds before transitioning or releasing (5 minutes).
    6. Live rolling: Focus on achieving and maintaining Knee on Belly during regular sparring rounds (2 rounds).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Immediately after successfully passing the opponent's guard.
    • When the opponent attempts to shrimp or bridge and you can transition to a pinning position.
    • When the opponent is flattened out and you have control of their upper body.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): As the knee comes down, the opponent uses their foot on the mat to push their hips away, creating space to bring their guard back.
    • Bridge and Roll: The opponent tightens their core, drives their feet into the mat, and explosively lifts their hips to roll you over.
    • Re-guarding: The opponent uses their legs to hook your legs and bring them back into their guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly in BJJ?

    Your knee might hurt if you are placing it directly on the opponent's hip bone or ribs, which are bony and sensitive. The correct technique involves placing your knee on their sternum or upper abdomen, a softer target that distributes pressure more effectively. Ensure your shin is perpendicular to the mat for optimal support and avoid twisting your knee inwards.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a strong, wide base with your supporting foot and leg extended. Drive your hips into their sternum with maximum forward pressure, using your weight and leverage rather than brute strength. Control their upper body with tight grips to prevent them from bridging effectively, and be ready to adjust your base if they try to turn into you.

    Q: Knee on Belly is most effective as a transition *from* Side Control rather than a direct move *to* it. If your opponent is actively trying to frame or bridge out of Side Control, you can use that momentum to drive your knee onto their chest and establish the Knee on Belly position, often forcing them to defend a new angle and relieving pressure from their frame.
