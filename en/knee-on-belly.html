Knee on Belly: The Fundamental BJJ Position for Beginners

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Knee on Belly is achieved after a successful Guard Pass, aiming to pin your opponent's torso with your knee and shin. It controls their hips and limits their movement, setting up submissions or further positional advancement.

White belts often struggle because they focus on pushing down with their hands, neglecting the core principle of hip pressure. This leads to instability and allows opponents to bridge or shrimp out.

The key insight is using your shin as a fulcrum, driving your hips forward and down, creating a stable base that pins their diaphragm and restricts their breathing.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a side-control position, transition your weight forward, placing your left knee (for example) onto the opponent's right abdominal area, perpendicular to their torso.
    2. Your right leg should be extended straight back, foot flat on the mat, providing a stable rear base.
    3. Your right hip should be directly over your opponent's left hip, creating a perpendicular line of pressure.
    4. Your left shin should be across the opponent's torso, with your left foot tucked towards your own right hip (not flailing).
    5. Your chest should be low, chest-to-chest with your opponent, maintaining a tight connection.
    6. Grip their right collar with your right hand and their right sleeve with your left hand, pulling them slightly towards you.
    7. Drive your hips forward and down, applying pressure through your left knee and shin into their abdomen, while keeping your rear leg planted.
    8. Ensure your left knee is angled slightly towards your opponent's head, not directly towards their feet, to maximize pressure and control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing your knee directly on the opponent's hip bone: This can cause severe hip joint impingement for both you and your opponent, leading to labral tears or fractures. Always aim for the soft tissue of the abdomen.
    • Arching your back excessively to create pressure: This strains your lumbar spine and makes you vulnerable to sweeps. Maintain a neutral spine and drive pressure from your hips.
    • Extending your shin directly forward, parallel to the opponent's body: This creates a weak base and allows easy shrimping. Angle your shin across the torso and tuck your foot.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice transitioning from side control to Knee on Belly, focusing on hip placement and knee angle. (50 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill: With a compliant partner, establish side control and practice moving into Knee on Belly, holding for 5 seconds. (20 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    3. Partner drill: Transition to Knee on Belly, hold for 10 seconds, and practice slight weight shifts. (20 reps each side, 30% resistance).
    4. Partner drill: Practice holding Knee on Belly for 15 seconds, allowing partner to resist slightly with bridge attempts. (15 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Partner drill: Transition to Knee on Belly, hold, and attempt to control opponent's bridging with controlled hip pressure. (10 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Focus on achieving and maintaining Knee on Belly for at least 10 seconds during positional sparring. (5 rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully passing your opponent's guard and establishing side control.
    • When your opponent attempts to shrimp away from side control and creates space.
    • When you want to create a dominant pinning position to set up submissions.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): If the opponent's knee is too far forward, the opponent can shrimp their hips back towards your knee, creating space to recompose guard.
    • Bridging: If the opponent's base is too wide or their weight is too far back, the opponent can bridge upwards, lifting your weight off them and potentially creating an escape.
    • Re-guarding: If the opponent is slow to transition or their weight distribution is off, the opponent can bring their legs back in to re-establish guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly in BJJ?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are directly applying pressure on the knee joint itself, or forcing it into an unnatural angle. The correct biomechanics involve placing the shin across the opponent's torso, using the knee as a pivot point, and driving pressure through your hips. Ensure your knee is bent at a comfortable angle and your shin is flat against their abdomen, not digging into your own joint.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your hip pressure and base are paraMount. Instead of relying on brute strength, focus on driving your hips forward and down, pinning their diaphragm. Keep your chest tight to their chest and use your grips to control their posture. Your rear leg's foot should be planted firmly, providing a solid anchor to prevent them from using their weight to simply push you off.

    Q: When is the best time to switch my Knee on Belly leg?

    The optimal time to switch your Knee on Belly leg is when your opponent attempts to bridge or escape towards the side you are currently pressuring. As they commit to their escape, you can use their momentum to pivot your hips and bring your other knee into position, maintaining continuous pressure and control. This transition should be fluid, using your hips to guide the movement rather than a sudden, jerky change.
