Knee on Belly is a dominant top position achieved after a successful Guard Pass, aiming to pin your opponent and set up submissions or sweeps.

Many white belts focus on simply placing their knee down, neglecting the crucial weight distribution and framing needed for effective control.

The key is to create a stable tripod using your shin, foot, and opposite knee, while driving your hips forward to off-balance your opponent.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a side-control-like position, hook your opponent's far armpit with your near-side arm, securing a "collar grip" on their gi lapel.
    2. Your near-side knee will drive forward towards your opponent's sternum, aiming slightly off-center to their shoulder.
    3. As your knee contacts their chest, your opposite leg (the one not yet on the ground) will extend backwards, creating a wider base.
    4. Transfer your weight forward, driving your hips down and slightly towards your opponent's head, as if trying to sit on their chest.
    5. Your far-side hand will establish a "cross-collar grip" on the opponent's opposite lapel, creating a strong frame.
    6. Your knee on the ground should be placed perpendicular to your opponent's torso, with your foot flat on the mat for a solid base.
    7. The shin of your knee-on-belly leg should be parallel to your opponent's spine, ensuring maximum pressure and control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing your knee directly on your opponent's hip bone: This can cause severe hip joint impingement and labral tears for your opponent and strain your own knee ligaments.
    • Driving your knee straight down without forward hip pressure: This allows your opponent to easily shrimp their hips out and escape the position, leading to a loss of control.
    • Extending your bottom leg too far behind you: This compromises your base and can lead to your opponent bridging or rolling you over, risking ankle or knee hyperextension.
    • Gripping too low on the collar: This offers less control and leverage, allowing your opponent to create space and posture up, negating the pressure of Knee on Belly.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drill: Practice transitioning from a simulated side control to Knee on Belly, focusing on hip pressure and base. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner Drill (No Resistance): Your partner lies flat; you practice establishing Knee on Belly with correct weight distribution. (30 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner Drill (Light Resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance (e.g., just keeps their arms in). Focus on maintaining control and pressure. (20 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner Drill (Active Defense): Your partner attempts to shrimp or bridge. You focus on maintaining the position and adjusting your base. (15 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Controlled Rolling: Execute Knee on Belly transitions during light sparring, focusing on achieving the position and holding it. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling: Attempt to utilize Knee on Belly as a primary position during sparring, focusing on controlling your opponent and setting up attacks. (Full round, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully passing your opponent's guard and achieving a dominant top position.
    • When your opponent attempts to shrimp or create space from side control, and you can transition to a more controlling position.
    • As a setup to transition to mount or other dominant positions.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimp and Hip Escape: The opponent actively shrimping their hips away from the knee-on-belly leg, creating space to recover guard. This is countered by driving hips forward and adjusting the knee placement.
    • Bridge and Roll: The opponent uses their legs and hips to explosively bridge, attempting to off-balance and roll the attacker. This is countered by maintaining a low base and framing with your arms.
    • Re-guarding with the Bottom Leg: The opponent brings their bottom leg up to hook your attacking leg, attempting to establish guard. This is countered by keeping your knee-on-belly leg tight and transitioning to a different pass.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee on belly feel unstable and my opponent keeps escaping?

    Your instability likely stems from insufficient forward hip pressure. Instead of just placing your knee down, actively drive your hips towards your opponent's chest, almost as if you are trying to sit on them. This shifts your weight forward, making it much harder for them to shrimp or bridge effectively.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a significantly larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Focus on precise alignment: ensure your knee-on-belly shin is parallel to their spine, and your hips are driving forward, not just down. Use strong collar grips on both sides to control their posture and prevent them from using their weight to flatten you out.

    Q: Can I attack submissions directly from Knee on Belly, or is it just for control?

    Knee on Belly is an excellent platform for submissions. From this position, you can effectively attack with Armbars by trapping an arm and pivoting your hips, or set up kimuras by controlling their arm and driving your weight. It also creates opportunities for chokes like the cross-collar choke as they defend.
