Knee on Belly: The Essential BJJ Position for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant positional control that transitions from Side Control, aiming to pin the opponent and set up submissions. It offers superior leverage and pressure.

White belts often struggle by placing their weight incorrectly or losing balance, negating the position's effectiveness and creating openings.

The core principle is to use your shin and knee as a fulcrum, driving your hips down and forward to create a stable, uncomfortable base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, secure a cross-collar grip with your far hand and a sleeve grip (opposite side) with your near hand. Your hips should be low and forward.
    2. Shift your weight onto your opponent's chest, lifting your hips slightly off the mat.
    3. Drive your near knee (the one closer to their head) onto their sternum or upper abdomen, ensuring your shin is perpendicular to their body.
    4. Place your far foot flat on the mat, hip-width apart from your knee, creating a stable base.
    5. Your hips should be angled slightly towards their head, not directly over their torso, to prevent them from bridging effectively.
    6. Drive your chest down towards their chest, keeping your back relatively straight and your core engaged.
    7. Maintain constant pressure through your knee and hips, creating a strong base and limiting their movement. Your far hand can post on the mat for added stability if needed.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing your knee directly on the opponent's hip joint or lower abdomen: This can hyperextend or compress the hip joint and put dangerous pressure on the lumbar spine, leading to potential hip flexor tears or lower back strains.
    • Driving your knee straight down vertically without hip forward pressure: This can cause your knee to buckle or slip, risking an ACL tear or meniscus damage.
    • Allowing your hips to drift back and creating a "dead zone": This compromises your base, making it easy for the opponent to shrimp or bridge, and can lead to shoulder impingement from twisting to maintain position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side control to Knee on Belly, focusing on hip and knee placement. (20 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (passive): Your partner lies flat. You practice achieving and holding Knee on Belly for 15 seconds, focusing on weight distribution. (10 reps, 10% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance (e.g., attempts to brace). You work on maintaining the position for 15 seconds. (10 reps, 25% resistance).
    4. Transition drill: Start in side control, achieve Knee on Belly, and have your partner attempt a basic escape (e.g., shrimp). You practice re-establishing control or transitioning to another position. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Submission setup drill: From Knee on Belly, practice setting up a basic submission (e.g., collar choke). Your partner defends passively. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    6. Light rolling: Incorporate Knee on Belly attempts during light sparring, focusing on achieving and maintaining the position. (5 minutes, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Transitioning from side control when the opponent is attempting to bridge or shrimp away.
    • After successfully passing the guard and achieving a dominant side control position.
    • When the opponent is turtled or presenting their back, creating an opportunity to establish control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimp and Hip Escape: The opponent shrims their hips away, creating space to bring their knee inside and re-establish guard.
    • Knee Tap and Bridge: The opponent taps your knee with their foot and bridges, using your own pressure to create an escape angle.
    • Elbow Frame and Turn In: The opponent uses their forearm to frame against your knee or hip, then turns their body towards you to relieve pressure and create space.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly BJJ?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are placing direct pressure on the kneecap or the joint itself. Instead, focus on driving your shin and the side of your knee onto the opponent's torso. Ensure your hips are driven forward, distributing your weight through your pelvis and shin, not solely your knee joint.

    Q: How do I do Knee on Belly against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on leverage and angle. Drive your hips forward aggressively and aim your knee towards their sternum, not their abdomen where they have more padding and strength. Use your cross-collar grip to pull their head towards your knee, disrupting their base and making it harder for them to use their weight effectively.

    Q: When is the best time to switch my Knee on Belly leg?

    Switching legs is best done when the opponent attempts to turn into you or create space by bridging. As they initiate that movement, use it as an opportunity to pivot your hips and drive your other knee onto their torso. This dynamic switch often catches them off guard and re-establishes control from a new angle.
