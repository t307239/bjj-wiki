Knee on Belly: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant control position, typically achieved after a successful Guard Pass. It aims to break down your opponent's base and prepare for submissions or further positional advancement.

White belts often struggle by placing too much weight on their knee, leading to discomfort and poor control. They also fail to establish a strong, stable base.

The single key insight is to use your shin and foot as a stable post, while driving your hip and chest weight into your opponent's torso, creating a painful, restrictive pressure.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start from a dominant side control position, with your opponent flat on their back.
    2. Place your knee closest to their head (e.g., left knee) firmly on the mat, just above their hip bone, perpendicular to their torso.
    3. Bring your other foot (e.g., right foot) back, with your toes digging into the mat for stability, creating a base width of approximately shoulder-width.
    4. Drive your hip on the same side as the knee on the mat (e.g., left hip) downwards and slightly forward, towards your opponent's hip.
    5. Simultaneously, lower your chest and torso, applying downward pressure onto their sternum or upper abdomen.
    6. Your free hand (e.g., right hand) can establish a cross-collar grip or sleeve grip on their gi, pulling them towards you to prevent them from bridging.
    7. Your knee on the mat should feel like a pivot point, not the primary weight-bearing structure; the weight should be distributed through your hip and chest.
    8. Maintain a slight bend in your supporting leg's knee (the foot on the mat) to absorb shock and maintain balance.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing the knee directly on the opponent's hip bone and pressing down: This can cause direct pressure on the iliac crest, leading to bruising and potential hip flexor strain. The correct alternative is to place the knee slightly above the hip crease, targeting the abdominal area for control.
    • Leaning back and putting excessive weight on the knee on the mat: This compromises your base and can lead to your knee or ankle ligaments (e.g., ACL, MCL, ATFL) being overstretched or torn if the opponent shrimps or rolls.
    • Allowing the supporting foot to be flat on the mat with toes pointing forward: This creates a narrow, unstable base, making you susceptible to sweeps. The correct alternative is to dig your toes into the mat and widen your base.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side control to Knee on Belly, focusing on hip placement and base width. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drilling (static): Have a partner lie flat. Practice establishing Knee on Belly with correct weight distribution and grip. Hold for 10 seconds. (20 reps, 10% resistance).
    3. Partner drilling (slow movement): Practice moving Knee on Belly from one side to the other while your partner remains relatively still. Focus on maintaining control. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Guard pass to Knee on Belly: Practice a simple guard pass (e.g., knee slice pass) and immediately transition to Knee on Belly. (20 reps, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled rolling: Engage in light rolling with a partner, focusing on maintaining Knee on Belly for at least 10 seconds before transitioning or allowing them to escape. (5 rounds, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Implement Knee on Belly during regular sparring, aiming to hold the position and work towards submissions or sweeps. (10 rounds, 90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully passing the opponent's guard and establishing side control.
    • When the opponent attempts to shrimp or bridge from side control, creating an opening to transition.
    • As a transitionary position when moving from side control to mount.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimp (Hip Escape): From Knee on Belly, the opponent can shrimp their hips away, creating space to recover guard. The Knee on Belly practitioner must maintain forward pressure and adjust their base to prevent this.
    • Bridge: The opponent can attempt a bridge to unbalance you. A strong base with the supporting foot dug in and hips driving forward mitigates this.
    • Technical Stand-Up: If the opponent creates significant space, they may attempt a technical stand-up. Maintaining a tight chest-to-chest connection and controlling their arms prevents this.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt so much when I do Knee on Belly, and how can I avoid this pain?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are placing too much direct pressure on the kneecap or the bony prominence of the hip bone. The correct biomechanics involve using the shin as a pivot and driving your hip and chest weight into the opponent's torso. Ensure your knee is slightly above the hip crease, and distribute your weight onto your hip and the ball of your supporting foot.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a much larger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on precise weight distribution and leverage. Drive your hip on the side of the knee on the mat aggressively into their torso, forcing them to carry your weight. Utilize collar or sleeve grips to control their upper body and prevent them from creating space to bridge or shrimp. A wider base with your supporting foot digging into the mat is crucial for stability.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Knee on Belly to another position, and what are common mistakes?

    The best time to transition is when you feel your opponent is unable to effectively defend or create space. Common mistakes include holding Knee on Belly for too long without attacking, allowing them to settle and recover, or transitioning too quickly without securing control. Observe their defensive reactions; if they are stiff and unable to move, it's a good time to attack or advance to Mount.
