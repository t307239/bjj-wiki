Knee on Belly: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

Knee on Belly is a dominant top position achieved after a successful Guard Pass, aiming to control and submit opponents.

White belts often struggle by placing too much weight on their knee or failing to establish a solid base, leading to instability.

The key is to create a tripod base with your shin, foot, and opposing hand, distributing weight effectively onto the opponent's torso.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, drive your lead knee (e.g., right knee) towards the opponent's hip.
    2. Place the shin of your lead leg perpendicular to the opponent's sternum, with your foot flexed and toes pointing forward.
    3. Your lead knee should be slightly behind your lead foot, forming a stable base.
    4. Drive your hips forward and down, transferring weight onto the opponent's chest and diaphragm.
    5. Place your non-lead hand (e.g., left hand) on the mat, palm down, fingers spread, roughly shoulder-width from your lead knee.
    6. Your opposite arm (e.g., right arm) should secure a grip on the opponent's collar, specifically the lapel near the sternum.
    7. Keep your supporting foot (e.g., left foot) on the mat, hip-width behind your lead knee, acting as an anchor and allowing for weight adjustment.
    8. Your head should be up, looking forward, maintaining a strong posture and balance.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing the knee directly onto the opponent's thigh or hip bone, causing direct pressure on the femur or pelvis, which can lead to hip flexor strain or pelvic fractures.
    • Keeping the lead knee pointed outwards or at an acute angle, stressing the medial collateral ligament (MCL) of the knee.
    • Slouching the upper body and leaning back, removing weight from the opponent's chest and creating an unstable base, which can lead to the opponent bridging or escaping.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side-control to Knee on Belly without a partner (0% resistance). Focus on the foot placement and hip drive. (20 reps)
    2. Partner assisted drilling: Have a partner lie passively on their back. Practice establishing Knee on Belly, focusing on weight distribution and grip (25% resistance). (15 reps)
    3. Light positional drilling: From side-control, work to establish Knee on Belly. Partner offers minimal resistance to escapes (50% resistance). (10 reps per side)
    4. Controlled positional sparring: Start from Knee on Belly and work to maintain the position against a partner who is actively trying to escape (75% resistance). (5 rounds, 1 minute each)
    5. Introduction to transitions: Practice transitioning from Knee on Belly into other dominant positions like mount or side-control, and practice defending against basic Knee on Belly escapes (90% resistance). (3 rounds, 2 minutes each)
    6. Live rolling: Implement Knee on Belly as a positional goal within rolling rounds, focusing on maintaining control and transitioning (100% resistance). (2 rounds, 3 minutes each)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After a successful guard pass where the opponent is on their back and you have established side-control.
    • When the opponent attempts to bridge or shrimp, creating an opening to transition to a more dominant top position.
    • As a transitional position to advance to mount or set up submissions like the armbar or kimura.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): As the opponent attempts Knee on Belly, immediately shrimp your hips away to create space and prevent their knee from landing on your torso.
    • Bridge and Roll: If the opponent's Knee on Belly is poorly established, bridge your hips forcefully upwards and attempt to roll them over, reversing the position.
    • Guard Recovery: If you feel the Knee on Belly being applied, try to bring your knees up to your chest to create a frame and re-establish your guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I do Knee on Belly and what's the correct placement?

    Your knee may hurt due to direct pressure on your patella or tibia against the opponent's bone, or hyperextension. The correct placement involves placing the shin of your lead leg perpendicularly across the opponent's sternum, with your foot flexed and toes pointed forward, creating a stable base without direct knee-on-bone pressure.

    Q: How do I maintain Knee on Belly against a much bigger opponent who is strong?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maximizing your weight distribution and base. Drive your hips forward and down, ensuring your lead knee is slightly behind your foot. Utilize your non-lead hand for a strong grip on their collar to prevent them from posturing up, and keep your supporting foot grounded to adjust your base as needed.

    Q: When is the best time to switch my Knee on Belly leg?

    Switching your Knee on Belly leg, often called a 'switch knee,' is best done when the opponent attempts to turn into your lead leg or bridge. This transition is initiated by lifting your lead knee off their chest, posting your lead foot on the mat, and driving your hips to the other side to place your opposite knee on their chest, creating a new base and maintaining pressure.
