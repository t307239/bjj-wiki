Loop Choke: A Precise BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Loop Choke is typically initiated from Closed Guard or sometimes butterfly guard, aiming to secure a submission by constricting blood flow to the brain.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise control over their opponent's posture and a tendency to rely on brute strength rather than leverage.

The key mechanical insight is creating a tight "loop" with your arm around the opponent's neck and using your other arm to cinch it down, leveraging their own weight against them.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a cross-collar grip with your right hand on the opponent's left lapel, thumb inside.
    2. Thread your left arm between the opponent's neck and their right shoulder, reaching across their back.
    3. Secure a grip on your own right sleeve with your left hand, creating a "loop" around the opponent's neck.
    4. Immediately begin to break down the opponent's posture by pulling them forward and down with your arms, simultaneously driving your hips slightly upwards.
    5. Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, bringing your right shoulder towards the opponent's left hip.
    6. Use your left forearm, pressed firmly against the back of the opponent's neck, to create a fulcrum.
    7. Your right hand, gripping your sleeve, pulls sharply upwards and slightly backwards, tightening the loop and compressing the carotid artery.
    8. Maintain a strong hip base, keeping your knees tight to your chest to prevent the opponent from posturing up.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to force the choke by "pulling hard" with the choking arm without breaking down posture: This can strain the rotator cuff ligaments (supraspinatus, infraspinatus) of the shoulder due to excessive external rotation and abduction.
    • Overextending the neck backwards when the choke is applied: This puts excessive strain on the cervical spine ligaments (anterior longitudinal ligament, ligamentum nuchae), risking sprains or worse.
    • Allowing the opponent to flatten your guard by not maintaining hip pressure: This can lead to knee hyperextension injuries as you are forced to absorb their weight incorrectly, damaging the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) or medial collateral ligament (MCL).
    • Gripping too low on the lapel initially: This allows the opponent too much space to posture up, leading to potential neck crank injuries if they forcefully extend their head.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the arm threading and sleeve grip motion on a dummy or air, focusing on the "loop" formation (50 reps).
    2. Solo drill: Practice the torso rotation and hip drive motion without a partner, visualizing the choke mechanism (50 reps).
    3. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner in your closed guard, practice establishing the grips and performing the breakdown and choke motion, focusing on timing and body mechanics (10 reps per side).
    4. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner resists slightly by trying to hold posture, you focus on breaking it down and executing the choke (15 reps per side).
    5. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend by posturing up, you focus on maintaining control and finishing the choke, but stop before full tap (20 reps per side).
    6. Live rolling (75-90% resistance): Attempt the Loop Choke from closed guard in a sparring situation, focusing on proper application and timing (5 attempts per round).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is posturing up slightly from closed guard, creating an opening between their neck and shoulder.
    • When the opponent is reaching over your head to control your shoulders, allowing you to thread your arm.
    • When you have successfully broken down the opponent's posture and they are leaning forward.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Posture Up Defense: The opponent immediately straightens their back, creating space and relieving pressure on the carotid artery. They should then attempt to separate your legs or create an escape route.
    • Grip Break: The opponent uses their free hand to peel your choking hand off your sleeve or lapel grip, disrupting the "loop" mechanism.
    • Head Escape: The opponent turns their head away from the choking arm, creating space and preventing the full compression of the carotid artery.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right wrist hurt when I try to finish the Loop Choke from closed guard?

    This is often due to gripping your sleeve too low or not rotating your forearm properly. Ensure your left hand grips your right sleeve at the elbow or forearm area, and as you pull, rotate your right forearm inwards (pronation) and upwards. This aligns the pressure on the carotid artery and reduces strain on the wrist joint, preventing hyperextension or ulnar deviation.

    Q: How can I effectively perform the Loop Choke against a much larger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is to prioritize breaking their posture *before* attempting the choke. Use your hips to drive them forward and down, and use your legs to keep them tight to your chest. Once their posture is broken, the loop choke becomes much more about leverage and precise application of pressure rather than pure strength, as their own weight will work against them.

    Q: Is it better to grip the lapel with my thumb inside or outside for the Loop Choke?

    For the standard Loop Choke from closed guard, a thumb-inside grip on the opponent's lapel is generally preferred. This grip provides a more secure anchor point and allows for a tighter pull when you cinch the choke. A thumb-outside grip can sometimes allow the opponent to escape more easily by turning into it.
