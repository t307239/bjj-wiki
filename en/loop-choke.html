The Loop Choke is typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to secure a submission by constricting the carotid arteries. It's a powerful finishing mechanic when applied correctly.

White belts often struggle with this choke due to a lack of understanding of leverage and weight distribution, leading to ineffective attempts and lost energy.

The key mechanical insight is creating a tight "loop" with your arm around the opponent's neck, using your opposite hand to "cinch" it down by pulling their head towards your chest.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a standard cross-collar grip with your right hand on the opponent's left lapel, thumb inside the fabric.
    2. Your left hand grips the opponent's right collar, thumb inside the fabric, creating a "figure four" grip.
    3. Simultaneously, begin to open your closed guard by posting your feet on the opponent's hips and creating space.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly to your left, creating a diagonal angle relative to the opponent's body.
    5. Thread your right arm (the one on the opponent's left lapel) underneath their chin, ensuring it goes past their shoulder.
    6. Pull the opponent's head towards your chest by drawing your left arm (the one on their right collar) backward and down.
    7. Maintain pressure on the opponent's hips with your feet to prevent them from posturing up and relieving the choke.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to "muscle" the choke by pulling straight down with your arms: This can strain your biceps and triceps, and will not effectively constrict the carotid arteries. The correct movement involves driving your hips forward and pulling the opponent's head into the 'loop' created by your arm.
    • Gripping too high or too low on the collar: Gripping too high may not allow enough fabric for a tight choke, leading to slippage and potential neck strain. Gripping too low reduces leverage. The ideal grip is just below the opponent's chin, ensuring you have a good amount of fabric.
    • Forgetting to open guard and drive hips: Trying to apply the choke while still in a tight closed guard limits your range of motion and leverage. This can lead to shoulder impingement as you try to force the arm through. Always open your guard and use your hips to create the angle and power.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip sequence and hip movement (opening guard, driving hips) with no partner, focusing on the feeling of creating the diagonal angle. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the full execution of the choke from closed guard, focusing on the feel of the arm threading and the head pull. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to posture or bridging. Focus on maintaining the angle and finishing the choke. (10 reps per side)
    4. Flow rolling (light): Incorporate the Loop Choke into positional sparring, attempting it when the opportunity arises, but without forcing it. (5 minutes, 25% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring (moderate): Focus specifically on attacking the Loop Choke from closed guard, with the partner actively defending. (5 minutes, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Loop Choke during regular sparring, prioritizing correct mechanics over immediate submission. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is posturing up slightly in your closed guard, creating a small gap.
    • When the opponent is pressing their weight down heavily, making it difficult for them to defend their neck.
    • During transitions where the opponent's head position is momentarily exposed.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Posture Up and Grip Break: If the opponent attempts the choke, immediately widen your base, posture up by extending your legs, and use your hands to break their grip on your collar. This relieves the pressure before it becomes dangerous.
    • Shrimp Away and Re-establish Guard: If the choke is being applied, quickly shrimp your hips away to create space, then use your legs to re-establish a closed guard or escape to a safer position. This prevents the opponent from cinching the choke effectively.
    • Head and Arm Escape: As the opponent threads their arm, try to tuck your chin and turn your head away from the choking arm while simultaneously trying to free your head and neck. This can disrupt the choke and allow you to escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Loop Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs because you're gripping the lapel too tightly with a pronated (palm down) wrist, or you're trying to pull the opponent's head down directly with your arms. Instead, focus on a neutral or slightly supinated wrist when gripping and use your hips to drive forward and pull their head into the choke, rather than just using brute force.

    Q: How can I effectively set up the Loop Choke against a bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Instead of trying to force the choke, focus on creating a significant hip angle by driving your hips forward and to the side. Use their weight against them by opening your guard and allowing them to press down, then use that pressure to help thread your arm and pull their head into the choke.

    Q: Is the Loop Choke always applied with a cross-collar grip, or can I use a different grip?

    While the cross-collar grip is the most common and effective for the traditional Loop Choke, variations exist. However, for white belts, mastering the cross-collar setup is paraMount. This grip provides the necessary leverage and control to isolate the opponent's neck and create the 'loop' for the submission.
