The Loop Choke is typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to secure a submission by compressing the carotid arteries. It's a powerful choke when executed correctly.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or incorrect body positioning, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Understanding the leverage is key.

The core mechanical insight is using your opponent's posture against them by creating a fulcrum with your forearm and driving your shoulder into their neck.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's gi lapel with your right hand, gripping the far side lapel just below their collarbone. This is a 'Lapel Grip'.
    2. Place your left arm inside their left arm, reaching across their neck to secure your own right lapel with your left hand. This is a 'Lapel Grip'.
    3. Arch your back and drive your hips upward, creating space and breaking their posture forward. Your pelvis should angle towards their head.
    4. Thread your left forearm under their chin, ensuring it is snug against their neck. Your left elbow should be pointing towards the ceiling.
    5. Extend your right arm straight, pulling your right lapel grip across their neck, creating the 'loop'. Your right hand grips your own gi.
    6. Rotate your torso to your left, simultaneously driving your right shoulder forward into their neck. This applies pressure.
    7. Close your guard tightly around their torso, preventing them from posturing up. Your knees should drive towards their armpits.
    8. Lean your upper body back slightly, using your weight to add downward pressure to the choke. This leverages your body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too high on the lapel: This can lead to your grip slipping, causing you to overextend your shoulder joint, potentially straining the rotator cuff or causing impingement.
    • Not creating enough space with your hips: This results in a weak choke and forces you to use excessive arm strength, risking biceps tendonitis or wrist sprains from twisting.
    • Allowing opponent to posture up: This relieves pressure on the carotid arteries and can lead to your arm being trapped, risking elbow hyperextension or shoulder dislocation if they try to stack you.
    • Forgetting to rotate the torso: Simply pulling the lapel without torso rotation limits the choke's effectiveness and can strain your shoulder joint by pulling it into an unnatural position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of grip acquisition and body positioning without a partner (10 reps each side).
    2. Practicing the entire sequence with a compliant partner, focusing on correct body mechanics and hip movement (10 reps).
    3. Drilling with light resistance, where the partner offers minimal defense but maintains their structure (5 reps).
    4. Incorporating a light push from the partner to simulate minor resistance, focusing on maintaining posture and grip integrity (5 reps).
    5. Drilling with increasing resistance, where the partner actively tries to defend by posturing up or creating space (3 reps).
    6. Live rolling scenarios where the Loop Choke is a primary submission option, at 75% intensity to allow for adjustments.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured down in your closed guard, creating a compact upper body.
    • When you have established a strong lapel grip and can isolate one of their arms.
    • When your opponent is trying to smash pass your closed guard and their head is low.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Defense 1 (Posturing Up): If the choke is being applied, the opponent should immediately try to sit up and bring their head forward. This breaks the angle and relieves pressure on the carotid arteries.
    • Defense 2 (Grip Release): If you can reach the choking arm's lapel grip, try to break that specific grip by pushing your knuckles into their hand and rotating your wrist.
    • Defense 3 (Arm Isolation): The defender can try to isolate the choking arm by bringing their opposite arm across their own body and 'framing' against the attacker's bicep, creating space.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Loop Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs because you're gripping your own lapel too tightly or with an unnatural angle. Ensure your hand is flat on your gi, and when you rotate your torso, your wrist stays in a neutral or slightly flexed position. Avoid twisting your wrist forcefully; let the rotation of your shoulder and torso do the work to apply the choke.

    Q: How can a white belt effectively use the Loop Choke against a larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on using your hips to break their posture and create a tight seal with your legs. The key is to get your forearm deep under their chin and then use your shoulder and upper body rotation to drive into them. Don't try to overpower them; instead, use their size against them by creating a strong fulcrum.

    Q: When should I NOT attempt the Loop Choke from closed guard?

    Avoid the Loop Choke when your opponent has excellent posture and is actively posturing up, as this makes the choke ineffective and can leave your arm vulnerable. Also, if your opponent is very heavy and smashing directly down into your guard without creating any space, it might be difficult to get the necessary depth and angle for the choke.
