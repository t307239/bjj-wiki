Loop Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Loop Choke is primarily initiated from the Closed Guard, aiming to secure a submission around the opponent's neck and one arm. It offers a powerful strangulation option that can be difficult to defend once locked.

White belts often struggle due to imprecise grips and a lack of understanding of the necessary spinal flexion, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. They frequently rely on brute strength rather than leverage.

The core mechanical principle is creating a tight 'loop' with your arm around the neck and shoulder, then using your body weight and spinal extension to constrict blood flow to the brain.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a cross-collar grip with your right hand on the opponent's left lapel, thumb inside. Your left hand grips the opponent's right sleeve at the wrist.
    2. Shimmy your hips to your right, creating an angle. Your left hip should be slightly elevated relative to your right.
    3. As you shimmy, bring your right arm across the opponent's neck, tucking your bicep tightly against their carotid artery. Your right forearm should be flush against their throat.
    4. Secure your right grip by interlocking your fingers or grabbing your right wrist with your left hand, creating a "loop" around their neck and left shoulder.
    5. Drive your left elbow down towards the mat, keeping your shoulder blade retracted. This creates tension on the lapel grip.
    6. Simultaneously, extend your spine and arch your back, pushing your hips forward into the opponent. This compresses the loop.
    7. Maintain constant pressure on the carotid artery with your right forearm and the lapel grip with your left hand, while your spinal extension applies the choking force.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Reaching too far across the neck with the choking arm: This strains the shoulder joint (rotator cuff ligaments) as the humerus is over-extended, leading to tears. The correct movement involves tucking the bicep firmly against the neck with a bent elbow.
    • Trying to 'pull' the choke closed with arm strength alone: This puts excessive strain on the biceps and elbows, risking tendonitis or muscle tears. The primary force comes from spinal extension and hip pressure, not arm pulling.
    • Not establishing a strong lapel grip or sleeve grip: This allows the opponent to easily posture up or create space, negating the choke and potentially forcing an awkward, high-elbow position that injures the choking shoulder's capsule. Always ensure a secure grip before initiating the final compression.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of grip establishment and arm positioning with no opponent (10 reps each side).
    2. Partner drilling: Opponent lies flat, you drill the grip setup and arm placement from closed guard (10 reps each side).
    3. Partner drilling: Opponent offers minimal resistance, you practice the hip shimmy, arm tuck, and grip lock (20 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drilling: Opponent offers light resistance to posture, you practice the spinal extension and hip drive to complete the choke (20 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling: Attempt the Loop Choke from closed guard in positional sparring, focusing on correct mechanics (5 minutes, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Incorporate the Loop Choke into regular rolling, aiming for submission when the opportunity arises (5 minutes, 90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • When the opponent postures up slightly within your closed guard, creating a gap for your arm to slide across.
    • When the opponent attempts to stack you, bringing their weight forward and down.
    • When the opponent tries to break your closed guard by posturing very high.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt and not feel choked when I attempt the Loop Choke from closed guard?

    Your neck pain, not a choke, indicates you are likely applying pressure to the trachea (windpipe) instead of the carotid arteries. The loop needs to be positioned so your forearm presses against the side of the neck, compressing the arteries. Ensure your bicep is tucked tightly against the side of their neck and your spinal extension drives your hips forward, not just a pulling motion.

    Q: How can I use the Loop Choke against a much larger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on precise hip shimmies to create a strong angle, ensuring your choking arm's bicep is deep against their neck and shoulder. Use your leg strength to drive your hips forward and upward as you extend your spine, compressing their frame. Do not rely on arm strength; instead, use your body's weight and leverage to create the choke.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to a Loop Choke from closed guard, and when should I abandon it?

    The ideal time to initiate is when the opponent postures slightly or attempts to stack, creating an opening. Abandon the attempt if they successfully prevent your arm from getting deep, if they manage to create significant space by posturing very high, or if they flatten you out completely, making it impossible to generate hip pressure. In these cases, resetting to a more secure guard or attempting a sweep is often better.
