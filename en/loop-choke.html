The Loop Choke is typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to control the opponent's neck and arm for a submission. It leverages a specific grip and body positioning to constrict blood flow.

White belts often struggle with this choke because they rely on brute strength rather than precise mechanics, leading to poor positioning and ineffective attempts.

The key mechanical insight is creating a tight "loop" with your arm around the opponent's neck and trapping their arm, then using your body to drive into the choke.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a grip on the opponent's far-side collar with your right hand. Grip should be deep, thumb inside the lapel, index knuckle against the opponent's carotid artery.
    2. With your left hand, reach under the opponent's armpit on the same side as your collar grip. Secure a grip on the opponent's gi sleeve, specifically their wrist or forearm area.
    3. Begin to break your closed guard by extending your legs slightly, creating a small space to initiate movement.
    4. Drive your right hip down and towards the opponent's left side. Simultaneously, begin to posture up slightly by straightening your back.
    5. Swing your left arm, which is gripping the sleeve, across the opponent's body, ensuring your elbow stays tight to their neck.
    6. As your left arm swings across, pull the opponent's sleeve-grip in towards your chest. This movement helps to pull their arm across their own body, isolating their neck.
    7. Simultaneously, drive your chest into the opponent's neck and shoulder area, creating pressure. Your right hip should remain low and angled.
    8. Tighten the choke by pulling your right collar grip towards your left elbow, while simultaneously using your core to drive your hips forward and up slightly, creating a crushing pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to crank the neck with your hands alone: This can lead to hyperextension or compression of the cervical spine, potentially causing nerve damage or disc injury. Instead, use your body weight and hip drive to create the choking pressure.
    • Over-extending your own elbow on the collar grip: This puts excessive strain on your elbow joint, risking a sprain or tear. Maintain a controlled grip with your forearm and wrist, using your shoulder to initiate the pull.
    • Allowing the opponent to stack you directly: If the opponent stacks straight down without angling, your spine is vulnerable to compression. Keep your hips angled and drive into them, not straight down.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the grip acquisition, hip angle, and body movement in the air for 10 repetitions per side.
    2. Partner drilling with no resistance (0% resistance): With a compliant partner in closed guard, practice the entire sequence of grips and body movements, focusing on precision. 5 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner drilling with light resistance (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to your guard break and movement. Focus on maintaining control and proper mechanics. 5 repetitions per side.
    4. Partner drilling with moderate resistance (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend but allows you to work the mechanics. Focus on generating the correct body pressure. 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Partner drilling with increasing resistance (75% resistance): Partner defends more actively, requiring you to adjust your mechanics. Focus on finishing the choke. 3 repetitions per side.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Attempt the Loop Choke in sparring situations. Focus on applying the technique correctly, not necessarily on getting the tap. 3-5 attempts per roll.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is posturing up slightly from your closed guard, creating an opening for your arm.
    • When the opponent's arm is extended or loosely positioned, making it easier to trap.
    • When you have established a strong collar grip and can control their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Defense: If the opponent attempts to stack you, immediately flatten your back and drive your hips into their chest to break their posture and prevent spinal compression. Maintain your grips.
    • Arm Escape: If the opponent feels the choke coming and tries to pull their trapped arm out, immediately tighten your grip on their sleeve and use your body to drive their arm across their body further, ensuring they cannot free it.
    • Guard Break: If the opponent defends by breaking your closed guard, immediately transition to a different control position like half guard or butterfly guard, or attempt a sweep to regain dominant positioning.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Loop Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs from gripping too tightly with your fingers alone or from an awkward angle. Ensure your collar grip is deep with your thumb inside and your knuckles aligned with the carotid artery, not just gripping the lapel edge. For the sleeve grip, focus on controlling their forearm or wrist with your palm and fingers, not just the gi fabric, and maintain a stable wrist angle to avoid hyperextension.

    Q: How can a white belt effectively use the Loop Choke against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on disrupting their posture before initiating the choke by using your legs to create angles and pulling their head down. When applying the choke, drive your hip down and across to create a leverage point, and use your body weight to apply pressure rather than pure grip strength. Ensure your arm creates a tight 'loop' around their neck and traps their arm securely.

    Q: Why can't I finish the Loop Choke even when I have the right grips?

    Often, the choke isn't finished due to a lack of body mechanics. You might be relying too much on grip strength. The key is to drive your chest into their neck and shoulder while simultaneously pulling your collar grip towards your choking arm's elbow. This combined movement, powered by your hips and core, creates the necessary pressure to constrict blood flow effectively. Ensure your hips are angled and driving forward.
