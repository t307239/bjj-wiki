Pendulum Sweep: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Pendulum Sweep starts from your guard, most commonly Closed Guard, aiming to invert your hips and sweep your opponent to gain top position. White belts often struggle with generating leverage, leading to stalled sweeps and potential energy loss. Success hinges on creating a dynamic hip hinge and using your legs as levers to unbalance your opponent's base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong closed guard grip: two hands on opponent's opposite collar (e.g., right hand on left collar, left hand on right collar).
    2. Secure opponent's opposite sleeve grip with your free hand (e.g., left hand on opponent's right sleeve).
    3. Initiate a slight hip lift, creating space to bring your right knee inside, towards your opponent's right armpit.
    4. Simultaneously, extend your left leg, driving your left heel towards your opponent's right hip bone.
    5. As your left leg extends, begin to pull down on the collar grips and push your opponent's shoulder away with your right elbow.
    6. Rotate your hips sharply to your left, allowing your right leg (bent at the knee) to swing up and over your opponent's right hip.
    7. Drive your opponent's weight forward and to their right by continuing the hip rotation and leg swing, ultimately unbalancing them and moving to mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'push' your opponent over with your hands instead of unbalancing them: This can strain your shoulders and elbows, leading to rotator cuff tears or elbow hyperextension.
    • Correct: Focus on pulling their weight forward and off-balance with your leg extension and hip rotation.
    • Swinging your leg too wide and losing control of your opponent's hip: This can result in your knee or ankle being trapped, risking ligament damage (ACL/MCL tears) or ankle sprains.
    • Correct: Keep your swinging leg's knee bent and controlled, aiming to hook their hip as you sweep.
    • Not creating enough hip space or lifting your hips sufficiently: This often leads to your opponent posturing up and potentially collapsing your guard, risking knee or hip impingement injuries.
    • Correct: Exaggerate the initial hip lift to create the necessary space for your knee to penetrate.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip lift, knee-in, leg extension, and hip rotation motion without a partner. (20 reps each side).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Partner stands with you in closed guard. Execute the entire sweep motion, focusing on grip control and hip movement. (10 reps each side).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner maintains a very loose posture. Execute the sweep, focusing on unbalancing them. (15 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (controlled resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the sweep but doesn't resist fully. Focus on driving through their defense. (20 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (positional sparring): Start in closed guard. Attempt the Pendulum Sweep with full intent. (5 attempts per round).
    6. Live rolling (full rolling): Incorporate the Pendulum Sweep into regular sparring. (10-15 minutes of rolling, 75-90% intensity).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up, creating space between your hips and theirs.
    • When your opponent's weight is forward, making them susceptible to being pulled down.
    • When you have established strong collar and sleeve grips, allowing for good control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent bases out: If your opponent recognizes the sweep and plants their feet wide, immediately transition to a guard pass attempt or a submission like a triangle choke.
    • Opponent collapses: If they fall forward, drive your shoulder into their chest and transition to mount or a knee-on-belly position.
    • Opponent defends by stacking: If they try to stack you, use your legs to push them away and create space to either recompose guard or attempt a sweep from a different angle.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my hip feel strained when I try to do the Pendulum Sweep?

    A strained hip often occurs if you're not creating sufficient space by lifting your hips and bringing your knee deep into your opponent's armpit. This forces your hip to twist in an unnatural, unsupported way. Ensure you get your knee inside their armpit *before* you begin the sweeping leg's motion, and focus on a fluid hip rotation rather than a forceful twist.

    Q: How do I Pendulum Sweep against a much bigger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. You must exaggerate the hip lift to create space, and ensure your knee penetrates deeply inside their armpit. Focus on pulling their weight forward with your collar grips and driving their opposite shoulder away with your elbow as you swing your leg. The key is to unbalance their mass, not to overpower them.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from a failed Pendulum Sweep to something else?

    If your opponent successfully defends the initial sweep motion by posturing up or stepping their hips back, you've likely created an opportunity. Instead of clinging to the failed sweep, immediately look to transition. If they posture, consider switching to a Triangle Choke setup by bringing your leg over their shoulder. If they step back, you might have an opening for a sweep from butterfly guard or to transition to a leg drag.
