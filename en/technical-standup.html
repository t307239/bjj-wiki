The Technical Stand-Up is a fundamental BJJ transition, moving from a grounded position to a standing one while maintaining control and safety. It's crucial for escaping bad spots and creating offensive opportunities.

White belts often struggle because they rush, losing their base and creating openings for their opponent to exploit. They forget the core principle of using their frames and momentum correctly.

The key insight is to act like a spring: compress, then extend, using your opponent's pressure against them to create upward force and a stable base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start in a seated guard or half-guard with your opponent attempting a pass. Establish a strong collar grip (lapel near the neck) with your right hand and a sleeve grip (opponent's right sleeve) with your left hand.
    2. Place your left foot flat on the mat, knee bent at 90 degrees, directly in front of your opponent's hip, creating a frame.
    3. Drive your right knee to the mat beside your left foot, forming a stable base with both knees on the ground, hips slightly elevated.
    4. Simultaneously, pull strongly on the collar and sleeve grips, drawing your opponent forward and down towards your chest.
    5. As your opponent's weight comes forward, shift your hips back and slightly to your left, tucking your left foot further under your body.
    6. Extend your left leg forcefully, pushing off the mat to create upward momentum, while keeping your right knee anchored.
    7. Drive your hips forward and rise to a staggered stance, maintaining your grips to control your opponent's posture and prevent them from re-establishing pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending the arm holding the collar grip: This can lead to a straight-armbar attempt by the opponent, causing hyperextension and potential ligament tears in the elbow.
    • Pushing off with the wrong foot: If you push off with your back foot too early or with insufficient base, you can lose balance and fall back, risking a knee injury or allowing your opponent to secure a dominant position.
    • Failing to create a frame with the lead knee: Not placing the left knee firmly on the mat and in front of the opponent's hip allows them to drive through, potentially crushing your shin or leading to a knee-on-belly transition without proper control.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the entire motion of the Technical Stand-Up without an opponent, focusing on smooth transitions and proper footwork for 10 reps.
    2. Partner assisted (25% resistance): Have a partner lie on their back and gently push against you as you perform the Technical Stand-Up, focusing on grip timing and hip movement for 5 reps per side.
    3. Static guard simulation (50% resistance): Your partner sits in closed guard. You establish the grips and execute the Technical Stand-Up as they apply minimal pressure, focusing on breaking their guard and standing for 5 reps.
    4. Guard pass defense simulation (75% resistance): Your partner attempts a basic guard pass (e.g., knee slice). You react by establishing the Technical Stand-Up, focusing on maintaining base and posture for 5 reps.
    5. Light rolling with focus (90% resistance): During rolling, actively look for opportunities to use the Technical Stand-Up when your opponent tries to pass your guard, focusing on successful execution and maintaining your base for 3 minutes.
    6. Full rolling (100% resistance): Integrate the Technical Stand-Up into your regular rolling sessions, using it as a transition tool whenever a suitable opportunity arises.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is actively attempting to pass your guard from a seated or open guard position.
    • When you are caught in a weak guard retention scenario and need to create space to reset or stand up.
    • As a means to transition from a compromised guard to a more stable fighting base.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Sweep to Mount: As the opponent commits to their pass, use a scissor sweep or tripod sweep to get on top into mount.
    • Back Take: If the opponent overextends on their pass, use hip movement and grip control to spin behind them for a back take.
    • Re-Guard: If the stand-up is compromised, immediately drop back to your guard, using your legs to create a frame and re-establish guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try the Technical Stand-Up?

    Your shoulder likely hurts due to improper frame creation. When you try to stand, if your opponent drives forward and your elbow flares out, it puts immense stress on the shoulder joint. To fix this, ensure your lead knee is firmly planted and angled towards the opponent's hip, creating a solid frame that prevents their forward pressure from reaching your arm directly.

    Q: How can I Technical Stand-Up effectively against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. Focus on using their weight against them by pulling strongly on the collar and sleeve to break their posture and draw their weight forward. Simultaneously, drive your hips back and then explode upwards, using your legs as the primary power source, not your arms. Think of it as using their mass to help you stand.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Technical Stand-Up in BJJ?

    The Technical Stand-Up is best used when your opponent is actively trying to pass your guard from a grounded position. It's a transition that allows you to regain your feet safely and maintain control, rather than just scrambling up haphazardly. It's also excellent for escaping bad situations where you might be flat on your back and need to create space to reset.
