The Technical Stand-Up is a crucial transition from bottom positions, like Closed Guard, to a standing base. It allows you to safely disengage or initiate takedowns.

White belts often rush this, neglecting posture and base, leading to instability and potential injury. Understanding the mechanics is key.

The core principle is creating a stable platform with your legs and hips while using your arms as levers to lift and propel your body upwards.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From bottom guard, establish a strong collar grip (one hand on opponent's collar, the other on the same side sleeve) with your opponent on their back.
    2. Simultaneously, drive your hips slightly forward and to the side of the opponent's torso, creating a narrow base.
    3. Bring your right knee (if moving to the right) up and place your right foot flat on the mat, aiming for a position directly under your hip.
    4. Your left leg should be extended backward, foot on the mat for balance, creating a stable tripod with your knees and right foot.
    5. Use your collar and sleeve grips to pull your opponent forward and down, breaking their posture.
    6. Drive through your right foot and the ball of your left foot, extending your hips upwards.
    7. As your hips rise, swing your left leg forward and plant your left foot next to your right foot, achieving a stable two-footed base.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Rushing the stand-up by not establishing a strong tripod base with the right foot under the hip: This can lead to hyperextension of the knee or ankle ligament sprains as the body's weight collapses onto an unstable limb.
    • Failing to create a frame with the arms by pushing directly into the opponent's chest instead of using grips to control their posture: This can result in shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strain if the opponent unexpectedly shifts their weight.
    • Extending the non-driving leg too far back without maintaining foot contact: This compromises balance, increasing the risk of falling and potentially twisting an ankle or knee upon impact.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg and hip movements without a partner, focusing on foot placement and hip drive. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner lies on their back, you perform the Technical Stand-Up. Focus on grip and body positioning. (10 reps)
    3. Light resistance drill: Partner offers minimal resistance by keeping their base solid. Focus on breaking their posture. (25% resistance, 10 reps)
    4. Controlled stand-up: Partner actively tries to prevent you from standing but doesn't use aggressive counter-movements. (50% resistance, 10 reps)
    5. Semi-live drilling: Partner attempts basic counter-movements (e.g., pushing hips). Focus on maintaining your base and posture. (75% resistance, 5 reps)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Technical Stand-Up into sparring, attempting it when the opportunity arises. (100% resistance, practice time)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is on their back and you are in their closed guard or open guard with control.
    • When you need to disengage from a bad position (e.g., opponent is about to pass your guard).
    • When you see an opportunity to transition to a takedown from a broken guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Push: As you begin to stand, the defender pushes your hips away with their hands, preventing you from getting your foot under your hip. You must maintain your frame and adjust your base, potentially re-establishing guard or switching your stand-up direction.
    • Knee Block: The defender attempts to block your rising knee with their own knee. You counter by driving your hips harder and ensuring your foot is securely planted, using your grips to keep their posture broken.
    • Posture Control: The defender maintains strong posture, making it difficult to pull them forward. Use your collar and sleeve grips to actively break their posture by pulling them down and to the side before initiating the stand-up.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right knee hurt when I do the Technical Stand-Up from closed guard?

    This often happens when your right foot is placed too far forward or too far back from directly under your hip. If it's too far forward, your knee joint absorbs excessive anterior shear forces. If it's too far back, your knee can hyperextend. Ensure your right foot is planted flat, with your knee directly above your ankle and your hip aligned over your knee during the initial drive.

    Q: How can I do the Technical Stand-Up against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. Focus on breaking their posture *before* you attempt to stand. Use your leg and hip structure to create a strong base, and don't be afraid to use your grips to pull them forward and down aggressively to create space. Your hips need to drive upwards with significant force to overcome their mass.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Technical Stand-Up, and should I always try to stand up?

    The Technical Stand-Up is best used when you have control of your opponent's posture from a bottom position and want to safely transition to standing or initiate a takedown. It's not always optimal; if you are in a stable guard and your opponent is not actively passing, remaining in guard might be more advantageous. Prioritize safety and opportunity over always attempting to stand.
