The Technical Stand-Up is a fundamental BJJ transition used to safely rise to your feet from a seated or kneeling position, often from guard or during scrambles.

White belts often rush this, forgetting the foundational principles of base and framing, leading to uncontrolled falls and potential injury.

The core principle is creating a stable base with your hips and using your hands as frames to push your opponent away, allowing you to stand without compromising your posture.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start on your knees, opponent in your closed guard. Secure a collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on their left arm with your left hand.
    2. Shift your hips to your right, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and the opponent's torso.
    3. Simultaneously, extend your left leg straight back, placing your left foot flat on the mat for base, keeping your knee slightly bent.
    4. Use your right hand (collar grip) to pull the opponent slightly forward and to your right, breaking their posture.
    5. Push off the mat with your right knee, extending your right leg to a kneeling position, aligning your right shin perpendicular to your torso.
    6. Drive your left foot forward and plant it firmly, aiming to place it roughly shoulder-width apart from your right foot.
    7. Simultaneously, push off the mat with your right knee while extending your hips upward and forward, driving through your planted feet.
    8. As you stand, maintain your frames: your left hand remains on their sleeve, and your right hand can post on their hip or knee for additional stability, keeping your base wide.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Standing straight up without creating a hip angle: This exposes your spine to hyperextension or compression, risking lumbar disc injury.

    Correct: Always initiate a hip turn to 45 degrees before attempting to stand.

    • Grabbing the opponent's gi and pulling them onto you: This invites them to maintain control or attack your base, risking knee ligament tears (ACL/MCL) if you're pulled into an awkward position.

    Correct: Use grips to create space and pull them slightly forward, not directly onto you.

    • Placing hands on the mat directly in front of your knees: This creates a narrow base and can lead to a wrist fracture if you lose balance and fall forward onto an outstretched hand.

    Correct: Ensure your feet are planted wider, and use your hands as frames on the opponent, not just as support.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drilling (0% resistance): Practice the hip angle, leg placement, and hip drive motion without an opponent. Focus on smooth transitions. (20 reps)
    2. Partnered Frame Creation (25% resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice creating the hip angle and pushing their hips away with your hands. (15 reps per side)
    3. Partnered Hip Turn and Foot Placement (50% resistance): Partner stays seated. You practice the hip turn, extending one leg back, and bringing the other foot forward. Partner offers minimal resistance. (15 reps per side)
    4. Assisted Stand-Up (75% resistance): Partner lies down. You perform the full Technical Stand-Up. Your partner can lightly grip your gi to simulate resistance, but does not actively prevent your stand. (10 reps per side)
    5. Controlled Stand-Up (90% resistance): Partner is in your guard (closed or open). You initiate the Technical Stand-Up. Partner offers moderate resistance, trying to prevent you from standing but without active submission attempts. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling Transition Practice (100% resistance): In a rolling situation, consciously look for opportunities to use the Technical Stand-Up when you are on your knees or seated and need to create space to stand. (Focus on 2-3 attempts per roll)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is in your closed guard and attempts to pass by posturing up.
    • When you are on your knees in your opponent's guard and need to disengage to stand.
    • During scrambles where you find yourself on your knees and need to establish a standing base.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Pull/Hip Bump: If the opponent attempts to stand, instead of following their movement, perform a hip bump sweep by rocking your hips to their side, creating an angle to sweep them.
    • Leg Entanglement: As they begin to stand, hook their leg with your foot (e.g., de la riva hook) and pull it to disrupt their base, forcing them to fall or abandon the stand-up.
    • Postural Control: If they initiate the stand-up but their base is weak, maintain your frames and push them back down into your guard by driving their hips away with your forearms and hips.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Technical Stand-Up?

    Your lower back pain likely stems from hyperextending or compressing your spine during the stand-up. This happens when you try to stand straight up without first creating a 45-degree hip angle and using your hands as frames to push your opponent away. The correct movement involves turning your hips and driving your weight forward through your legs, not by arching your back.

    Q: How do I Technical Stand-Up against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, creating a strong frame is paraMount. Ensure your grips (collar and sleeve) are secure and use them to pull their weight forward and slightly off-balance. Your hip turn and the extension of your back leg create the initial separation. Drive through your planted feet, keeping your base wide, to lift your hips and stand.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Technical Stand-Up in BJJ?

    The Technical Stand-Up is most effective when you are in a compromised position like being on your knees in guard or during a scramble, and you need to safely return to a standing base. It's ideal when your opponent is trying to control you from a seated or guard position and you want to disengage without getting swept or submitting.
