North-South Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The North-South Choke is a submission applied from the North-South position, aiming to cut off blood flow to the opponent's brain.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of precise pressure and framing.

True mastery comes from controlling the opponent's head and shoulder girdle with your own body's structural alignment.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish North-South position: Your chest is on the opponent's chest, head facing their feet.
    2. Secure the cross-collar grip: Your right hand grips the opponent's left lapel, thumb inside, approximately at the sternoclavicular joint.
    3. Secure the secondary grip: Your left hand grips the opponent's right lapel, thumb inside, at the same height.
    4. Drive your hips down: Lower your pelvis towards the mat, creating a strong base and preventing the opponent from bridging.
    5. Align your body: Your chest should be directly over the opponent's carotid artery, and your hips should be slightly angled towards their head.
    6. Pinch your elbows: Bring your elbows together towards your torso, creating inward pressure.
    7. Extend your hips: Apply a slight upward and forward push with your hips, while simultaneously squeezing your arms inward.
    8. Maintain head control: Keep your head pressed against the opponent's shoulder or neck to prevent them from turning their head.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too low on the collar: This allows the opponent's head to escape the choke and can strain the wrist ligaments of your gripping hand.

    The correct grip is high on the lapel, near the neck.

    • Trying to lift the opponent with your arms: This leads to shoulder impingement and elbow hyperextension.

    Focus on driving your hips down and forward for pressure.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of grips and body alignment: 10 repetitions per side, 0% resistance.
    2. Partner drilling of grip exchange and hip drive: 10 repetitions per side, 0% resistance.
    3. Partner drilling with static resistance (opponent remains still): 5 repetitions per side, 25% resistance.
    4. Partner drilling with light, predictable movement (e.g., opponent attempts to shrimp): 5 repetitions per side, 50% resistance.
    5. Partner drilling with moderate resistance (opponent actively defends): 3 repetitions per side, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the North-South Choke when the opportunity arises, 100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong North-South position after a guard pass or sweep.
    • When the opponent is turtled or attempting to defend a mount or side control position.
    • When you have superior control of the opponent's upper body and head.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimp and escape: The opponent can shrimp their hips away to create space and relieve pressure on the carotid artery.
    • Bridge and roll: The opponent can bridge their hips upwards and attempt to roll, disrupting the choke's mechanics.
    • Hand fight and frame: The opponent can use their free hand to frame against your shoulder or head, creating space and breaking your grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the North-South Choke?

    Wrist pain often results from gripping the lapel too low or with a bent wrist. Ensure your grip is high on the lapel, near the opponent's sternoclavicular joint, and keep your wrist straight and neutral. Drive pressure through your forearm and elbow, not by bending your wrist.

    Q: How do I apply the North-South Choke effectively against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus intensely on hip pressure and body alignment. Your hips must be heavy and driving down to negate their size advantage. Use your chest to pin their head and shoulder, and ensure your grip is high and tight to maximize leverage on the carotid artery.

    Q: I feel like I'm just squishing my opponent, not choking them. What am I missing?

    You are likely missing the critical component of isolating the opponent's head and shoulder. Your chest should be pressed firmly against their carotid artery, and your arms should be pinching inwards to create the choke. The pressure comes from constricting blood flow, not just compressing their body.
