North-South Position: A Biomechanical Guide for White Belts

The North-South position is a dominant control position achieved when your body is perpendicular to your opponent's, with your head near their hips and their head near your hips.

White belts often struggle here by overextending their base or allowing their opponent to bridge effectively, losing the control and potentially their posture.

The key to North-South is maintaining a tight, low base and using your weight distribution to stifle your opponent's hip movement and escape options.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Side Control, transition by hip-scissoring your hips forward and over your opponent's torso, aiming to align your sternum with their hip bone.
    2. Place your chest firmly on their upper abdomen/lower ribcage area, creating a "smash" effect.
    3. Your head should be positioned near their hip joint, pointing towards their feet.
    4. Your opponent's head will be near your hip joint, pointing towards your feet.
    5. Establish a "seatbelt" grip: place your right arm (if your right side is towards their head) across their upper back, hooking their left shoulder/neck area.
    6. Secure your left arm by gripping your own right bicep or wrist, creating a strong cross-face frame against their head and neck.
    7. Drive your hips down and slightly forward, creating pressure through your chest and sternum into their torso.
    8. Keep your knees close to the mat, with your feet tucked slightly behind your hips, maintaining a stable, low base.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "smashing" with your head: This can hyperextend your cervical spine, leading to neck pain or injury. The correct action is to drive your sternum into their torso while using your arms for control.
    • Overextending legs and creating a wide base: This allows your opponent to easily bridge or shrimp, potentially injuring your knees or ankles if they trap them. Keep your base narrow and your knees close to the mat.
    • Letting your opponent's hips "escape" by shrimping: This often happens when your own hips are too high or not driving forward. Maintain constant hip pressure and keep your chest connected to their torso.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo execution: Practice transitioning from side control to North-South, focusing on hip movement and grip placement. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner assisted: Your partner lies passively. You practice moving into North-South, establishing grips, and holding the position. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Light resistance: Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and control points. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled movement: Practice transitioning to North-South and holding for 10 seconds, then releasing. Your partner offers slight pushback. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring: Start from North-South and work to maintain control for 30 seconds. Your partner attempts to escape. (3 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate North-South into your rolling. Aim to achieve and maintain the position for as long as possible. (2 rounds, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have successfully transitioned from side control and your opponent's hips are aligned away from you.
    • After a failed guard pass where you end up in a perpendicular position.
    • When your opponent attempts to shrimp away from side control, and you can "catch" them in the perpendicular alignment.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): From North-South, the defender attempts to shrimp their hips away, creating space to bring their knee inside. This is effective if the attacker's pressure is too high or their base is too wide.
    • Bridging: The defender can attempt a bridge by driving their hips up into the attacker's chest, aiming to create an angle to roll or escape. This works best if the attacker's weight is too far forward.
    • "Bucking" or "Sprawling" the hips: The defender can try to actively push their hips up and away, creating a "bucking" motion to disrupt the attacker's base and create an opening for escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to hold North-South?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are using your head to "smash" your opponent, creating hyperextension. Instead, drive your sternum firmly into their upper abdomen or lower ribcage. Your head should be near their hip, but not actively pressing them down; your arms and chest do the work of control.

    Q: How do I maintain North-South against a bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, focus on the "smash" aspect of your weight distribution. Drive your hips down and slightly forward, creating a heavy pressure through your chest and sternum into their torso. Keep your knees tight to the mat and your base narrow to prevent them from using their size to bridge effectively.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from side control to North-South?

    The optimal time to transition to North-South from Side Control is when your opponent attempts to shrimp away from your pressure. As their hips move away, you "catch" that movement by driving your hips forward and over their torso, aligning yourself perpendicular to them. This prevents them from regaining guard or escaping.
