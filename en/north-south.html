North-South Position: A Biomechanically Precise BJJ Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The North-South position is a dominant control position achieved when your chest is perpendicular to your opponent's torso, and your head is near their hips.

White belts often struggle by giving too much space or collapsing their structure, allowing the opponent to recover guard or escape.

The key is maintaining a tight, perpendicular connection with your hips low and your weight driving forward to break their posture.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, transition your hips so your body is perpendicular to the opponent's torso. Your chest should be aligned with their shoulders.
    2. Place your lead knee on the mat, slightly forward of their hip line, creating a strong base.
    3. Extend your trail leg back, keeping your foot on the mat for stability. Your base should be roughly shoulder-width apart.
    4. Secure a "cross-collar grip" with your lead hand on the opponent's far-side collar, pulling their head towards your chest.
    5. Place your trail hand on the opponent's opposite hip or the mat behind their hip to prevent them from turning into you.
    6. Drive your hips forward and down, keeping your chest pressed into their upper chest/neck area.
    7. Ensure your head is positioned near their hip on the side of your trail leg, creating a "head-and-arm" control.
    8. Maintain constant downward pressure with your hips and chest, preventing them from bridging or turning.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "driving" with your head: This can cause a hyperextension injury to the cervical spine (neck) or strain the trapezius muscles.
    • Correct movement: Maintain a neutral head position, driving with your hips and chest, not your skull.
    • Allowing the opponent to "hip escape" by giving too much space: This leads to your opponent creating frames with their arms or knees, allowing them to recover guard.
    • Correct movement: Keep your hips low and your chest tight against their body, minimizing any gaps.
    • Releasing the cross-collar grip prematurely: This allows the opponent to create space and turn their hips away, making the position unstable.
    • Correct movement: Maintain a firm cross-collar grip throughout the transition and control phase.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo movement: Practice transitioning from side control to North-South without a partner, focusing on hip angle and base. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Static partner drill: With a compliant partner lying on their back, practice achieving North-South from side control, focusing on grips and weight distribution. (20 reps per side, 10% resistance)
    3. Controlled transition drill: Start in side control. Your partner offers minimal resistance as you transition to North-South. Focus on maintaining control. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Positional sparring (defense focus): Start in side control. Your partner attempts to escape guard. You focus on achieving and holding North-South. (10 rounds, 50% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring (offense focus): Start in North-South. You attempt to secure a submission or transition to a better position while your partner defends. (10 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate North-South transitions and control into your rolling sessions, prioritizing positional stability over immediate submissions. (3 rounds, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have successfully passed the opponent's guard and are in side control, looking to improve your control.
    • When the opponent attempts to shrimp or bridge from side control, creating an opportunity to shift your body perpendicular.
    • When you have achieved a dominant position where the opponent's hips are facing away from you.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape to Guard Recovery: If the opponent fails to maintain a tight connection and gives you space, bridge your hips towards them and shrimp your legs to re-establish closed guard.
    • Turtle Escape: If the opponent collapses their weight too much, allowing you to turn onto your hands and knees, you can "turtle up" and attempt to regain guard or initiate a scramble.
    • Upa (Bridge) Counter: If the opponent is applying excessive downward pressure and you feel your spine is being compressed, a well-timed bridge can momentarily create space to escape or transition.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to hold North-South position in BJJ?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are using your head as the primary point of contact and pressure. This can lead to hyperextension or strain. Instead, focus on driving your hips and chest forward, keeping your head in a neutral position near the opponent's hip. Your goal is to use your body weight and structure, not to force their head down with your own.

    Q: How can I maintain North-South position against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, maintaining the perpendicular connection and hip pressure is crucial. Focus on securing a strong cross-collar grip to control their head and prevent them from turning into you. Use your lead knee to anchor yourself near their hip and your trail leg for base. Drive your hips low and forward, making it difficult for their size advantage to translate into leverage.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to North-South from side control in BJJ?

    The ideal time to transition to North-South from Side Control is when your opponent is trying to "shrimp" or bridge away from you. This movement often creates an opening where their hips turn away, allowing you to pivot your body and establish the perpendicular position. Look for moments of slight disconnection to make your shift.
