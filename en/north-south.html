North-South Position: A White Belt's Essential Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

North-South is a dominant control position where you are perpendicular to your opponent's torso, facing their head, with your chest connected to their upper body.

Many white belts struggle because they focus on "holding" rather than "controlling" the opponent's structure and weight.

The key is to create a strong base and use your hips to drive their weight down, preventing them from bridging or turning in.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, transition your hips towards your opponent's head.
    2. Place your lead knee (closest to their head) on the mat, slightly forward of their head.
    3. Drive your chest down onto their upper chest, aiming to make sternum-to-sternum contact.
    4. Extend your lead leg back, creating a stable base with your knee and foot on the mat.
    5. Your trailing leg should be bent, with your foot planted firmly on the mat behind your hips, providing a second anchor.
    6. Grip their far collar with your lead hand, pulling their head slightly towards your chest.
    7. Grip their far sleeve (or wrist if sleeve grip is unavailable) with your trailing hand, controlling their arm's ability to post.
    8. Keep your hips low and drive your weight forward and slightly down, collapsing their structure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly posting your lead knee: This can hyperextend your knee joint, damaging the ACL or MCL if the opponent bridges forcefully.

    The correct movement is to keep a slight bend in the knee and ensure your foot is directly underneath or slightly behind your hip for support.

    • Letting your hips rise too high: This creates a gap, allowing the opponent to escape by shrimping or bridging, and can lead to your own shoulder being exposed to an armbar or kimura.

    The correct action is to maintain a low, driving hip position, making it difficult for them to create space.

    • Grabbing the opponent's head with your arm: This is an ineffective grip that puts your own arm in danger of a guillotine or armbar and doesn't control their structure.

    The correct grip is on the collar, pulling their head into your chest to maintain connection and control.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of hip movement and base setup: 10 reps each side (0% resistance).
    2. Practicing grip acquisition (collar and sleeve) on a stationary dummy or partner: 10 reps (0% resistance).
    3. Partner holds a neutral position (e.g., on hands and knees), you practice establishing North-South: 5 reps each side, 25% resistance.
    4. Partner resists slightly by trying to prevent your chest connection (no bridging): 5 reps each side, 50% resistance.
    5. Partner actively tries to bridge or turn while you hold North-South: 5 reps each side, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Focus on achieving and maintaining North-South for 30 seconds, then transition: 3 rounds, 90-100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully passing the guard and ending up perpendicular to the opponent's head.
    • When an opponent attempts a sweep from guard and you can secure a dominant perpendicular position.
    • As a transition from side-control when the opponent is facing away from you.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Bridge and turn: The opponent bridges their hips up and turns their body towards your chest, creating space to recover guard.
    • Shrimp and recover guard: The opponent shrims their hips away, creating space to bring their legs back between you and establish guard.
    • Hip escape and re-guard: If you are too far away, the opponent can hip escape and re-establish a guard (e.g., half guard or closed guard).

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to get to North-South?

    Your neck might hurt if you are craning your head to look at your opponent or trying to force your head into their space. In North-South, your head should be positioned to the side of their head, facing their shoulder, allowing your chest to make solid contact. Maintain a neutral spine and let your body weight do the work of controlling their structure.

    Q: How do I maintain North-South against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on collapsing their structure by driving their weight down through your hips and sternum. Use your legs to create a wide, stable base. Instead of trying to lift them, aim to pin their shoulders to the mat and control their hips by keeping your own hips low and active, preventing them from using their weight effectively.

    Q: When is the best time to attack submissions from North-South?

    The best time to attack from North-South is when you have established solid control and the opponent is struggling to escape. Common submissions include the North-South choke (by sliding your arm under their head and gripping your own bicep) or transitioning to a Kimura if they defend by posting their arm. Ensure their structure is compromised before committing to a submission to avoid creating escape opportunities.
