This choke is typically initiated from the Closed Guard, aiming to secure a submission by compressing the carotid arteries. It's a powerful finishing tool when applied correctly.

White belts often struggle with this by over-relying on strength or incorrect grip placement, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Understanding the mechanics is key.

The core principle is creating a stable frame with your legs and using your arms to isolate and constrict the neck, leveraging your hips for power.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a deep right cross-collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside the collar, knuckles pointing towards your opponent's sternum.
    2. Place your left hand on your opponent's left hip bone, creating a frame to prevent them from posturing up.
    3. Angle your hips to your right by lifting your left hip slightly off the mat, creating space to slide your right arm deeper under their neck.
    4. As your right arm slides, secure a gable grip or figure-four grip with your left hand on your right forearm, knuckles facing your opponent's shoulder.
    5. Drive your right elbow down towards their sternum, while simultaneously pulling your left forearm towards your body.
    6. Keep your legs tightly locked around their waist, preventing them from escaping the guard or posturing.
    7. Continue to drive your hips forward and slightly up, creating a fulcrum with your chest and upper back to maximize pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Elbow Hyperextension: Extending your right arm too straight and forcefully when establishing the grip can lead to hyperextension of the elbow joint, potentially tearing the ulnar collateral ligament.
    • Neck Strain: Trying to force the choke by craning your neck or pulling your opponent's head down with your arms can cause cervical spine strain or disc compression.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Reaching too far with your right arm without proper hip angling can lead to shoulder impingement as your rotator cuff muscles get pinched between your humerus and glenoid fossa.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip & Frame Drill: Practice securing the right cross-collar grip and left hip frame motion with no resistance (0%). Perform 10 repetitions.
    2. Solo Hip Angle & Arm Slide Drill: Practice the hip angle and sliding the right arm under the neck, followed by the left hand grip, with no resistance (0%). Perform 10 repetitions.
    3. Partnered Isolation Drill (No Resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the full sequence of grips and body positioning without any pressure applied (0%). Focus on smooth transitions. Perform 5 repetitions.
    4. Light Resistance Drill: Partner offers minimal resistance to your arm slide and hip angle. Focus on maintaining your frames and grips (25%). Perform 5 repetitions.
    5. Controlled Application Drill: Partner allows you to establish the choke and apply pressure, but can tap early. Focus on the correct mechanics and hip drive (50%). Perform 5 repetitions.
    6. Live Rolling (Controlled): Attempt the choke during live rolling with a partner who is aware you are practicing it. Focus on timing and execution, not necessarily finishing (75%). Perform 3 attempts or 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up while you are in closed guard.
    • When your opponent is trying to stack you from closed guard, creating an opening for the arm.
    • When your opponent is turtling or trying to escape your closed guard, exposing their neck.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Defending the Grip: If the right cross-collar grip is secured, immediately bring your chin down to your chest and try to peel their right hand off your collar with your own right hand.
    • Breaking the Frame: If they establish the left hip frame, immediately try to shrimp your hips out to the left to break their base and relieve pressure.
    • Escaping the Guard: If the choke is being applied, the primary defense is to break their leg lock by stepping over their head or to their side and escaping to a neutral or dominant position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Baseball Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs from an improper grip on the collar. Ensure your thumb is inside the collar and your knuckles are facing your opponent's sternum, not pointing upwards. Also, avoid over-extending your wrist; maintain a slight bend, allowing your forearm muscles to do the work. If pain persists, re-evaluate your grip placement.

    Q: How can I do the Baseball Choke against a bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is leveraging your hips and creating angles. Focus on a deep cross-collar grip and use your legs to control their posture by squeezing your knees together and driving your hips slightly to the side. The hip angle is crucial for creating the space needed to slide your arm under their neck, and the hip drive will generate the necessary power.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Baseball Choke in BJJ?

    The Baseball Choke is most effective when your opponent is trying to posture up or stack you from your closed guard. These actions often create a momentary opening under their neck for you to slide your arm through. It's also a good option when they are attempting to pass your guard and expose their neck.
