Baseball Choke: A White Belt's Guide to BJJ

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Baseball Choke is a powerful submission initiated from the Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain. It's deceptively simple but requires precise mechanics.

Many white belts fail by muscling the choke or compromising their posture, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Focus on the setup and leverage, not raw strength.

The key insight is using your opponent's posture against them, creating a fulcrum with your forearm to drive the choke home.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a deep cross-collar grip with your right hand on their left lapel, thumb inside the collar.
    2. Simultaneously, secure a second grip with your left hand on the same lapel, just below your right hand, palm facing away from you.
    3. Drive your hips forward, creating a slight angle, so your left hip is closer to their right side.
    4. Lean your weight into their chest, forcing them to post their hands on your thighs.
    5. With your right arm, begin to pull their head down and slightly to your left, creating slack.
    6. Feed your left hand deeper into the collar, ensuring it's snug against their neck.
    7. Extend your right arm forcefully upwards, pivoting your elbow to create a strong fulcrum against their neck, while simultaneously driving your hips into them.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure with your forearm directly across your own carotid artery when trying to force the choke: This can cause dizziness and temporary loss of consciousness, with a risk of a weak grip leading to a fall. Always ensure the choke is applied to the opponent's neck, not your own.
    • Overextending your elbow in the finishing motion: This can lead to elbow joint hyperextension or ligament strain. Focus on driving your hips and pulling with your arms, not just snapping your elbow.
    • Failing to maintain closed guard and allowing the opponent to posture up: This creates a gap, negates the choke's effectiveness, and can lead to your guard being passed, with a risk of knee or hip strain when scrambling. Keep your knees tight and hips engaged.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill grip and hip movement: Practice securing the grips and driving your hips forward without a partner. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill with no resistance: Practice the full sequence with a compliant partner, focusing on smooth transitions and grip placement. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drill with light resistance: Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and adjust your hips. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill with controlled defense: Your partner attempts to defend passively, allowing you to practice breaking posture and securing the choke. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Sparring with focus on position: Attempt the Baseball Choke from closed guard during controlled sparring, prioritizing correct mechanics over submission. (5 attempts, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Baseball Choke into your regular sparring sessions, looking for opportunities and refining your timing and pressure. (3 attempts, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up from your closed guard, creating an opportunity to attack their neck.
    • When you have a strong cross-collar grip and can control their posture.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you from closed guard, opening a lane for the choke.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posture Up and Grip Break: As the choke is being set, the defender can immediately straighten their posture, pulling their chin down and breaking the collar grips with both hands. This relieves pressure and disengages the choke.
    • Hip Escape and Guard Pass: The defender can quickly hip escape to their side, creating space to break the closed guard and initiate a guard pass. This avoids the choke entirely.
    • Shrimp and Re-Guard: If the choke is partially locked, the defender can shrimp their hips away and attempt to recompose guard, either closed or open, to reset the positional battle.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Baseball Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain in the Baseball Choke often stems from gripping the lapel too tightly with your fingers extended or from an incorrect fulcrum point. Ensure your grip is firm but not rigid, and that your finishing motion pivots from your elbow, driving your hips into your opponent to create the pressure, rather than solely relying on wrist flexion.

    Q: How can I effectively use the Baseball Choke against a much larger BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on leverage and posture control rather than brute strength. Secure deep collar grips and use your hips to create angles, forcing them to lean into you. Your finishing motion should be a sharp upward pull with your choking arm while driving your hips forward, using their weight against them to complete the choke.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Baseball Choke, and what if my opponent defends by posturing up?

    The ideal time is when your opponent is trying to posture up from your closed guard. If they posture up to defend, it often creates an opportunity to transition. As they straighten, drive your hips forward and use your cross-collar grip to pull their head down, feeding the other hand deeper to re-establish the choke or transition to another submission if the opening closes.
