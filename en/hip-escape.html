The Hip Escape, or 'shrimping', is a fundamental defensive BJJ technique used to create space and re-establish guard from disadvantageous positions like side-control or Mount.

Many white belts fail because they rely on brute strength or jerky movements, ignoring the core biomechanical principles of leverage and rotation.

The key insight is to use your hips as a lever, rotating your pelvis to 'peel' your opponent's weight off you and slide your body underneath.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from side-control, your goal is to move your hips away from your opponent's chest.
    2. Place your feet flat on the mat, knees bent, with your opponent controlling your hips.
    3. Arch your lower back slightly, creating a small gap at your waist.
    4. Drive your right foot (if opponent is on your left) into the mat, anchoring your base.
    5. Simultaneously, rotate your pelvis to the left, driving your left hip towards your left shoulder.
    6. Extend your left leg, sliding it underneath your opponent's chest as you rotate.
    7. Bring your right knee towards your chest, tucking it in to create a frame against your opponent's hip.
    8. Continue to slide your hips away until you can bring your feet back to the mat and establish guard (e.g., closed-guard).
    9. Your opponent's weight should now be on your hips and knees, not your chest or head.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your legs straight out: This can hyperextend your knees, risking ACL or MCL tears, as your opponent's weight will press down on your straightened limbs.
    • Trying to push with your hands alone: This strains your shoulders and elbows, potentially leading to rotator cuff injuries or dislocations, as you lack a strong base.
    • Rounding your back and lifting your hips too high: This exposes your spine to hyperextension or compression injuries, especially if your opponent applies a stack pass.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Escape (no partner): Practice the full motion of arching, rotating, and sliding for 20 repetitions, focusing on hip movement.
    2. Hip Escape with Light Resistance (0% resistance): Lie on your back, have a partner gently rest their hands on your hips, and perform hip escapes.
    3. Hip Escape to Guard Establishment (25% resistance): From side-control simulation, practice hip escapes until you can establish closed guard, 10 reps per side.
    4. Controlled Hip Escape (50% resistance): Your partner applies light pressure to prevent easy escape; focus on creating space and sliding, 8 reps per side.
    5. Hip Escape with Resistance (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to maintain side-control; you must create space and move, 6 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling Hip Escape (100% resistance): In rolling, actively look for opportunities to hip escape from mount or side-control, aiming for 3 successful escapes per round.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent passes your guard and establishes side-control on your chest or hip.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard from your closed guard and is inside your legs.
    • When you are mounted and your opponent is settled, looking to apply pressure or transition.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • To escape side-control: Drive your foot into the mat, arch your back, and rotate your hips away, sliding your leg underneath your opponent's chest to create space.
    • To re-establish guard from mount: Bring one knee towards your chest while simultaneously shrimping your hips away, creating a frame with your shin to prevent them from settling back down.
    • To create space when your opponent is inside your guard: Arch your back and hip escape, using your foot on their hip as a lever to push them away and bring your knees back to close the guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Hip Escape in BJJ?

    Lower back pain during hip escapes often stems from a lack of core engagement and improper spinal mechanics. Instead of arching your lower back significantly, focus on initiating the movement from your pelvis by tilting it forward and upward. Ensure your feet are planted firmly to provide a stable base, and drive through your heels to assist the rotation. This transfers the force to your hips and glutes, protecting your lumbar spine.

    Q: How can I Hip Escape more effectively against a much larger and stronger BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Do not try to overpower them. Instead, focus on creating small, precise moments of space by arching your back and rotating your hips. Use your foot on their hip as a strong lever, pushing off the mat with your anchored foot to slide your body. The key is to 'peel' their weight off you rather than trying to push their entire body away.

    Q: When is the best time to use a Hip Escape in BJJ, and should I always aim to get my guard back?

    The hip escape is primarily a defensive tool to regain guard or escape disadvantageous positions like side-control or mount. The 'best' time is when you feel your opponent settling in and your space is being compromised. While re-establishing guard is a common goal, sometimes the hip escape is used simply to create enough space to turn and face your opponent or to transition to a better defensive posture, not necessarily to immediately lock up Closed Guard.
