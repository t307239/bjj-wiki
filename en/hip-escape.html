The Essential BJJ Hip Escape for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Hip Escape, or 'Shrimping', is initiated from a defensive position like being flattened on your back. It creates space to regain guard or improve your position.

Many white belts fail by pushing with their hands or lifting their hips too high, losing their base and creating openings.

The core insight is to pivot on your supporting foot and drive your hips away, creating a 'curving' motion rather than a straight push.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start on your back, knees bent, feet flat on the mat, facing your opponent. Your opponent is typically in your closed guard or on top of you.
    2. Bend your knees towards your chest, creating a slight frame with your forearms against your opponent's upper chest or shoulders, if possible.
    3. Plant your left foot firmly on the mat, toes pointed slightly outwards. This is your pivot point.
    4. Simultaneously, lift your hips slightly off the mat, just enough to clear your right hip from the mat's surface.
    5. Drive your left foot into the mat and rotate your pelvis counter-clockwise, pivoting on your left heel.
    6. Extend your right leg out and to the left, creating space between your hips and your opponent's body.
    7. Bring your right knee towards your chest as you continue to pivot, tucking it to help bring your left leg back underneath you.
    8. Plant your right foot on the mat and re-establish your base, now facing a slightly different angle, ready to move to guard or another defensive posture.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly pushing with hands: This can lead to wrist sprains or hyperextension of the elbow joint if the opponent applies pressure.
    • Lifting hips too high: This compromises your base, risking a knee hyperextension if you land awkwardly or if the opponent stacks you.
    • Straight leg extension: A straight leg extension can strain the hamstring or cause a groin pull if the movement is not controlled and coordinated with the hip pivot.
    • Trying to 'jump' sideways: This lacks a clear pivot point and can lead to ankle sprains or uncontrolled falls.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Escapes: Lie on your back, practice the hip escape motion 20 times per side, focusing on the pivot and hip movement (0% resistance).
    2. Partnered Hip Escapes (No Resistance): Your partner lies on top of you, unresisting. Perform 10 hip escapes, focusing on creating space (25% resistance).
    3. Partnered Hip Escapes (Light Pressure): Your partner applies light downward pressure with their hands on your shoulders. Perform 10 hip escapes, feeling the need to generate hip power (50% resistance).
    4. Guard Retention Drills: Your partner attempts to pass guard from the top. Execute hip escapes to maintain or re-establish guard for 5 minutes (75% resistance).
    5. Specific Position Escapes: Start from side control or mount. Your partner maintains the position while you practice hip escapes to create space for recovery (90% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling (Positional Sparring): Focus on using hip escapes to escape bad positions for 3-minute rounds (100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent has achieved a dominant position like side control or mount and is flattening you out.
    • When your opponent is inside your closed guard and you need to create space to open it or transition.
    • When you are caught in a submission attempt and need to create immediate distance to defend.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Recovery: After a successful hip escape, immediately bring your knees to your chest and work to re-establish your guard (e.g., closed guard).
    • Framing and Posturing: Use your forearms to create frames against your opponent's hips or shoulders to prevent them from closing the distance again.
    • Transition to a Better Position: Use the created space to transition to a more advantageous defensive position like half-guard or to initiate your own sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to hip escape in BJJ?

    Lower back pain often stems from trying to push with your hands or arch your back excessively instead of pivoting. The correct movement involves rotating your pelvis and driving off your planted foot. Focus on keeping your core engaged and using your hips and legs as the primary movers, not your lumbar spine.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, the key is leveraging your hips more efficiently. Instead of just pushing, think of 'curving' your body away. Ensure your supporting foot is firmly planted and use your planted foot's heel to drive and pivot your hips. Maximize the space created by extending your free leg fully.

    Q: No, the hip escape is not a straight line movement. It's a dynamic, curved motion. You pivot on one foot, drive your hips away, and bring your knees towards your chest. The goal is to create a 'C' shape with your body to maximize distance and avoid moving directly into their pressure.
