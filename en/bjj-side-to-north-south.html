    More Questions

    How do I transition from side control to north-south without losing my position?

    The key is to maintain chest-to-chest pressure and keep your hips low. As you rotate, ensure your shoulder stays connected to your opponent's neck or shoulder to prevent them from bridging or turning into you.

    What are the common mistakes beginners make when going for north-south?

    A common mistake is lifting your hips too high, which can create space for your opponent to escape. Another error is not maintaining head control, allowing them to turn their head and create an angle to relieve pressure.

    What are the advantages of reaching the north-south position from side control?

    North-south offers excellent control and opens up various submission opportunities like kimuras, armbars, and chokes. It also allows you to transition to other dominant positions if your opponent defends effectively.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent always escape my Side Control when I try to transition to North-South position?

    Your opponent likely escapes because you are not properly controlling their hips and shoulders during the transition. Ensure your chest stays heavy on their sternum and your hip on their hip, creating a strong base, while your shoulder drives into their far shoulder to prevent them from turning in.

    Q: How can I effectively transition from Side Control to North-South position without losing control of a bigger opponent?

    To maintain control against a larger opponent, focus on creating leverage by driving your hips forward and down, pinning their shoulder with your own. Simultaneously, use your far arm to hook their far arm or shoulder, preventing them from bridging or creating space.

    Q: My knees get tangled and I feel awkward when I try to switch from Side Control to North-South, what am I doing wrong?

    Your knees are likely getting tangled because you're not keeping your base tight and mobile. As you transition, keep your base leg (the one closer to their head) extended and anchored, while your other leg pivots around your opponent's hips, maintaining a low center of gravity and preventing your legs from crossing.
