Stand In Base is a crucial transition from a compromised guard position, usually Closed Guard, to a stable, upright stance ready for takedowns or sweeps.

White belts often rush this, forgetting the core principle of maintaining connection and leverage.

The key is to use your opponent's weight against them, creating an unbalancing moment as you elevate yourself.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure collar grip with your right hand, index finger inside the opponent's gi collar, palm facing upwards.
    2. Simultaneously, secure sleeve grip with your left hand on the opponent's right sleeve, near the wrist, palm facing downwards.
    3. Place your left knee on the mat, directly beneath your left hip, creating a stable tripod with your left foot and right foot.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly to the right, aligning your pelvis over your left knee.
    5. Begin to elevate your right leg, pushing off the mat with your right foot, keeping your shin vertical.
    6. Simultaneously, pull down and slightly across with your collar grip, and pull your opponent's sleeve towards your body with your sleeve grip.
    7. As you stand, keep your base wide, with your feet shoulder-width apart, and maintain a slight forward lean to absorb opponent's weight.
    8. Ensure your head is up and your eyes are looking forward, scanning for your opponent's reactions.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your knee straight out from your hip while trying to stand: This can cause anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears due to shear forces on the knee joint.

    Correct: Keep your supporting knee bent and directly beneath your hip, creating a stable base.

    • Gripping too loosely on the sleeve or collar: This allows the opponent to easily break your grip and regain control, potentially leading to a sweep or pass attempt.

    Correct: Maintain firm, precise grips with your thumb wrapped around for maximum control.

    • Attempting to stand too upright too quickly without establishing a base: This puts excessive pressure on your lower back and can lead to lumbar strains or disc injuries.

    Correct: Focus on driving your hips forward and using your legs to support your weight before fully standing.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip placement and hip movement without a partner (10 reps).
    2. Solo drill: Practice the full Stand In Base motion, focusing on leg drive and hip elevation, visualizing an opponent (10 reps).
    3. Partner drill: With a compliant partner lying on their back, practice the grip and hip drive to get to your knees (25% resistance, 5 reps).
    4. Partner drill: Progress to standing up fully from the initial position, with the partner offering minimal resistance (50% resistance, 5 reps).
    5. Partner drill: Introduce light, controlled pulling from the partner as you attempt Stand In Base (75% resistance, 5 reps).
    6. Live rolling: Attempt Stand In Base transition during sparring, focusing on maintaining base and control (100% resistance, 2-3 attempts per round).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When an opponent has established a strong closed guard and you want to break their control to pass.
    • When the opponent attempts a sweep from closed guard and you need to regain a neutral or advantageous standing position.
    • As a setup to a takedown when you have secured a dominant grip and feel the opponent's weight distribution is favorable.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Opponent attempts to pull you back down: Maintain your grips and drive your hips forward and down, using your legs to absorb the pull and transition to a knee-on-belly or side control.
    • Opponent tries to sprawl their legs: Immediately transition to a leg drag grip or double-under pass setup by driving your hips low and forward.
    • Opponent attempts a triangle choke during the transition: Keep your posture tall and drive your head into their chest, using your collar grip to control their head and break the choke before it fully forms.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Stand In Base from closed guard?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are not keeping your supporting knee directly underneath your hip when you drive up. This misalignment places excessive shear force on your knee ligaments, especially the ACL. Ensure your supporting leg's knee is stacked directly below your hip, forming a stable column of support.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Focus on securing deep collar and sleeve grips to control their posture. Drive your hips forward and into them as you elevate, using their own weight against them. Do not try to muscle them; instead, find the precise moment their weight is shifted and exploit it with a sharp, controlled upward movement.

    Q: You should stop attempting Stand In Base if the opponent has established dominant grips that prevent you from breaking their guard cleanly, or if they are actively countering your attempts to stand. If you cannot achieve a stable base quickly, it's safer to disengage the Stand In Base attempt and revert to a more controlled guard pass strategy to avoid being swept or submitted.
