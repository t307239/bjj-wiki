Stand In Base: The White Belt's Biomechanically Sound Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Stand In Base is a foundational transition from a guard position to a stable, upright stance, typically used to initiate takedowns or re-establish guard.

White belts often rush this, losing balance and creating openings for their opponent's attacks.

The key is controlled weight transfer, utilizing your base to support your upward movement without exposing your joints.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on the opponent's left sleeve with your left hand.
    2. Place your left foot flat on the opponent's hip, knee pointing slightly outwards, creating a stable base.
    3. Simultaneously, drive your right hip upwards, initiating the upward movement and initiating a slight hip angle away from the opponent.
    4. Begin to bring your right knee forward and inward, aiming to place it on the mat between your opponent's legs.
    5. As your right knee touches the mat, shift your weight forward, transferring it onto your left foot and right knee.
    6. Drive your hips forward and upward, using your left leg as a lever and your right knee as a pivot point on the mat.
    7. Once upright, maintain a wide base with your feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, and hips tucked under to absorb potential impacts.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your legs during the upward drive can hyperextend your knee joints, risking ACL or MCL tears. Instead, keep a slight bend in both knees as you rise.
    • Attempting to stand up by pushing off the opponent's shoulders with your hands can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears if they push back. Always prioritize pushing off their hips and using your legs for elevation.
    • Failing to maintain hip connection and allowing your hips to drift away can create a large gap, exposing your lower back to hyperextension if the opponent attempts to stack you. Keep your hips tucked and close to their body as you ascend.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the entire movement pattern without a partner, focusing on smooth transitions and controlled weight shifts. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (passive): Your partner lies on their back in closed guard. You perform the Stand In Base sequence, and they offer no resistance. (25 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance by lightly gripping your sleeves or collar. Focus on breaking their grips and executing the stand. (20 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Guard retention focus: Partner attempts to maintain closed guard as you transition. You focus on breaking their guard and standing. (15 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Takedown initiation: Partner is on their back. You Stand In Base and immediately transition to a double leg takedown attempt (no completion). (10 reps, 75% resistance)
    6. Controlled rolling: Implement Stand In Base in live rolling scenarios, aiming to use it to initiate a takedown or re-establish guard. (5 rounds, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is holding a tight closed guard and you intend to pass or create distance for a takedown.
    • When your opponent attempts a sweep from closed guard and you want to defend by standing.
    • When you want to transition from guard to a more dominant standing position to initiate a wrestling-style attack.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • If opponent attempts Stand In Base, immediately hip escape to the side and shrimp your hips to create space and prevent them from establishing a stable base. Then, re-establish your guard or attempt a sweep.
    • If opponent stands and you are still in their guard, immediately attempt a scissor sweep by extending your legs and sweeping their base. Alternatively, use your hands to push their knees apart and attempt to break their posture.
    • If opponent stands and you are in a position where they can pass, immediately attempt to re-guard by shooting your hips back and closing the distance, or use your hands to push their hips away and create space to recover your guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my left knee hurt when I try to Stand In Base from closed guard?

    This often happens if you are pushing off your opponent's shoulders with your hands instead of driving your hips up and off their hips. When you push their shoulders, your body weight can force your knee into an unnatural, hyperextended position. Focus on using your legs to drive your hips upward and keep a slight bend in your knee as it comes forward.

    Q: How can I safely Stand In Base against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, maintaining a strong base and controlled weight transfer is paraMount. Ensure your initial foot placement on their hip is solid, and drive your hips up with intent. Avoid trying to 'muscle' your way up; instead, use the leverage of your legs and the angle of your hips to create upward momentum. If they resist heavily, it might be safer to break their guard and transition to a different position rather than forcing the stand.

    Q: I keep getting swept when I try to Stand In Base, what am I doing wrong biomechanically?

    You are likely not maintaining sufficient connection or hip angle as you ascend. If your hips drift away from your opponent's body as you stand, you lose your base and create an easy angle for them to sweep. Ensure your hips remain tucked and close to their body throughout the entire upward motion, using your legs to drive you vertically rather than pushing horizontally.
