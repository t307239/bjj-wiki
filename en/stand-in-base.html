Stand In Base is a crucial transition from a grounded position, typically guard or half-guard, to a stable, upright stance. It allows you to reset, improve your position, or prepare for takedowns.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and proper framing, leading to off-balance situations and potential injury.

The core mechanical insight is creating a stable tripod with your hands and feet, distributing your weight evenly over these points to become unmovable.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a deep half-guard or closed guard position. Place both hands flat on the mat, fingers spread wide, directly beneath your shoulders.
    2. Bring your knees towards your chest, tucking your hips under your body to shorten your lever.
    3. Shift your weight onto your hands and feet, lifting your hips slightly off the mat.
    4. Ensure your knees are angled slightly forward, creating a narrow base.
    5. Keep your elbows tight to your body, preventing them from flaring outwards.
    6. Maintain a neutral spine, avoiding excessive arching or rounding.
    7. Gradually extend your legs, pushing your hips back and up to achieve a stable, upright base.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Elbow Flare: Placing hands too far forward or wide causes elbows to flare out. This compromises shoulder joint integrity, leading to potential rotator cuff tears or labral damage when force is applied.
    • Hyperextended Knees: Pushing legs straight without a slight bend puts excessive stress on the knee ligaments, risking ACL or MCL tears, especially if an opponent drives into your legs.
    • Rounded Back: Collapsing the spine when lifting hips strains the lumbar erector muscles and intervertebral discs, potentially causing a disc bulge or muscle strain.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Lift: Practice lifting hips and holding a stable base for 10 seconds. Repeat 10 times (0% resistance).
    2. Solo Stand-Up: From a grounded position, practice the full stand-in-base motion without a partner. Repeat 10 times (0% resistance).
    3. Partner Assisted Lift (No Pressure): Partner gently holds your legs. Practice lifting your hips and standing up. Repeat 5 reps per side (25% resistance).
    4. Partner Assisted Stand-Up: Partner provides light pressure on your hips. Focus on maintaining your base and standing up. Repeat 5 reps per side (50% resistance).
    5. Controlled Stand-Up: Partner attempts to prevent your stand-up with moderate pressure. Focus on stability and controlled movement. Repeat 5 reps per side (75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling Integration: Apply Stand In Base during live rolling when opportunities arise, focusing on maintaining a strong base. Perform 3 rounds (90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your guard is being passed and you need to reset to a neutral standing position.
    • When you are in a compromised position like half-guard and want to create space to establish a better guard or stand up.
    • When your opponent is attempting a submission from a static position and you need to create a stable base to defend or escape.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Bump to Stand: If an opponent is pressing heavily into your hips while you attempt to stand, execute a hip bump sweep. As they recoil, use that momentum to stand.
    • Guard Pull to Stand: If you are in open guard and your opponent is posturing up, pull them forward into your guard, then use their momentum to initiate a Stand In Base.
    • Knee Tap to Stand: If an opponent is holding your legs low, tap one knee to the mat while simultaneously driving the other foot into the mat to push off, creating a lever to stand.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to Stand In Base in BJJ?

    Wrist pain typically stems from improper weight distribution. If your hands are too far forward or too wide, your body weight is concentrated on a small area of your wrist, causing hyperextension or shear forces. Ensure your hands are directly under your shoulders, fingers spread wide for maximum surface area, and your elbows are tucked to engage your forearms and protect your wrists.

    Q: How do I Stand In Base effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is to shorten your lever and create a solid tripod base before they can effectively use their weight. Tuck your knees tightly to your chest, place your hands directly beneath your shoulders, and lift your hips. Focus on distributing your weight evenly across your hands and feet, making yourself a heavy, immovable object before they can generate significant downward pressure.

    Q: When is the best time to use Stand In Base in BJJ, and what if my opponent immediately tries to pass?

    Stand In Base is most effective as a defensive reset when you feel a Guard Pass is imminent or when you've lost dominant positioning. If your opponent immediately attempts a pass, your strong, stable base should make their initial push ineffective. You can then use the momentum of their pass attempt to either transition to a better position or use a counter like a hip bump to regain control.
