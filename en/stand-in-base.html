Stand In Base typically begins from a seated or kneeling position, often after a sweep or when transitioning from guard. Its purpose is to establish a stable, upright posture to avoid being swept or to initiate a takedown.

White belts often fail because they rush the process, leading to a compromised base and vulnerability to sweeps or positional advancement by the opponent.

The key mechanical insight is creating a wide, stable base by widening your stance and lowering your center of gravity before fully committing to standing.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or kneeling guard recovery, establish two-on-one grips on the opponent's sleeves or biceps, pulling them slightly forward to break their posture.
    2. Shift your weight onto your feet, ensuring your knees are bent and your hips are slightly tucked.
    3. Widen your foot stance to at least shoulder-width, aligning your feet roughly parallel or slightly angled outward.
    4. Simultaneously, begin to drive your hips downwards, lowering your center of gravity.
    5. As your hips descend, drive your knees outward, creating a stable tripod base with your feet and hips.
    6. Maintain a neutral spine, avoiding rounding or arching.
    7. Keep your head up and eyes forward, scanning for your opponent's reactions and potential threats.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Standing up too quickly with feet too close together: This compromises your base, making your ankles, knees, and hips vulnerable to hyperextension or twisting injuries if the opponent attempts a sweep.
    • Rounding your back while attempting to stand: This puts excessive strain on your lumbar spine and makes you unstable, risking a back injury and easy reversal.
    • Overextending your knees: This can lead to ligament damage (ACL, MCL) as your knees are not supported by a stable base and can buckle under pressure.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the footwork and hip descent motion without a partner. Focus on achieving a wide, stable base. (10 reps)
    2. Solo drill with light resistance: Use a training dummy or pillow to simulate pulling on sleeves as you perform the Stand In Base motion. (10 reps)
    3. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice Stand In Base from a seated position. Partner offers no resistance. (20 reps)
    4. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner gently attempts to push you back down or shift their weight as you stand. Focus on maintaining base. (20 reps)
    5. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent you from standing or attempts a light sweep as you establish base. (30 reps)
    6. Live rolling (75-90% resistance): Apply Stand In Base in a rolling scenario, focusing on maintaining stability against a resisting opponent. (5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After successfully executing a sweep and the opponent is on their back.
    • When recovering guard and needing to establish a stable upright position.
    • When transitioning from a guard pass and the opponent attempts to re-guard.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • The Hip Bump Sweep: If the opponent attempts Stand In Base, immediately drive your hips into their base while simultaneously bumping your shoulder into their chest, creating an angle to sweep.
    • The Knee Tap: As the opponent begins to stand, quickly reach your arm across and tap their knee closest to you, driving it to the mat to disrupt their base and potentially sweep.
    • The Guard Pull: If you see an opponent attempting to Stand In Base from a distance, you can quickly pull guard yourself, bringing them back into your guard and negating their attempt.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Stand In Base in BJJ?

    This pain is often caused by rounding your spine and lifting with your back muscles instead of using your legs and hips. To fix this, focus on keeping your spine neutral and driving your hips down and forward as you widen your stance. Imagine pushing the floor away with your feet and glutes, not just pulling yourself up with your back.

    Q: How do I effectively Stand In Base against a much bigger and heavier BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, emphasize creating a wider base and a lower center of gravity. Use strong two-on-one grips on their sleeves to control their posture and prevent them from using their weight effectively. Drive your hips down significantly and ensure your knees are driving outward to create a solid, unmovable platform before you attempt to stand fully.

    Q: Is it better to Stand In Base with my knees pointing outwards or forwards?

    For a stable Stand In Base, your knees should point slightly outwards, in line with your toes. This alignment creates a strong tripod base with your feet and hips, distributing weight effectively and protecting your knee joints. Pointing knees directly forward can limit your base width and increase stress on the knee ligaments.
