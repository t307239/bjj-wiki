Stand In Base is a transition from a guard position, typically closed or open guard, to a stable standing base. It allows you to initiate takedowns or create space safely.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or incorrect weight shifts, leading to instability and vulnerability.

The key mechanical insight is maintaining a strong posterior chain engagement while actively pushing the opponent's base away with your legs.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure grips on the opponent's gi pants, just above the knees, using a gable grip with your hands.
    2. Simultaneously, place your feet flat on the opponent's hips, knees slightly bent, creating a wide, stable base.
    3. Begin to extend your legs, pushing the opponent's hips directly away from you, creating space.
    4. As your legs extend, sit up and shift your weight onto your posterior chain, keeping your back straight and core engaged.
    5. Drive your hips forward and slightly down, creating a powerful lever with your legs.
    6. Simultaneously, begin to stand up by driving through your heels, maintaining tension on the pant grips.
    7. Keep your base wide and knees tracking over your toes as you rise, ensuring a stable, upright posture.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Lifting your hips too high before extending your legs: This can hyperextend the lumbar spine, risking a lower back disc injury. The correct movement is to push the opponent's hips away first, then sit up.
    • Gripping too low on the opponent's pants (e.g., ankles): This provides insufficient leverage and can lead to shoulder or elbow hyperextension when the opponent pulls back. Grip higher, above the knee, for optimal control.
    • Standing up too quickly without establishing base: This often results in the opponent falling forward or pulling you down, potentially causing knee or ankle sprains. Ensure your feet are firmly planted and your weight is balanced before standing.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg extension and hip drive motion without a partner, focusing on hip mobility and posterior chain activation. (50 reps)
    2. Partnered drilling (no resistance): Your partner stands passively. Execute the Stand In Base, focusing on grip placement and weight transfer. (20 reps)
    3. Partnered drilling (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the push and pull. Focus on maintaining your base. (30 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Partnered drilling (controlled resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent you from standing, but not with full force. Work on breaking their posture and completing the stand. (40 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Live drilling (positional sparring): Start from closed guard and attempt Stand In Base, with partner defending normally. Focus on successful transitions. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Implement Stand In Base during regular sparring, aiming for successful transitions to standing. (3 rounds, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating an opportunity to create space.
    • When you need to transition from guard to a standing base to initiate a takedown or reset.
    • When the opponent is leaning heavily forward, making them susceptible to a stand-up.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Sweep (e.g., Harai Goshi): As you extend your legs, the opponent can counter by stepping their leg back and sweeping yours. Maintain your balance by keeping your weight low and your supporting leg strong.
    • Posture Control Re-establishment: If the opponent realizes you are attempting Stand In Base, they may try to pull you forward or drop their weight. Counter by tightening your grips and using your legs to push their hips away, preventing them from pulling you off balance.
    • Guard Pass Attempt: If you fail to establish a solid base, the opponent might try to pass your guard. Be ready to re-establish your guard or transition to a different defensive position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Stand In Base in BJJ?

    Lower back pain during Stand In Base often stems from hyperextending your spine rather than using your legs and posterior chain. You might be lifting your hips too high before pushing your opponent's hips away. Focus on pushing their hips straight out first, then sitting up and driving your hips forward, engaging your glutes and hamstrings. This transfers the force through your legs and core, not your lower back.

    Q: How can I use Stand In Base effectively against a much bigger and stronger BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Instead of trying to muscle them, focus on precise biomechanics. Secure deep grips on their pants above the knees. Use your feet on their hips to create a powerful, controlled push, driving their base away. As you stand, keep your hips low and drive through your heels, maintaining a wide, stable base. Think of it as using their weight against them by creating a fulcrum.

    Q: The optimal time to attempt Stand In Base from closed guard is when your opponent is postured up and their weight is forward, making them less stable. Look for moments when they are reaching down to break your guard or when they are leaning in. Avoid attempting it when they are fully squared up and their weight is directly over their hips, as this will be much harder to break their base.
