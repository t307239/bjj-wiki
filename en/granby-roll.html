The Granby Roll: A White Belt's Essential BJJ Transition

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Granby Roll is a fundamental transition used to escape bad positions or advance to better ones from the ground. It's often employed when stuck in Side Control or a vulnerable guard position.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of body mechanics, leading to awkward movements and potential injury. They might over-rely on arm strength rather than hip and shoulder rotation.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to initiate the rotation and drive your shoulders through, creating momentum and space, rather than simply trying to 'roll over'.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, establish a strong underhook with your lead arm, gripping the opponent's far hip crease with your hand. Your trail arm should frame against their hip or shoulder.
    2. Angle your hips to be perpendicular to your opponent, creating a slight space between your torso and theirs.
    3. Drive your trail leg (the one further from the opponent's head) slightly forward, planting your foot flat on the mat.
    4. Initiate the roll by driving your lead shoulder (the one with the underhook) down and forward, tucking your chin to your chest.
    5. Simultaneously, lift your hips off the mat, using your planted trail leg as a pivot point.
    6. As your body rotates, extend your trail arm, pushing off the mat for added momentum and to create space.
    7. Continue the rotation, aiming to bring your hips over your head, finishing in a shrimp or technical stand-up position, depending on the context.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect neck tuck and shoulder drive: Leads to direct impact on the cervical spine (neck) and potential whiplash injury. The correct movement involves tucking the chin firmly to the chest and driving the shoulder in a controlled, rolling motion.
    • Over-extension of the trail arm: Pushing off the mat with a locked, straight arm can cause hyperextension of the elbow joint and strain the ulnar collateral ligament. The trail arm should be used to guide the rotation and push with a slightly bent elbow.
    • Allowing the head to lead the roll: If the head leads without proper shoulder and hip engagement, the spine can twist unnaturally, risking lumbar or thoracic injury. The roll must be initiated by the shoulder drive and hip elevation.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Rotation Drill: Lie on your back, tuck your chin, and practice initiating the hip-driven shoulder roll without any movement. Focus on hip angle and shoulder drive. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Solo Granby Roll with Mat Push: Perform the Granby Roll motion, focusing on the trail arm push-off from the mat. Ensure a full rotation. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    3. Partnered Granby Roll (No Resistance): With a partner lying on their side, practice the Granby Roll escape from side control. Partner remains passive. (20 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partnered Granby Roll (Light Resistance): Partner applies minimal pressure. Focus on creating space with your frames and driving the roll. (20 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Partnered Granby Roll (Moderate Resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the roll by maintaining pressure. Focus on the biomechanics of escaping their weight. (15 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling Application: Attempt the Granby Roll during rolling when you find yourself in a compromised position. Focus on successful transition and maintaining control. (As opportunities arise, 90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When trapped in side control with the opponent's weight heavily on your chest and hips.
    • When your opponent has a strong cross-face and is flattening you out.
    • As a means to escape a bad guard retention situation and regain guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The 'Sit-Up' Counter: As the white belt initiates the roll, the opponent quickly sits up and brings their knees towards their chest, creating a tight defensive shell. This prevents the rolling motion by blocking hip and shoulder space.
    • The 'Knee-on-Ground' Counter: The opponent places their knee firmly on the mat on the side of the rolling person's head. This acts as an anchor, preventing the roll and allowing them to maintain control or transition to a better position.
    • The 'Frame and Push' Counter: The opponent uses their arms to frame against the rolling person's hips and shoulders, actively pushing them away and disrupting the rolling momentum. This requires good grip fighting and weight distribution.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt after attempting the Granby Roll?

    Your neck likely hurts because you are not tucking your chin to your chest properly before initiating the roll. This causes your head to hit the mat first or your neck to bend at an awkward angle. Ensure your chin is glued to your sternum, and your shoulders lead the rotation, not your head.

    Q: How can I use the Granby Roll against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and momentum become even more critical. Focus on creating even a small aMount of space with your frames before initiating the roll. Use your hips to drive your shoulders through their weight, rather than trying to muscle your way out. The trail arm push-off is vital for generating the necessary momentum.

    Q: Not necessarily. The primary goal of the Granby Roll is to escape a compromised position and improve your situation. Depending on the opponent's reaction and your own body mechanics, you might end up on your hands and knees, in a shrimp position, or even in a position to attempt a sweep or submission. Prioritize regaining a more advantageous position.
