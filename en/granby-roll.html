Granby Roll: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Granby Roll is a dynamic transition used primarily from bottom positions like Closed Guard or half guard to escape pressure or improve your position.

White belts often rush this move, creating uncontrolled momentum and high injury risk instead of using precise body mechanics.

The key is generating forward momentum through hip extension and a controlled shoulder rotation, not brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start in closed guard, hips elevated slightly off the mat. Maintain a strong grip on your opponent's sleeves, specifically at the wrist.
    2. Simultaneously, use your hands to push off your opponent's hips or thighs, creating a small amount of space.
    3. Initiate a powerful hip extension, driving your hips upwards and slightly forward, aligning your pelvis towards your head.
    4. As your hips extend, tuck your chin to your chest and begin to rotate your shoulders away from the direction of your hips' movement.
    5. Keep your arms tight to your body, elbows bent at approximately 90 degrees, to protect your shoulder joints.
    6. Drive your lead shoulder (the one rotating away from your hips) downwards and forward, leading the roll.
    7. Continue the forward momentum, allowing your body to roll over your lead shoulder and tuck your knees towards your chest.
    8. Aim to land on your feet with your opponent still trapped between your legs or in a position to immediately transition to a sweep or pass.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to roll by flinging your head and neck forward without hip extension: This can lead to cervical spine hyperextension or compression, causing whiplash or disc injury. Instead, tuck your chin to your chest and lead with your shoulder after hip extension.
    • Rolling with arms fully extended and rigid: This places excessive stress on the shoulder joint and elbow ligaments, risking dislocations or tears. Keep your elbows bent at 90 degrees to absorb impact and maintain joint integrity.
    • Trying to roll by pushing off the opponent's knees with your hands: This often results in ineffective momentum and can lead to wrist sprains or fractures if the opponent is heavy or resists. Use your hands on the opponent's hips or thighs for a more stable base.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Extension & Shoulder Rotation Drill: Practice the hip extension and shoulder tuck motion without an opponent. Focus on the feeling of generating power from the hips. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Granby Roll on Soft Surface: Perform the full Granby Roll on a thick mat or crash pad, focusing on the tuck and rotation. (30 reps)
    3. Partnered Granby Roll (No Resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the Granby Roll from closed guard, focusing on precise grips and hip drive. (20 reps each side)
    4. Partnered Granby Roll (Light Resistance): Partner applies minimal pressure to your hips. Execute the Granby Roll, feeling the need to generate more hip power. (20 reps each side)
    5. Granby Roll Against Basic Guard Pass Attempt: Partner attempts a simple guard pass (e.g., knee slice). Use the Granby Roll to escape. (10 reps each side)
    6. Live Rolling Scenario (Controlled): Implement the Granby Roll during controlled rolling when your guard is being passed or you are in a disadvantageous position. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent establishes a strong knee-on-belly or other pressure pass, threatening to flatten your guard.
    • When opponent is posturing up in your closed guard and you want to create space to transition to open guard.
    • When you feel your guard is about to be completely passed and you need a dynamic escape.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Bump Sweep Counter: If the opponent overcommits to a pass and leaves their weight forward, use your hips to bump them offline as you initiate the Granby Roll, turning their momentum against them.
    • Stalling the Roll: If the opponent anticipates the Granby Roll and plants their weight heavily, use your arms to frame against their hips and prevent the roll, transitioning to a sweep instead.
    • Re-guarding: If the Granby Roll doesn't fully escape the pressure, immediately focus on re-establishing guard by bringing your knees to your chest and framing against the opponent's hips.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt after attempting a Granby Roll?

    Neck pain after a Granby Roll usually stems from leading the roll with your head instead of your hips and shoulders. When you tuck your chin to your chest and drive your hips forward, your cervical spine is protected. If you throw your head forward without this core engagement, you risk hyperextension or compression of the neck.

    Q: How can I use the Granby Roll effectively against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, the Granby Roll relies even more on precise timing and hip extension rather than pure strength. Focus on creating a small space by pushing their hips away, then explosively extending your hips upwards. Use their weight against you by rotating over your shoulder quickly, aiming to get your hips under their center of gravity.

    Q: Is the Granby Roll a sweep or a pass?

    The Granby Roll is primarily a transition technique, not a sweep or a pass itself. Its main purpose is to escape a bad position or to reposition yourself to initiate a sweep or pass. You might end up in a position where you can immediately attack for a sweep, or you might use it to create space to establish a better guard.
