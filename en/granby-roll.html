The Granby Roll is a forward somersault used to transition between positions, most commonly when escaping a bad spot or creating space. It's often initiated from a bottom position like Side Control or mount.

White belts often struggle with the Granby Roll due to a fear of forward movement and a lack of understanding of the necessary body mechanics. This leads to stiff, ineffective attempts that increase injury risk.

The key to a successful Granby Roll lies in using your hips and shoulders to create a rotation, rather than just flailing your limbs. Think of it as a controlled, forward-rolling motion initiated by your core.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a bottom position (e.g., being flattened out in side control), establish a strong base by framing with your forearms against your opponent's hips or chest. Ensure your elbows are tucked.
    2. Initiate the roll by tucking your chin to your chest, creating a rounded shape. Simultaneously, drive your hips slightly forward and to the side you intend to roll.
    3. Begin the forward momentum by pushing off your opponent's hips with your framing arms. Keep your core engaged to maintain body control.
    4. As you initiate the roll, actively drive your shoulder opposite the direction of the roll into the mat. This acts as the pivot point for your rotation.
    5. Keep your legs tucked towards your chest, forming a compact ball. This minimizes the surface area for your opponent to grab and aids in faster rotation.
    6. Continue the rolling motion, allowing your body to naturally complete the somersault. Aim to land on your knees or in a combat base.
    7. Immediately as you complete the roll, re-establish your base and look to secure a dominant position or create immediate space by framing again.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting the Granby Roll by simply flailing your arms and legs without a controlled tuck of the chin and core engagement. This can lead to hyperextension of the neck and cervical spine injury.
    • Rolling with an unengaged core and hips, causing your body to become too flat and wide. This exposes your back and limbs to attacks like the rear-naked choke or leg locks, and can result in shoulder or hip joint sprains.
    • Not tucking the chin to the chest, leading to an uncontrolled head movement during the roll. This can cause whiplash injuries or concussions, especially if the head strikes the mat awkwardly.
    • Trying to force the roll with excessive upper body strength instead of using hip and shoulder drive. This strains the shoulders and elbows and often results in incomplete, awkward rolls that leave you in a worse position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the tuck, chin-to-chest, and hip drive motion on a soft mat without rolling. Focus on core engagement. (50 reps)
    2. Solo drill: Perform the full Granby Roll movement on a soft mat, focusing on a tight tuck and smooth, controlled rotation. (50 reps)
    3. Partner drill (0% resistance): From a static position (e.g., opponent lightly holding your arms), practice initiating the Granby Roll and completing the rotation to your knees. (20 reps each side)
    4. Partner drill (25% resistance): Opponent is in a passive side control. You initiate the Granby Roll to escape to your knees. Opponent offers minimal resistance to the roll. (20 reps each side)
    5. Partner drill (50% resistance): Opponent is in a more active side control, attempting to maintain pressure. You execute the Granby Roll to create space and transition. (15 reps each side)
    6. Live rolling (75% resistance): Integrate the Granby Roll as a transitional escape when caught in a bad position. Focus on timing and execution under light pressure. (5 minutes, focus on escape)
    7. Live rolling (90% resistance): Use the Granby Roll strategically in rolling exchanges. Be prepared to defend counters. (5 minutes, full rolling)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Escaping a flattened-out side control where your opponent has heavy pressure and you cannot create space by other means.
    • When your opponent has you in a deep pass attempt (e.g., knee-slice) and you need to create immediate forward momentum to reverse the situation.
    • When you are caught in mount and your opponent is aggressively smashing you, making it difficult to shrimp or bridge effectively.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Leg Grab/Ankle Pick: As the white belt initiates the roll, the defender (opponent) can quickly step in and grab the trailing leg or ankle, preventing them from completing the rotation and potentially sweeping them.
    • Head and Arm Control: If the roll is not tight, the defender can reach over and control the head and arm of the rolling opponent, preventing them from completing the rotation and potentially securing a submission.
    • Trapping the Body: A skilled opponent can anticipate the roll and use their weight to trap the rolling person's hips or upper body, preventing them from gaining any forward momentum and forcing them to stop or land awkwardly.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt after attempting the Granby Roll?

    Your neck likely hurts because you are not tucking your chin to your chest firmly enough before initiating the roll. This allows your head to move independently and strike the mat or strain your cervical spine. Always lead the roll with your chin tucked into your sternum, creating a rounded shape that protects your head and neck.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, the key is efficiency and speed over brute force. Focus on making yourself as small and compact as possible by tucking your knees tightly to your chest. Use the opponent's weight against them by initiating the roll at an angle that leverages their forward pressure, rather than trying to roll directly through them.

    Q: The Granby Roll is primarily an escape and transition tool for when you are in a bad position and traditional escapes are not working. It's most effective when you feel flattened out, such as in heavy side control or mount, and need to create immediate forward momentum to disrupt your opponent's control and regain a more advantageous position.
