The Granby Roll is a fundamental BJJ transition, typically initiated from a guard position like Closed Guard or half guard, aiming to create space and improve your position, often to a guard passing scenario or to regain guard.

White belts often struggle because they lack the understanding of weight distribution and leverage, leading to awkward, ineffective movements and potential injury.

The core principle is using your hips and shoulders as a fulcrum to pivot your body, transferring weight to your opponent's base and off your own.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting Position: Assume closed guard with opponent in your guard, hips close. Grip their far collar with your dominant hand (e.g., right hand on their left collar) and their sleeve with your other hand (e.g., left hand on their right sleeve).
    2. Hip Angle: Simultaneously, drive your hips upwards and slightly to your left (if right-hand dominant). Imagine trying to sit up quickly.
    3. Weight Transfer: As your hips rise, extend your legs forcefully, pushing their hips away to create initial space. This is not a pull, but a push with the legs.
    4. Shoulder Rotation: Initiate a strong rotation of your torso, leading with your right shoulder towards the mat on your right side. Your head should turn to look in the direction of the roll.
    5. Arm Frame: Maintain the collar grip to help pull yourself forward and the sleeve grip to control their arm, preventing them from posting out and stopping your momentum.
    6. Leg Drive: Continue to drive with your legs, pushing their base away as your body pivots. Your left leg will likely clear their hip first.
    7. Mat Contact: Aim to have your right shoulder and upper back contact the mat. Your left leg should be extended, and your right leg tucked or bent.
    8. Completion: Continue the rotation until you are on your knees or in a low base, facing your opponent. Your right arm should be free, and your left arm still controlling their sleeve.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Neck Flexion: Attempting to roll by tucking the chin forcefully to the chest. This can cause cervical spine hyperextension or compression, leading to neck pain or nerve damage.
    • Correct: Keep the head neutral, looking in the direction of the roll, and let the shoulder initiate the rotation.
    • Uncontrolled Hip Drive: Throwing the hips up and forward without a clear direction or opponent reaction. This can result in landing awkwardly on your tailbone or coccyx, causing fractures or severe bruising.
    • Correct: Drive hips up and *slightly* to the side, using leg extension to create space and guide the rotation.
    • Over-Reliance on Arm Pull: Trying to pull yourself into the roll solely with your grips without hip and shoulder engagement. This places excessive strain on the shoulder joint, potentially leading to rotator cuff tears or dislocations.
    • Correct: Emphasize the upward hip drive and torso rotation, using grips for control and momentum assistance.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip & Shoulder Rotation: From a seated position, practice the hip drive and shoulder rotation movement without any opponent or resistance. Focus on the feeling of pivoting. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Mat Touch: Stand up, then practice the hip drive and shoulder rotation, aiming to touch your shoulder to the mat and return to a low base. (50 reps)
    3. Partner Assisted Rotation (0% Resistance): With a partner lying on their back, you are in their guard. Practice the Granby Roll motion, with your partner providing minimal assistance to guide your rotation. Focus on your body mechanics. (10 reps per side)
    4. Partner Assisted Space Creation (25% Resistance): Partner attempts to hold guard. You practice the leg push and hip drive to create space, then execute the Granby Roll. Partner offers very light resistance to your leg push. (15 reps per side)
    5. Controlled Transition Drill (50% Resistance): From closed guard, you initiate the Granby Roll. Partner does not resist the roll but tries to maintain a stable base. Focus on smooth execution and landing in a base. (20 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling Application (75-90% Resistance): In rolling, attempt the Granby Roll when the opportunity arises. Partner actively defends but focuses on maintaining position rather than actively stopping the roll. (5-10 attempts over several rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up heavily inside your closed guard, creating an opportunity to create space.
    • When you are stuck in a bad half guard and need to disengage to reset or improve your position.
    • When your opponent is attempting a guard pass and you need to transition to a more advantageous position or regain guard.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Heavy Hip Pressure: As the Granby Roll begins, the defender can immediately drive their hips down and forward, flattening their base and preventing the attacker's hips from rising effectively. This stops the pivot.
    • Posting the Opposite Arm: The defender can quickly post their arm furthest from the rolling attacker onto the mat. This creates a stable base, allowing them to resist the rotation and avoid being swept.
    • Leg Extension and Base: The defender can extend their legs, creating a wider base and pushing back against the attacker's leg extension. This disrupts the space-creation element essential for the Granby Roll's success.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to Granby Roll?

    Neck pain during the Granby Roll typically occurs when you tuck your chin too aggressively to your chest, attempting to force the roll with your head. This creates hyperextension or compression of the cervical spine. Instead, keep your head neutral and look in the direction you are rolling, allowing your shoulders and hips to initiate the movement.

    Q: How do I Granby Roll against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is to maximize the space created by your leg extension and hip drive. Focus on a powerful push with your legs to get their hips off you, and then a sharp, decisive shoulder rotation. Do not try to muscle through them; use their weight against them by creating a dynamic pivot point with your own body.

    Q: The Granby Roll is best used as a transition to create space or to escape bad positions, not as a primary sweep. It's most effective when your opponent is posturing up, or when you are stuck in a compromised guard (like a bad half guard) and need to disengage to reset or transition to a better position. It's a proactive movement to improve your situation.
