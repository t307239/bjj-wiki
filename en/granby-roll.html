Granby Roll: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Granby Roll is a transition used to escape bad positions, most commonly from being flattened out in Side Control or mount. It allows you to re-establish guard or move to a more dominant position.

White belts often struggle with the Granby Roll due to a lack of body awareness and an instinct to simply push with their hands, leading to inefficient movement and injury.

The key mechanical insight is to use your hips and core to drive the rotation, rather than just pushing with your upper body, creating a powerful, fluid movement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a flattened side control or mount position, establish a strong grip on your opponent's far hip with your near-side hand (e.g., left hand grips opponent's right hip).
    2. Simultaneously, secure a strong grip on your opponent's far-side sleeve with your far-side hand (e.g., right hand grips opponent's left sleeve).
    3. Tuck your chin to your chest and drive your lead shoulder (the one closest to the opponent's head) into the mat.
    4. Initiate the roll by driving your hips forward and upward, creating a powerful rotation around your tucked shoulder.
    5. As your hips drive, use your gripping hands to pull your opponent's hip and sleeve inward, guiding their body's rotation with yours.
    6. Maintain a tight tuck of your knees towards your chest throughout the rotation to protect your spine and maintain a compact shape.
    7. Aim to land on your feet or in a position where you can immediately re-establish your guard or move to a dominant position, keeping your hips lower than your opponent's.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to push up with straight arms from the mat: This places excessive stress on the elbows and shoulders, potentially leading to hyperextension injuries (e.g., torn UCL in the elbow or rotator cuff tears).
    • Letting your head and neck twist unnaturally during the roll: This can cause cervical spine strain or whiplash, especially if your head is not tucked securely.
    • Rolling with hips too high and legs extended: This exposes your knees and ankles to potential hyperextension or twisting injuries if you land awkwardly.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo dissociation drill: Practice the hip and shoulder rotation movement on the mat without a partner. Focus on the feeling of driving hips forward. (50 reps)
    2. Partner assisted dissociation: Have a partner gently guide your hips and shoulders through the rolling motion, providing minimal resistance. (25 reps)
    3. Controlled Granby Roll from side control: Start in side control, have your partner lightly resist your attempt to roll. Focus on the hip drive and grip engagement. (10 reps per side)
    4. Granby Roll from flattened out position: Your partner flattens you in side control or mount. Execute the Granby Roll with 25% resistance.
    5. Granby Roll with increasing resistance: Progress to 50% resistance, focusing on maintaining your grip and hip drive against a more active opponent.
    6. Live rolling application: Apply the Granby Roll in live rolling scenarios, aiming to escape bad positions. 90% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent flattens you out from side control, making it difficult to create space or shrimp.
    • When your opponent is in your guard and you are being stacked, preventing you from establishing a strong guard.
    • When you are caught in a bad position after a failed sweep or submission attempt, and need to quickly disengage and reset.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Trap: If the Granby Roller attempts to roll, immediately extend your leg on the side they are rolling towards, trapping their leg and preventing them from completing the rotation. Maintain pressure on their hip.
    • Hip Block: As they initiate the roll, use your hips to block their forward momentum by driving your hip into their hip on the side they are attempting to roll away from. This disrupts their rotational axis.
    • Grip Break and Re-control: If you have a strong grip on their pants or gi, attempt to break their grip as they roll and immediately transition to a dominant control position like side control or mount.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to Granby Roll?

    Shoulder pain during the Granby Roll often stems from improper weight distribution and a lack of core engagement. Instead of driving with your hips, you're likely pushing with your shoulder directly into the mat. Ensure you tuck your chin and drive your hips forward and upward, using your core to initiate the rotation, not just your upper body pressing down.

    Q: How do I Granby Roll against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, the Granby Roll relies even more on precise mechanics and leverage. Focus on securing a strong grip on their hip and sleeve to guide their weight as you roll. Drive your hips explosively and keep your body compact by tucking your knees tightly. The goal is to use their size against them by initiating a controlled rotation, not to overpower them with brute strength.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Granby Roll, and when should I avoid it?

    The Granby Roll is best used as a defensive escape when you are being severely flattened or stacked, and traditional shrimping or bridging is ineffective. Avoid it when you have significant space to shrimp, when your opponent is actively threatening a submission that requires you to maintain a specific structure, or when you are already in a compromised position where a roll could lead to further danger.
