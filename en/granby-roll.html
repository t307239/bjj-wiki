Granby Roll: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Granby Roll is a transition used primarily from bottom positions like Closed Guard or half guard to escape pressure or improve your position. It allows you to rotate your body and re-establish a more dominant or safer guard.

White belts often struggle with the Granby Roll due to a lack of body awareness and a tendency to force the movement rather than letting physics do the work. This can lead to ineffective transitions and potential injuries.

The key mechanical insight is to use your hips and shoulders to create a rolling axis, driving forward with your legs to generate momentum, rather than trying to 'muscle' through the movement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, break opponent's grips on your biceps. Maintain a hip angle of approximately 45 degrees to your opponent.
    2. Secure a gable grip around your opponent's far arm, or control their far sleeve with your near hand. Your weight should be slightly forward, hips elevated.
    3. Tuck your chin to your chest, creating a rounded upper back. This protects your neck and prepares for the roll.
    4. Initiate the roll by driving your hips forward and slightly up, initiating a rotation through your torso. Your legs should extend slightly to propel you.
    5. As your body rotates, your head will naturally lead the roll. Your near arm will extend to brace against the mat, preventing a flat fall.
    6. Continue the rotation, aiming to land on your shoulder and the side of your head, with your opponent's arm still controlled.
    7. Immediately drive your hips into your opponent as you finish the roll, aiming to establish a passing position or regain guard.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect neck tuck: Tucking the chin too hard or at an awkward angle can lead to cervical spine hyperextension or flexion injury, potentially damaging the intervertebral discs. Always maintain a neutral head position relative to your spine, initiating the roll from the hips.
    • Flat-footed landing: Landing heavily on the feet or knees instead of rolling onto the shoulder and side of the head can cause ankle sprains, knee ligament tears (ACL/MCL), or fractures due to excessive impact force. Focus on a controlled, rotational landing on your padded shoulder.
    • Over-rotation with no base: Attempting to roll too far without establishing a base can leave you vulnerable and off-balance, potentially leading to shoulder dislocations or rotator cuff tears if you fall awkwardly. Control the rotation and immediately work to establish a stable base upon landing.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Rotation Drill: Practice tucking your chin and rotating your hips in a circular motion on the mat, feeling the movement. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Granby Roll with Mat Brace: Perform the Granby Roll motion, tucking your chin and rolling onto your shoulder, using your free hand to brace the mat. (50 reps per side)
    3. Partner Assisted Granby Roll (0% resistance): Your partner lies on their back, and you practice the Granby Roll transition, with them offering minimal resistance. (25 reps per side)
    4. Partner Assisted Granby Roll (25% resistance): Your partner offers slight pressure, and you execute the Granby Roll to transition. Focus on maintaining control of their arm. (50 reps per side)
    5. Partner Granby Roll Escape (50% resistance): Your partner is in a dominant position (e.g., side control), and you initiate the Granby Roll to escape and regain guard. (50 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling Granby Roll Attempts (75% resistance): During rolling, actively look for opportunities to use the Granby Roll when appropriate, with your partner reacting to your attempts. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is heavy in your closed guard, crushing your hips and making it difficult to breathe.
    • When your opponent is posturing up from your closed guard, threatening to pass.
    • When you are stuck in half guard and need to create space to switch to a better position or escape.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Head and Arm Control: If your opponent attempts a Granby Roll and you have a head and arm control (e.g., an over-under pass setup), you can often maintain pressure and prevent the roll, or even transition to a submission. Keep your weight distributed forward and your hips tight.
    • Knee Shield / Frame: If your opponent attempts the Granby Roll from guard, and you can establish a strong knee shield or frame with your arm against their hip or shoulder, you can disrupt their momentum and prevent the rotation. Push away with your knee or elbow.
    • Hip Escape and Base: If the Granby Roller fails to complete the roll and ends up in an awkward position, immediately hip escape and establish a strong base. This prevents them from recovering guard or advancing their position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to Granby Roll?

    Your neck likely hurts because you are leading the roll with your head instead of your hips and shoulders. This forces your cervical spine into an unnatural and potentially injurious position. To fix this, focus on tucking your chin to your chest and driving your hips forward and up, creating a rolling axis that goes through your torso, not just your head.

    Q: How can I Granby Roll effectively against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are crucial. Instead of trying to muscle the roll, focus on using their weight against them. As they apply pressure, create a small gap with your hips, secure a grip on their arm or lapel, and use a sharp, explosive hip drive to initiate the rotation. The goal is to get your body moving before they can fully react and stifle your momentum.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Granby Roll from bottom position?

    The Granby Roll is best attempted when your opponent is applying significant downward pressure or attempting to static pass your guard. This is when their weight is forward and committed, making it easier to disrupt their base and use their own momentum against them. Avoid attempting it when they are postured up and mobile, as this offers less leverage and more opportunity for them to counter.
