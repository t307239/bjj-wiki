Back Mount is a dominant position achieved by securing your opponent's back, controlling their upper body, and establishing hooks with your legs.

White belts often struggle with Back Mount due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and frame control, leading to easily escaped positions.

The key mechanical insight is maintaining your hips lower than your opponent's and creating a constant connection through your hooks and chest pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a seatbelt grip: Left hand grips opponent's right collar, right hand grips opponent's left collar. Your chest presses into their upper back.
    2. Insert your right hook: Bring your right foot across their hip crease, hooking their inner thigh. Your right shin should be perpendicular to their spine.
    3. Insert your left hook: Bring your left foot across their opposite hip crease, hooking their inner thigh. Your left shin should be perpendicular to their spine.
    4. Connect your feet: Your ankles should be crossed behind their hips, creating a 'figure-four' leg entanglement.
    5. Lower your hips: Drive your hips down towards the mat, creating a base that is lower than your opponent's hips.
    6. Maintain chest-to-back connection: Keep your chest firmly against their upper back to prevent them from creating space.
    7. Control their posture: Use your collar grips to pull their head down and away from their shoulders, breaking their posture.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect hook insertion: Allowing your hooks to become too loose or not fully engaging their hips can lead to the opponent turning into you.
    • Injury risk: This can cause knee valgus stress on the ACL and MCL if the opponent forcefully turns, or strain in the hip flexors.
    • Correct movement: Ensure your shin is perpendicular to their spine and your foot is firmly hooked behind their hip crease.
    • Overextending your neck: Trying to 'choke' from the back by pulling with your arms alone without proper body connection strains the cervical spine.
    • Injury risk: Ligamentous strain in the neck and potential for disc compression.
    • Correct movement: Use your body weight to drive their head down, not just arm strength.
    • Letting your hips rise: If your hips are higher than your opponent's, they can easily peel your hooks off and escape.
    • Injury risk: Can lead to hip flexor strains or groin pulls if you attempt to re-establish hooks under pressure.
    • Correct movement: Actively drive your hips down and forward, maintaining a tight connection.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0%): Practice entering Back Mount from a neutral position (e.g., standing or from a failed guard pass attempt) on a dummy or air. Focus on hook insertion and hip connection. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drill - static (25%): Partner lies on their stomach. You practice establishing Back Mount with hooks and chest connection. Partner offers minimal resistance. (10 reps)
    3. Partner drill - controlled movement (50%): Partner attempts to lightly shrimp or bridge while you maintain Back Mount. Focus on hip control and re-establishing hooks. (10 reps)
    4. Partner drill - defensive escape (75%): Partner actively tries to escape from Back Mount using common defenses (e.g., turning in, hand-fighting grips). You focus on maintaining position and preventing the escape. (5 reps per side)
    5. Scenario drilling (90%): Start from a position where Back Mount is achievable (e.g., opponent turns their back after a failed takedown). Partner provides realistic resistance. (3 rounds, 1 minute each)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Apply Back Mount techniques during sparring. Focus on securing the position and not just the submission attempt. (5 minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent turtles up, exposing their back.
    • After a successful takedown where the opponent lands on their stomach.
    • During transitions where you secure control of the opponent's back.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Turning In: The opponent turns their hips towards the side of the attacking grappler's lead leg (the leg closer to their head).
    • Biomechanics: The opponent drives their hips into the attacker's thigh and uses their shoulders to create space, often leading to a guard recovery.
    • Hand Fighting and Grip Breaking: The opponent actively fights the collar grips with their own hands and wrists.
    • Biomechanics: The opponent uses their thumbs to peel away the attacker's fingers from the collar, creating space to turn or escape.
    • Bridging and Shrimping: The opponent attempts to create space by bridging their hips and shrimping their legs away.
    • Biomechanics: The opponent uses their legs to push off the attacker's hips and simultaneously bridges their torso, aiming to dislodge the hooks.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to get Back Mount?

    Your lower back pain is likely due to an anterior pelvic tilt and over-extension. When you try to secure the Back Mount, you might be arching your back excessively instead of using your glutes and hamstrings to drive your hips forward. Focus on engaging your core and glutes to create a 'posterior pelvic tilt,' which flattens your lower back and allows your hips to sit lower and more securely behind your opponent.

    Q: How can I maintain Back Mount against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary focus must be on maintaining a tight connection and preventing them from using their mass to create space. Ensure your hooks are deeply embedded behind their hips, and your chest is glued to their upper back. Actively drive your hips down and slightly forward, creating a lower base than theirs. Use your legs to control their hip movement, not just your arms.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from securing the back to attacking a submission from Back Mount?

    The optimal time to transition from securing the back to an attack is when you have established control of your opponent's posture and they are unable to effectively defend. This means your hooks are secure, your chest is connected, and you've broken down their posture by pulling their head towards their shoulder. Rushing a submission before establishing this control will often result in losing the position.
