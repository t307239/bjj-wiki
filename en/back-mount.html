Back Mount: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Back Mount is a dominant control position where you have both hooks in and are positioned behind your opponent's torso.

It offers a superior angle for attacking chokes and is often achieved after a successful takedown or sweep.

White belts often lose this position by becoming too static or neglecting their base, allowing the opponent to escape.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure both of your opponent's hips with your shins, creating a 'seatbelt' grip on their upper body with your arms (one arm over their shoulder, one under their armpit).
    2. Drive your chest into your opponent's upper back, maintaining constant upper body pressure and preventing them from turning into you.
    3. Keep your hips low and connected to their hips, ensuring your weight is distributed over their center of gravity.
    4. Maintain a tight frame by keeping your elbows tucked and your forearms pressing against their chest or shoulders.
    5. When attacking, ensure your head is on the same side as your choking arm, creating a leverage advantage.
    6. To prevent escape, constantly adjust your hooks; if one leg comes loose, immediately re-establish it by driving your knee towards their hip.
    7. If your opponent tries to turn into you, shift your weight to the side they are turning towards and use your chest to push them back down.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too loosely on the opponent's torso: This allows the opponent to create space and disengage your hooks, potentially leading to them turning into you and escaping the position.
    • Allowing your hips to rise too high off the opponent's hips: This creates a gap, enabling the opponent to bridge or roll, potentially injuring your lower back or causing you to lose control.
    • Forgetting to maintain head control: If your head is not actively pressing into their shoulder or head, the opponent can turn their head and create space to escape or even initiate a submission.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip-to-hip connection and seatbelt grip without a partner. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drilling (static): Have a partner lie on their stomach. Practice establishing the back mount position and maintaining the hooks and seatbelt grip. (5 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drilling (controlled movement): Your partner attempts to posture up slightly or turn. You practice maintaining your position by adjusting hooks and pressure. (5 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drilling (escape attempts): Your partner actively tries to escape by turning or shrimping. You focus on maintaining control and re-establishing hooks. (5 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (focus on position): Spar with a partner, prioritizing achieving and maintaining back mount. Do not focus on submissions initially. (3 x 3 minute rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full intensity): Spar with a partner, aiming to achieve back mount and transition to submissions. (5 x 3 minute rounds, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully executing a takedown and securing the back.
    • Following a sweep where you land behind your opponent.
    • When your opponent gives up their back during scrambles or transitions.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape by turning into the opponent: As soon as you feel the opponent's hooks are loose or their chest pressure is weak, initiate a hard turn towards the side of their choking arm, aiming to get your hips to the mat and face them.
    • Bridge and roll: If the opponent's hips are too high, bridge your hips up and roll towards the side of their body, attempting to dislodge their hooks and get to a top position.
    • Shrimp and re-guard: If the opponent has a strong body triangle, focus on shrimping your hips away to create space, then attempt to untangle your legs and establish guard (e.g., closed guard or half guard).

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do my hips hurt when I'm in back mount?

    Your hips might hurt because you are not maintaining proper hip connection with your opponent. Ensure your shins are tightly wrapped around their hips, and your own hips are low and pressed into their pelvis. This distributes weight correctly and prevents hyperextension or awkward angles on your hip joints.

    Q: How can I maintain back mount against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maintaining constant chest-to-back pressure and keeping your hips glued to theirs. Use your upper body strength to control their posture and prevent them from creating space. Prioritize securing the seatbelt grip tightly and keeping your head on the same side as your choking arm for leverage.

    Q: When should I transition from back mount to a submission?

    Transition to a submission when you have established a strong back mount with both hooks in and your opponent is unable to escape. Look for opportunities when they attempt to defend a choke, as this often creates openings for Armbars or other submissions. Always ensure your position is secure before committing to a submission attempt.
