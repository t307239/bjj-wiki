Back Mount is a dominant position in BJJ where you control your opponent's back, facing away from you. It offers superior leverage for submissions and prevents your opponent from escaping easily.

Many white belts struggle because they focus on 'holding on' rather than establishing a secure, biomechanically sound base, leading to them being easily unseated or losing control.

The key is maintaining control of the hips and head while keeping your weight distributed forward, creating a 'hug' that limits their mobility and sets up your attacks.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure both of the opponent's hips with your legs, creating a 'seatbelt' grip. Your thighs should press against their inner thighs, and your feet should be clasped behind their glutes (or ankles if legs are extended).
    2. Place your right arm over their left shoulder, gripping their right collar with your right hand, thumb inside. Your left arm goes under their right armpit, gripping their left collar with your left hand, thumb inside.
    3. Ensure your chest is pressed firmly against their upper back, driving your sternum into their shoulder blades.
    4. Your hips should be lower than their hips, creating a downward pressure.
    5. Maintain a tight frame by keeping your elbows tucked in and close to your body.
    6. Keep your weight distributed forward, driving your hips into their hips.
    7. Continuously adjust your grips to prevent them from breaking your control, focusing on maintaining collar control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping only the opponent's arms: This leaves their hips and core uncontrolled, allowing them to shrimp out or roll. The shoulder and hip joints are vulnerable to hyperextension if you rely solely on arm control.
    • Trying to 'hug' too tightly with your arms: This strains your shoulder joints and limits your ability to adjust your base, risking shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears. Instead, focus on keeping your elbows tucked and chest to back pressure.
    • Letting your hips rise too high: This creates a gap between your hips and theirs, allowing them to create space and potentially escape or reverse. Your hips should remain lower than theirs to maintain a strong base. This can also strain your lower back if you arch excessively.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice securing the Back Mount position from a static starting point (e.g., opponent lying on their stomach) 10 times per side, focusing on grip and body placement (0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner lies on their stomach. You practice entering Back Mount and establishing grips 10 times per side (0% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance to your entry and grip establishment. Focus on maintaining control for 30 seconds, then switch roles (25% resistance).
    4. Controlled Back Mount entry: Your partner starts on their knees. You practice securing Back Mount as they attempt to defend by turning. Hold for 30 seconds (50% resistance).
    5. Back Mount retention drill: You start in Back Mount. Your partner attempts to escape by turning or unhooking your legs. Focus on maintaining control and preventing escapes for 1 minute (75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Implement Back Mount entry and retention during live rolling, focusing on maintaining the position and transitioning to attacks (90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent turtles up from guard or during scrambles.
    • After successfully executing a takedown where you secure their back.
    • When your opponent attempts to escape from mount or side control and exposes their back.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Elbow-to-knee escape: Opponent tucks their chin, brings their knees to their chest, and drives their elbow into the mat to bridge and roll. This requires precise timing and body positioning to avoid being swept.
    • Bridging and turning: Opponent uses their hips to bridge upwards and simultaneously turns their body away from your choking arm. This creates space for them to escape the hooks.
    • Defending the choke: Opponent uses their free arm to block your choking arm and simultaneously tries to create space by pushing your chest away with their free hand.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to maintain Back Mount?

    Lower back pain often stems from excessive arching and a lack of hip connection. Instead of arching your back to stay on, focus on keeping your hips lower than your opponent's and driving your sternum into their upper back. This creates a strong posterior chain engagement, using your glutes and hamstrings to maintain the position without straining your lumbar spine.

    Q: How can I secure Back Mount against a much larger opponent who is strong?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on superior technique and precision over brute force. Prioritize securing your hooks first, ensuring your feet are clasped behind their glutes. Then, drive your chest into their upper back and use your hips to create a downward pressure, limiting their ability to stand or posture up. Your arm grips should be focused on controlling their posture and preventing them from breaking your base.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a submission from Back Mount as a white belt?

    As a white belt, your primary goal from Back Mount is to establish and maintain the position securely. Only attempt submissions when you have a very strong, locked-in Back Mount with your opponent unable to escape. Focus on the Rear Naked Choke initially, as it has a high success rate when applied correctly. Ensure your hooks are secure and your chest-to-back pressure is maintained before committing to a submission attempt.
