Back Mount: The Ultimate Control Position in BJJ

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Back Mount is a dominant position achieved from the opponent's back, offering unparalleled control and submission opportunities.

White belts often struggle by either losing the position due to poor body mechanics or by focusing too much on submissions before securing the control.

The key is maintaining a strong "seatbelt" grip and keeping your hips low and tight to the opponent's back, preventing them from turning into you.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure both of the opponent's arms with your forearms across their chest, establishing the "seatbelt" grip. Your right forearm crosses over their left shoulder, and your left forearm crosses over their right shoulder.
    2. Place your hips on the opponent's lower back, ensuring your pelvis is flush against their sacrum.
    3. Hook your opponent's hips with the instep of your feet, creating a "grapevine" by placing your feet on the outside of their thighs and applying pressure inwards.
    4. Drive your chest into the opponent's upper back, creating forward pressure to flatten them out.
    5. Maintain a low center of gravity by keeping your knees bent and your hips close to the mat.
    6. If opponent attempts to turn into you, immediately adjust your hooks and hip position to face the direction they are turning, maintaining your seatbelt grip.
    7. To transition to a submission, secure one arm with your legs and initiate a body triangle or prepare for a choke, always maintaining hip pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to "hug" the opponent's torso with your arms instead of securing a seatbelt grip can lead to the opponent escaping by shrugging their shoulders and creating space. This uncontrolled movement can strain your shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) by forcing it into an extreme external rotation. Maintain the seatbelt grip with forearms across the chest.
    • Letting your hips lift off the opponent's back allows them to bridge and roll you off. This sudden loss of base can cause hyperextension of the lumbar spine if your back is not properly braced. Keep your hips glued to their sacrum.
    • Overextending your legs in the grapevine, creating too much space between your feet and their hips, allows the opponent to "explode" out by hip escaping. This can lead to knee hyperextension, potentially damaging the ACL or PCL. Keep your hooks tight and apply inward pressure.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice securing the seatbelt grip and hooking the legs from a prone position on a dummy or mat. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drill, no resistance (0% resistance): From the opponent's back, practice establishing the seatbelt and grapevine hooks, focusing on hip tightness and chest pressure. (10 reps)
    3. Partner drill, passive resistance (25% resistance): Your partner remains still, allowing you to practice maintaining the position as they try to subtly create space. Focus on maintaining hip pressure and seatbelt integrity. (5 minutes)
    4. Partner drill, light resistance (50% resistance): Your partner actively tries to defend by shrugging shoulders or hip escaping. You practice your corrections to maintain position. (5 minutes)
    5. Controlled drilling (75% resistance): Your partner attempts to escape from back mount. You focus on maintaining the position and preventing the escape for a set time (e.g., 30 seconds) before transitioning to a submission attempt. (5 rounds)
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Implement back mount as a goal from the start of sparring, focusing on securing and maintaining the position against a resisting opponent. (3 rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully executing a takedown and transitioning to your opponent's back.
    • During scrambles where you manage to get behind your opponent.
    • After successfully defending a sweep or submission and ending up in a superior position behind them.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Turn-in Defense: When the opponent attempts to turn into you, immediately shift your hips and hooks to face the same direction, keeping your seatbelt grip tight and driving your chest into their shoulder. This prevents them from creating space to escape.
    • Elbow-Knee Escape: If the opponent secures a strong seatbelt and begins to flatten you, the primary defense is to bring your knees to your chest, creating a tight frame, and then attempt to bring your elbow and knee on the same side together to create a "wall" and push off. This is often followed by attempting to turn onto your side or back.
    • Hand-Fighting the Seatbelt: If the opponent's seatbelt is loose, you can try to peel their choking arm away with your hands and then use your own arms to frame and create space to turn away and regain guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to get to Back Mount?

    Your lower back likely hurts because you are arching your back excessively to try and "hug" your opponent. This hyperextends your lumbar spine. Instead, focus on keeping your hips tight and flush against your opponent's sacrum, and drive your chest into their upper back. This uses your core and glutes for stability, rather than your lower back.

    Q: How do I maintain Back Mount against a much bigger opponent who keeps trying to bridge and roll me?

    Against a larger opponent, your hip and leg connection is paramount. Ensure your "grapevine" hooks are tight, with the instep of your feet digging into the back of their thighs, and apply constant inward pressure. Simultaneously, keep your chest driven into their upper back and your hips on their sacrum. This creates a strong base that is difficult for them to bridge against.

    Q: When is the best time to try for a submission from Back Mount, or should I just focus on holding the position?

    As a white belt, your primary focus should be on securing and maintaining the Back Mount position with excellent hip and grip control. Once you have established that solid control for at least 5-10 seconds, and your opponent is struggling to escape, then you can begin to look for submission opportunities like the Rear Naked Choke or Body Triangle. Prioritize control before attacking.
