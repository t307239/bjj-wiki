Your First Steps to Dominating Back Mount in BJJ

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Back Mount is a dominant position in BJJ, achieved when you are behind your opponent, controlling their torso with your legs and upper body.

White belts often struggle by losing control of the hips or failing to secure a tight grip, leading to escapes or reversals.

The key is establishing a secure "seatbelt grip" and controlling the opponent's base with your hips and legs, creating a stable platform for submissions.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the "seatbelt grip": Place your right arm over the opponent's left shoulder, gripping your own left bicep with your right hand. Your left arm goes under their right armpit, gripping your own right bicep with your left hand.
    2. Hook the legs: Insert your right foot's instep deep into the opponent's left inner thigh crease, and your left foot's instep deep into their right inner thigh crease. Your heels should drive towards your own hips.
    3. Hip angle: Angle your hips approximately 45 degrees to the opponent's spine. This prevents them from turning into you or away from you easily.
    4. Weight distribution: Drive your chest into the opponent's upper back, creating downward pressure. Your hips should be low, creating a "bridge" to connect your hooks.
    5. Leg tension: Maintain constant tension in your hooks, squeezing your knees together and driving your heels back towards your glutes. This prevents them from dislodging your legs.
    6. Upper body connection: Keep your head up and your chest pressed firmly into their upper back. Avoid letting your head drop or your upper body detach.
    7. Maintain the seatbelt: Ensure your arm grips are tight and do not loosen. This prevents them from creating space to turn or escape.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect hook placement: Driving your feet too high on the opponent's hips or too low on their calves can lead to your hooks being easily "kicked out" by the opponent. This can cause knee ligament sprains (MCL/LCL) due to excessive lateral stress. The correct placement is the instep deep in the inner thigh crease.
    • Neck strain from head position: Letting your head fall to one side while trying to maintain the seatbelt grip can lead to cervical strain or nerve impingement. The correct posture involves keeping your head up and centered, looking forward.
    • Lower back hyperextension: Attempting to "climb" too high on the opponent's back without a solid base and proper hip control can cause your lower back to arch excessively, leading to lumbar strain. Maintain a stable base with your hips connected to their back.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice securing the seatbelt grip and inserting hooks from a kneeling position. (5 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Solo drill: Practice maintaining the seatbelt grip and leg hooks while rocking side-to-side, simulating opponent movement. (10 reps, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (no resistance): Opponent lies on their stomach. You practice climbing to their back and securing the seatbelt grip and hooks. (5 reps, 0% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (light resistance): Opponent actively tries to defend by shrugging shoulders and moving hips. You focus on maintaining grip and hooks. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    5. Partner drill (controlled): Opponent attempts to escape from Back Mount. You practice maintaining control and preventing escape for 30 seconds. (5 rounds, 50% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Implement Back Mount attempts and defense during sparring. Focus on maintaining the position once achieved. (3 rounds, 75-90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully taking the opponent's back during a scramble or takedown.
    • When the opponent "swims" an arm inside to defend a guard pass and leaves their back exposed.
    • When an opponent attempts a poorly executed sweep from a guard position, allowing you to disengage and secure their back.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Window" Escape: If the opponent has a loose seatbelt grip, create space by shrugging your shoulders and turning your head towards the side of the trapped arm. Then, "swim" your arm through the "window" and bring your elbow to the mat to create a frame and turn your hips away.
    • The "Elbow Escape" (Hip Escape): If your hooks are secure but the upper body pressure is too much, drive your hips to one side and "walk" your feet up their body, creating space to turn and face them. This often involves a "biscuit rolling" motion.
    • The "Arm Trap and Turn" Defense: If the opponent secures a strong seatbelt grip but your legs are not fully hooked, try to trap their arm around your neck and turn your body into that arm, using your head and shoulder to break their grip and create an opportunity to escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do my hips hurt when I try to maintain Back Mount?

    Your hip pain is likely due to your knees being too far apart or not fully engaged with your hooks. When your knees are wide, the adductor muscles are overstretched. Ensure your insteps are deep in the opponent's inner thigh creases, and actively squeeze your knees together, driving your heels towards your glutes. This creates a "figure four" lock with your legs and protects your hips.

    Q: How can I maintain Back Mount against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maintaining superior positional control rather than brute strength. Your "seatbelt grip" is paramount; keep it tight and prevent them from bridging or turning. Use your legs actively to "hug" their hips and prevent them from creating space. Drive your chest into their upper back to maintain downward pressure, and continuously adjust your hip angle to stay behind their center of gravity.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a submission from Back Mount?

    The optimal time to attack from Back Mount is when you have achieved a "tight" position: secure seatbelt grip, both hooks in, and your chest connected to their upper back. Wait for the opponent to try and defend or escape; their movement often creates openings. If they try to peel your arms off, that's your cue to initiate a submission like a rear-naked choke. Don't rush; a stable position is your foundation for a successful submission.
