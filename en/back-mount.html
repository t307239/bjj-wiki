Back Mount is a dominant position achieved when you are behind your opponent, controlling their upper body. It offers superior control and access to submission opportunities, making it a primary goal for any grappler.

White belts often struggle by losing their base, allowing the opponent to escape easily. They might focus too much on the choke and forget about maintaining control and positional integrity.

The key mechanical insight is maintaining a consistent "seatbelt grip" with your arms and "hooking" with your feet, keeping your hips low and tight to their back. This creates a stable, inescapable platform.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the "Seatbelt Grip": Wrap one arm over the opponent's shoulder, gripping your own bicep. Wrap the other arm under their opposite armpit, gripping your own bicep. Your chest should be pressed against their upper back.
    2. Insert "Hooks": With your hips low, drive your shins forward and hook your feet behind the opponent's thighs, just above the knees. Your heels should be flexed, engaging your hamstrings.
    3. Maintain Hip Pressure: Keep your hips as close to the opponent's lower back as possible. This prevents them from creating space to turn or sit up.
    4. Weight Distribution: Distribute your weight onto your opponent's chest and shoulders, driving your hips downward and slightly backward. This pins their upper body and restricts their movement.
    5. Head Position: Keep your head slightly to one side, aligned with their neck, to avoid being head-butted and to prepare for submissions.
    6. Frame with Forearms: If the opponent tries to create space by pushing your head, use your forearms to create a frame against their shoulders, maintaining that tight connection.
    7. Adjust "Hooks": If the opponent attempts to roll, adjust your hooks by driving your heels deeper into their thighs and keeping your hips low to maintain control of their hips.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Hyperextension: Trying to "hook" with straight legs, especially when the opponent is moving, can lead to hyperextension of the knee joint, potentially tearing the ACL or PCL.
    • Correct Movement: Always maintain a slight bend in your knees when inserting hooks, and actively use your hamstrings to pull their hips in.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Over-extending your arms to "grab" the opponent's back without a proper seatbelt grip can cause impingement in the shoulder joint, leading to rotator cuff issues.
    • Correct Movement: Focus on gripping your own biceps, creating a tight, contained frame around their upper body.
    • Lower Back Strain: Trying to "ride" the opponent with your hips too high off their back, or arching your own back excessively, can lead to lumbar spine strain or disc compression.
    • Correct Movement: Keep your hips low and actively press your chest into their upper back, using your core for stability.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Back Mount Control Drill: Starting on a dummy or mat, practice achieving the seatbelt grip and hooks, holding for 30 seconds. Repeat 10 times. (0% resistance)
    2. Opponent Assisted Back Mount: Your partner lies on their stomach. You practice achieving back mount and holding the seatbelt and hooks, with them offering minimal resistance to escapes. Hold for 30 seconds. Repeat 5 times. (25% resistance)
    3. Back Mount Entry and Control: Your partner is in a neutral position. You practice securing the back mount from a common entry (e.g., after a takedown or scramble). Hold for 30 seconds. Repeat 5 times per side. (50% resistance)
    4. Back Mount to Rear Naked Choke Transition: From back mount, practice securing the rear-naked-choke grip and applying light pressure. Your partner offers passive defense. Transition back to control. Repeat 5 times. (75% resistance)
    5. Live Rolling - Defensive Back Mount: During live rolling, focus on maintaining back mount for 1 minute if you achieve it. Your partner actively tries to escape but not to submit you. Repeat 3 times. (90% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling - Full Back Mount Application: During live rolling, focus on achieving, maintaining, and attacking from back mount. Your partner defends normally. Aim for 2 minutes of control or submission attempt. Repeat 3 times. (100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Immediately after successfully executing a takedown and ending up behind your opponent.
    • During scrambles where you can secure a dominant position on their back.
    • When your opponent attempts to turn into you from a neutral or guard position and you can capitalize.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Escape to Turtle": If the attacker's hooks are not fully secured and their hips are not tight, the defender can drop their hips to the mat, create a "bridge" with their hips, and roll towards the side of the attacker's "over-hook" arm, attempting to get to their knees (turtle).
    • The "Elbow Escape" (if hooks are loose): If the attacker's hooks are not deep, the defender can drive their elbow into the attacker's hip on the "under-hook" side, creating space to shrimp their hips out and turn away.
    • The "Hand Fight to Turn In": If the attacker's seatbelt grip is loose, the defender can use their hands to "frame" against the attacker's arms and chest, creating space to turn their hips towards the attacker and face them, then attempt to regain guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt so much when I try to get Back Mount?

    Your lower back pain is likely caused by an excessive lumbar curve, meaning your hips are too high off your opponent's back. This puts immense strain on your spinal erector muscles. To fix this, focus on keeping your hips low and tight to your opponent's lower back, actively pressing your chest into their upper back. Use your core to maintain this connection and prevent arching.

    Q: How can I maintain Back Mount against a much bigger opponent who is strong?

    Against a stronger, bigger opponent, your "hooks" and "seatbelt grip" are paramount. Ensure your hooks are deeply dug into the back of their thighs, not just the calves. Actively pull their hips towards you by flexing your hamstrings. Your seatbelt grip should be tight, gripping your own biceps firmly, to prevent them from creating space to turn or explode. Focus on constant pressure and small adjustments rather than brute force.

    Q: When is the best time to try for a submission from Back Mount, and should I always go for the Rear Naked Choke?

    The best time to attempt a submission is when your opponent is exhausted or has made a mistake that compromises their defense, like turning their head or exposing their neck. While the Rear Naked Choke is the most common and effective, don't be afraid to transition if an opportunity arises. If they defend the choke by tucking their chin, you might have an opening for a body triangle or even an Armbar if they expose an arm. Always prioritize maintaining control before attacking.
