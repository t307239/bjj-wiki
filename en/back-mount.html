Mastering Back Mount in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu: A White Belt's Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Back Mount is a dominant position achieved from behind an opponent, offering superior control and submission opportunities. It is the ultimate goal for controlling an opponent's upper body without them seeing you.

White belts often struggle with Back Mount due to an overreliance on strength and a lack of understanding of leverage and weight distribution. They tend to grip too loosely or position their hooks inefficiently.

The key mechanical insight is maintaining a "seatbelt grip" and "hooks" that form a stable, connected structure, allowing you to dictate your opponent's posture and movement by controlling their hips and shoulders.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a "Seatbelt Grip": Place your right arm over your opponent's left shoulder, cupping their left collarbone with your palm. Your left arm goes under their right armpit, gripping your own right wrist or bicep. Ensure your chest is connected to their upper back.
    2. Insert "Hooks": With your hips tight to their hips, bring your right leg inside their right thigh and your left leg inside their left thigh. Your shins should be perpendicular to their torso, and your feet flexed, digging your heels into their inner thighs.
    3. Maintain Hip Connection: Keep your pelvis pressed firmly against your opponent's lower back, preventing them from posturing up or creating space. Your weight should be distributed forward, not backward.
    4. Control Shoulder Rotation: Use your seatbelt arm to prevent your opponent from turning their torso towards you. A slight pull on their collar with your overhook arm restricts their head movement.
    5. Stabilize with Hooks: Your "hooks" are your primary connection to their hips. Actively flex your hamstrings and calves to maintain pressure, preventing them from "bridging" or "bucking" effectively.
    6. Weight Transfer for Control: If your opponent tries to turn towards you, shift your weight by moving your hips in the opposite direction of their turn. This destabilizes them and maintains your positional advantage.
    7. Adjust for Posture: If your opponent is posturing up, drive your hips forward and slightly downward, pulling their shoulders back with your seatbelt grip to break their posture and create submission opportunities.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Hook Placement: Placing hooks too wide or with flexed ankles allows the opponent to bridge or escape. This can lead to hyperextension of the knee joint if the opponent forcefully escapes.
    • Over-reliance on Neck Grip: Gripping only the collar without securing the hips leads to a loss of control, making you vulnerable to sweeps. This can strain the cervical spine if the opponent suddenly changes direction.
    • Leaning Back: Leaning your weight backward to maintain the position invites your opponent to bridge and escape, potentially causing lumbar strain or a painful fall. The correct action is to drive hips forward and maintain chest-to-back contact.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling "Seatbelt Grip" and "Hooks": Practice transitioning to Back Mount from a simulated takedown or scramble, focusing on correct grip and hook insertion. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill "Seatbelt Grip" and "Hooks" with passive partner: Partner remains still while you practice establishing Back Mount and holding the position. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Partner drill "Seatbelt Grip" and "Hooks" with light resistance: Partner attempts to peel your grips or turn out, allowing you to practice maintaining connection. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    4. "Seatbelt Grip" and "Hooks" retention drill: From Back Mount, your partner attempts to escape by turning or bridging. You focus solely on maintaining your grips and hooks. (5 rounds of 30 seconds, 75% resistance)
    5. Back Mount entry and control drill: Start from a neutral position, and work to achieve Back Mount. Once secured, hold for 15 seconds before resetting. (10 reps, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling focusing on Back Mount entry and retention: Prioritize achieving Back Mount and maintaining it for as long as possible, or attempting a submission from it. (3 rounds of 3 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After a successful takedown where you land behind your opponent.
    • During scrambles when you secure an overhook and can transition to the back.
    • When an opponent attempts to turtle and you can secure the position before they fully defend.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Bridging and Turning: If the attacker's hooks are loose, bridge your hips forcefully towards their hooks and simultaneously turn your body into their overhook arm to create space and escape.
    • Elbow-Knee Escape: If the attacker's hooks are tight, drive your knees to the mat and bring your elbows to your knees, forming a protective shell. Then, attempt to rotate your hips to face the attacker.
    • Peeling Hooks: With tight grips, the attacker can try to peel your hooks off by extending their legs and creating space. You must anticipate this by tightening your hooks and driving your hips forward, making it difficult for them to create leverage.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent always escape my Back Mount when I try to control their arms?

    You are likely neglecting the most crucial aspect: hip connection and hook stability. Instead of focusing solely on arm control, ensure your hips are glued to their lower back and your hooks are actively digging into their inner thighs. This "seatbelt" structure with strong hooks prevents them from creating the necessary leverage to escape or posture up, allowing you to then safely isolate and attack their limbs.

    Q: How do I maintain Back Mount against a much larger and stronger opponent?

    Leverage and weight distribution are paramount. Do not try to match their strength directly. Instead, focus on keeping your chest glued to their upper back and your hooks tight to their inner thighs. Use your hips to constantly adjust your weight, preventing them from posturing up or turning into you. A solid "seatbelt grip" on their collar and wrist is essential for controlling their head and upper body.

    Q: Is it better to go for a submission immediately from Back Mount or try to secure the position first?

    For white belts, securing the position first is always the priority. A hasty submission attempt without proper control will likely result in your opponent escaping. Focus on establishing a strong "seatbelt grip" and tight "hooks" that prevent any movement. Once their posture is broken and they are trapped, then you can confidently initiate a submission like a rear-naked choke.
