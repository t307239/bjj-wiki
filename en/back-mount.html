Back Mount: The Ultimate BJJ Position Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Back Mount is a dominant BJJ position where you control your opponent from behind, ideally with your legs hooked around their hips. It allows for superior control and access to submissions, aiming to finish fights quickly and safely.

White belts often struggle with Back Mount because they focus on 'holding on' rather than establishing proper body mechanics and control points. This leads to them being easily dislodged or losing the position due to poor weight distribution.

The key mechanical insight is the "seatbelt grip" combined with tight "hooks" (feet inside opponent's thighs), creating a stable base and preventing escapes by controlling the opponent's hips and shoulders. This allows for controlled transitions and submission attempts.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the Seatbelt Grip: Place your left arm across the opponent's chest, gripping your own right bicep with your left hand. Your right arm should go under their armpit, gripping your own left bicep.
    2. Establish Hooks: Insert your feet inside the opponent's thighs, with your heels digging towards their glutes. This is crucial for maintaining your base.
    3. Hip Angle: Angle your hips slightly to one side, creating a tight connection. Avoid staying directly on their back, which allows them to posture up.
    4. Weight Distribution: Drive your chest into their upper back. Your weight should be distributed forward, pressing them down and preventing them from turning.
    5. Control the Shoulders: Use your arms to prevent the opponent from turning their shoulders towards you. Keep their head turned away from the side you are angled towards.
    6. Maintain Connection: Ensure your hips are always connected to their hips. Any space created is an opportunity for escape.
    7. Leg Tightness: Keep your hooks tight and active, constantly adjusting to prevent them from clearing your legs.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Neck Strain: When attempting to secure the Seatbelt Grip, a white belt might forcefully pull the opponent's head down with one arm while the other arm is not yet securely in place. This can cause hyperextension or flexion injuries to the cervical spine (neck) due to excessive torque on the ligaments and vertebrae. The correct method involves controlling the shoulders and chest first, then securing the grip with controlled movement.
    • Knee Ligament Damage: Attempting to 'hook' the opponent's legs by extending your legs too far or at an awkward angle, especially when the opponent is posturing up or moving laterally, can lead to anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) or medial collateral ligament (MCL) tears in the knee. The hooks should be inserted and maintained with a bent knee and active pressure inwards.
    • Lower Back Strain: A white belt might arch their lower back excessively to try and 'stick' to the opponent's back, especially when losing balance. This puts immense pressure on the lumbar spine and can lead to muscle strains or disc compression. Maintaining a neutral spine and using core engagement with hip connection is the correct approach.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drills (0% Resistance): Practice establishing the Seatbelt Grip and Hooks from a kneeling position on a dummy or mat. Focus on smooth transitions and body positioning. (20 reps)
    2. Passive Partner Drill (25% Resistance): With a compliant partner, practice taking the Back Mount from a control position (e.g., opponent on hands and knees). Focus on securing the Seatbelt and Hooks without resistance. (10 reps)
    3. Controlled Entry Drill (50% Resistance): Your partner offers light resistance as you transition to Back Mount from a common starting point like a failed takedown or guard pass attempt. Focus on maintaining position. (15 reps)
    4. Escaping Back Mount Drill (75% Resistance): Your partner attempts to escape from Back Mount. You focus on maintaining your hooks and Seatbelt grip, preventing them from clearing your legs. (10 reps)
    5. Live Rolling - Focus on Entry (90% Resistance): During live rolling, your primary goal is to successfully achieve Back Mount at least once. Focus on the transition and initial control. (3 rounds)
    6. Live Rolling - Full Application (100% Resistance): Integrate Back Mount seamlessly into your rolling. Practice maintaining the position, transitioning to submissions, and defending escapes. (5 rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After a successful takedown where you land behind the opponent.
    • When the opponent attempts to turtle and you can secure a grip on their hips or back.
    • During a scramble where you can isolate and control one of their legs, then transition behind.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The 'Trap and Roll' Escape: If the opponent has weak hooks, you can try to peel one of their feet off your hip. Then, turn into them, bridging your hips and rolling to try and end up in their guard or on top. Requires precise timing and hip movement.
    • The 'Elbow Escape': If the opponent's hooks are too far down your thighs, you can frame with your forearm between their hooks and your hips. Then, bridge and shrimp your hips away to create space and attempt to roll or turn into them. This requires good core strength and hip mobility.
    • The 'Arm Slice' Defense: If they are too high on your back, you can attempt to 'slice' your arm between their body and yours, creating a barrier. This often leads to them losing their grip and can be a precursor to turning into them or escaping.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do my shoulders and neck hurt when I try to get the seatbelt grip on someone's back in BJJ?

    This pain usually occurs when you try to force the seatbelt grip by pulling the opponent's head and upper body with excessive force. Instead of pulling their head, focus on controlling their shoulder and chest with your arms and body. Secure the grip by reaching across their chest to your opposite bicep, and under their armpit to your other bicep, ensuring your elbows are tucked and your spine is neutral.

    Q: How can I maintain Back Mount against a much bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary goal is to prevent them from creating space. Use your hooks to keep their hips tight to your own, and angle your body slightly to one side. Drive your chest into their upper back, not just their shoulders, to minimize their ability to posture up. Focus on maintaining a strong seatbelt grip and actively using your core to absorb their weight and movements.

    Q: I keep losing Back Mount right after I get it. What am I doing wrong biomechanically?

    Losing Back Mount often happens because you're not establishing a strong enough 'frame' or connection. Ensure your hooks are deeply embedded inside their thighs, not just on their calves. Crucially, your chest should be pressed into their upper back, and your hips should be angled, preventing them from turning into you or posturing up. Think of it as 'riding' their back like a horse, not just holding on.
