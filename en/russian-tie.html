The Russian Tie: A Biomechanically Sound BJJ Takedown for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Russian Tie is initiated from a standing clinch, typically after a collar grip. Its primary goal is to off-balance your opponent and secure a takedown by controlling their posture and head.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage and an overreliance on brute strength. They might try to force the movement instead of using angles and weight transfer.

The key mechanical insight is to use your opponent's forward momentum against them by creating a strong cross-body connection and then attacking their base with a controlled hip displacement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent standing, you achieve a collar grip with your right hand on their left collar, palm facing up, thumb inside.
    2. Simultaneously, your left arm reaches across their back, securing a grip on the back of their neck or the upper trapezius, just below the occipital bone.
    3. Your hips should be slightly angled, approximately 30 degrees to your right, creating a frame between your bodies.
    4. Your lead foot (left foot) steps slightly forward and to the outside of their lead foot (their left foot).
    5. Drive your right shoulder into their chest, slightly below their chin, while simultaneously retracting your left arm, pulling their head down and to your right.
    6. Shift your weight onto your lead left leg, driving your hips forward and slightly to your right, disrupting their base.
    7. Execute a step-through with your right leg behind their left leg, if possible, to complete the takedown to your right side.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to yank the head down with only arm strength: This can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears in both you and your opponent due to uncontrolled force.
    • Stiff-arming the collar grip without hip engagement: This puts excessive strain on your elbow joint, risking a hyperextension injury.
    • Stepping directly forward instead of angling the hips: This exposes your knee to potential twisting injuries, especially if the opponent resists by stepping backward.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip acquisition and body positioning without a partner. Focus on hip angle and shoulder drive. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill with no resistance: Your partner stands passively. Focus on the sequence of grips, footwork, and hip movement. (25 reps)
    3. Partner drill with light resistance (25%): Partner offers minimal resistance to the pull and hip drive. Focus on feeling the connection. (20 reps)
    4. Partner drill with moderate resistance (50%): Partner actively tries to maintain balance, allowing you to feel the effect of the Russian Tie. (15 reps)
    5. Partner drill with significant resistance (75%): Partner uses their weight and posture to resist. Focus on adjusting your angles and weight transfer. (10 reps)
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Attempt the Russian Tie during sparring, focusing on timing and correct execution under pressure.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up from a closed guard or half guard, creating a standing opportunity.
    • During a collar-and-sleeve or double collar grip exchange in the clinch.
    • When you have successfully controlled your opponent's head and upper body posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Base Reinforcement: If you feel the Russian Tie starting, immediately widen your base and drive your hips back towards the mat to resist the forward pressure.
    • Collar Grip Release and Footwork Adjustment: As soon as the head is pulled, release the collar grip and step your lead foot backward, creating distance and breaking the cross-body connection.
    • Neck Crank Defense and Posture Recovery: If the head pull is aggressive, use your free hand to defend your neck and simultaneously drive your posture upwards, creating space to disengage.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to pull their head down for the Russian Tie?

    Your shoulder is likely hurting because you are trying to use only arm strength to pull the head, creating a shearing force on the joint. Instead, engage your core and hips by driving your shoulder into their chest and rotating your torso. This transfers the pulling force through your entire body, not just your arm.

    Q: How can I effectively use the Russian Tie against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and angles are paraMount. Focus on getting your body deeper into the clinch, ensuring your shoulder is firmly against their chest. Instead of a direct pull, use your hips to drive them backward and off-balance, attacking their base with your legwork rather than relying solely on upper body strength.

    Q: When is the best moment to initiate the Russian Tie in BJJ?

    The optimal time to initiate the Russian Tie is when your opponent is slightly off-balance or moving forward into you, especially after you've secured a dominant collar grip. Look for moments when their posture is compromised or when they are attempting to establish their own control, creating an opening for you to exploit their movement.
