The Ultimate Russian Tie Takedown Guide for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Russian Tie is a standing control position that initiates a takedown, typically from a collar-grip clinch. It allows you to break your opponent's posture and off-balance them.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise control, relying on brute strength instead of leverage. This leads to wasted energy and openings for counters.

The key is to use your head and shoulder to control their head and neck, creating a structural imbalance you can exploit.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent grip: Collar grip with your right hand on their left collar, palm facing inwards.
    2. Your head placement: Place your right temple firmly against their left temple, driving your head into their neck and shoulder.
    3. Your left hand grip: Secure a grip on their left elbow or upper bicep, preventing them from pulling their arm away.
    4. Weight distribution: Shift your weight onto your lead leg (left leg), keeping your hips low and slightly forward.
    5. Shoulder pressure: Drive your right shoulder into their upper back, collapsing their posture forward.
    6. Lumbar flexion: Use your head and shoulder pressure to force their spine to flex forward at the lumbar region.
    7. Off-balancing motion: As they flex forward, step your right foot slightly behind your left foot and pull their elbow forward while pushing their head down with your head.
    8. Takedown execution: Drive through with your hips and legs to complete the takedown, aiming to land in a dominant position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect head placement: Driving your head directly into their forehead can cause concussions for both parties. The correct placement is temple-to-temple, driving into their neck/shoulder for control.
    • Excessive neck cranking: Applying rotational force to their neck can injure the cervical spine ligaments. Maintain head pressure forward and slightly down, not lateral rotation.
    • Over-reliance on arm strength: Trying to pull their arm with only your bicep strength will lead to shoulder impingement. Use your entire bodyweight and leverage to move their arm.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill (0% resistance): Practice the grip and head placement with a dummy or shadow opponent, focusing on body angles and weight transfer for 10 reps.
    2. Partner drill - static (25% resistance): With a willing partner, practice the grips and initial head/shoulder pressure without movement, focusing on feel for 10 reps.
    3. Partner drill - controlled movement (50% resistance): Partner stands still, you execute the entire Russian Tie sequence, guiding their movement and completing the takedown for 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drill - dynamic (75% resistance): Partner offers light resistance, allowing you to feel the off-balancing and drive through for 5 reps per side.
    5. Live rolling - focused attempt (90% resistance): During rolling, actively look for opportunities to initiate the Russian Tie and attempt the takedown, focusing on technique over success for 3 minutes.
    6. Live rolling - spontaneous (100% resistance): Use the Russian Tie naturally within the flow of rolling, aiming for successful execution against full resistance for 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent clinches with a collar grip and you have a similar grip.
    • When your opponent attempts a collar-tie or headlock and you can secure the Russian Tie grip first.
    • When you are pressed against the cage and can use it to help break posture before initiating the tie.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Whizzer counter: If they attempt the Russian Tie, immediately bring your right arm (on their left side) over their back and hook your hand onto their right armpit, creating a strong whizzer to prevent posture break.
    • Footwork escape: As they drive their head in, step your left foot back and to the side, creating space and breaking their postural control. Immediately transition to a single leg or sprawl.
    • Grip break and sprawl: If you feel the pressure mounting, quickly break their collar grip with your left hand and simultaneously sprawl your legs back to defend the takedown.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Russian Tie?

    Your neck pain likely stems from either the opponent's head pressure being misaligned, or you are trying to force the tie with neck muscles alone. Ensure your temple is firmly on their temple and drive your shoulder into their upper back, using your entire body's weight to break their posture, not just neck flexion.

    Q: How can I use the Russian Tie against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on getting your head deeper into their neck and shoulder pocket, creating a strong anchor. Use their momentum against them by stepping in and driving your hips forward as you pull their elbow, rather than trying to lift them. Prioritize breaking their posture before attempting the takedown.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Russian Tie during a BJJ match?

    The Russian Tie is most effective when your opponent is postured up and you have a strong collar grip. It's also a great option when transitioning from a failed takedown or when you've just secured a dominant grip in a clinch. Avoid attempting it when you are already off-balanced or they have a dominant grip on you.
