Deep Half Guard: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Deep Half Guard is a guard position initiated from a bottom half guard scenario, aiming to control the opponent's leg and hip to set up sweeps or submissions.

White belts often struggle with Deep Half Guard due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and proper framing, leading to them being easily passed or injured.

The core principle is creating a strong base and using your body as a wedge to prevent the opponent from settling their weight, allowing you to manipulate their structure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a standard half guard, secure your opponent's leg by placing your left arm (if right-handed) under their knee pit, gripping your own right bicep. Your right leg hooks behind their right shin, creating a "hook".
    2. Your left leg slides inside their right thigh, creating a "wedge" between your thigh and their hip, ensuring your knee is pointed towards their hip.
    3. Turn your hips 45 degrees away from your opponent, so your left hip is closer to the mat than your right hip. This creates an angle.
    4. Drive your left shoulder into their right hip, creating downward pressure and preventing them from posturing up.
    5. Secure a "seatbelt" grip: your right arm wraps around their waist, with your left hand gripping your right wrist. This prevents them from posturing.
    6. Maintain a strong frame with your left leg, keeping it tight between their legs to prevent them from passing.
    7. Shift your weight slightly onto your left hip, driving your hips forward and up into their hip. This creates a lifting and sweeping pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to "pull" your opponent down by yanking their collar without establishing a proper base can hyperextend your elbow joint if they resist, leading to ligamentous tears (e.g., UCL sprain).
    • Trying to "muscle" through a pass by keeping your hips flat and square can lead to your knee being trapped and twisted, potentially damaging the ACL or meniscus.
    • Over-extending your hips to "lift" your opponent without a secure seatbelt grip can result in your own back being exposed to a pass or a dangerous submission like a leg lock.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice entering the Deep Half Guard position from a seated guard, focusing on the leg hook and hip wedge mechanics. (5 minutes, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner stands, you are on the mat. Practice securing the Deep Half Guard grips and wedge. Partner gently resists. (5 minutes, 25% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (entry and hold): Partner attempts a basic pass (e.g., knee slice). You secure Deep Half Guard and hold the position. (5 minutes, 50% resistance).
    4. Sweep attempt drill: From Deep Half Guard, practice initiating a basic sweep (e.g., elevator sweep) with light resistance from your partner. (5 minutes, 60% resistance).
    5. Guard retention drill: Your partner actively tries to pass your Deep Half Guard. Focus on maintaining your frame and hip wedge. (5 minutes, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (positional sparring): Start in Deep Half Guard and attempt to sweep or submit. Partner defends and attempts to pass. (3 rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is pressuring forward into your half guard with their weight on their knees.
    • When they attempt to "stack" you from a half guard position.
    • When you have a strong underhook or crossface in half guard and can transition.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass: The opponent drives their weight forward, attempting to flatten you. Maintain your hip wedge and use your leg to push their knee away, creating space to recover to a standard half guard or butterfly guard.
    • Knee Slice Pass: The opponent drives their knee across your body. Use your "hook" leg to maintain distance and your wedge leg to prevent their knee from sliding past your hip. Look to shrimp out or switch to a different guard.
    • Hip Escape/Shuck: The opponent attempts to step their leg over your body. Keep your hips tight, use your wedge to control their leg, and focus on creating an angle to escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to get into Deep Half Guard?

    Your knee pain likely stems from the "wedge" leg. If your knee is not properly aligned with your opponent's hip and your thigh is not creating a solid base, your knee joint is absorbing shear forces. Ensure your knee is pointed towards their hip and your thigh is pressing firmly into their inner thigh, creating a stable frame instead of a vulnerable point.

    Q: How do I use Deep Half Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and angles are crucial. Focus on using your body as a wedge to prevent their weight from settling. Instead of trying to lift them directly, use your hip angle to "unbalance" them. The seatbelt grip becomes paraMount to control their posture and prevent them from using their size advantage to crush you.

    Q: You should transition out if your opponent has successfully "stacked" you and their weight is firmly on your chest, or if they have managed to get their knee past your hip. In these situations, attempting a sweep is often too late. Focus on recovering to a more stable guard like closed guard or standard half guard by shrimping out or creating an angle.
