BJJ Mount: The Ultimate White Belt Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Mount is an offensive position achieved when you are on top of your opponent, with your torso over their torso and your hips in contact with their hips.

It offers superior control and access to submissions, making it a dominant position to secure.

White belts often struggle with maintaining the Mount due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and base.

The key insight is to maintain a strong, low base with your hips driving forward, creating a "seatbelt" of control around their upper body.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Side Control, establish cross-face control with your lead arm under their chin and your forearm pressing against their cheekbone.
    2. Your trail arm secures a "seatbelt" grip on their far shoulder blade, preventing them from turning into you.
    3. Drive your hips forward, sinking them low towards their hips, creating a stable base.
    4. Bring your lead knee up and over their torso, aiming to place it near their far armpit.
    5. Your trail leg hooks their hip on the same side as your lead knee, creating an inverted 'V' shape with your legs.
    6. Distribute your weight primarily onto their chest and sternum, with your hips acting as a fulcrum.
    7. Keep your knees tight to their body, preventing them from creating space to bridge or shrimp.
    8. Maintain a strong head position, typically looking over their shoulder, to help balance and control their upper body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing knees too high on the opponent's torso, often above the rib cage: This allows the opponent to bridge effectively, potentially causing hyperextension or sprains in the instructor's knees or ankles as they are pulled down.
    • Allowing hips to drift back, creating a wide base: This reduces forward pressure and gives the opponent space to shrimp, potentially leading to knee or ankle twists if they escape.
    • Gripping the opponent's gi too loosely or not at all: This makes it easy for them to create space and escape, risking shoulder or elbow injuries for the attacker if they over-extend.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice transitioning from side control to Mount, focusing on hip placement and knee drive, 10 reps per side, 0% resistance.
    2. Partner drill: With a compliant partner, practice establishing Mount from side control, holding for 10 seconds, 10 reps per side, 25% resistance.
    3. Partner drill: Practice maintaining Mount for 30 seconds while the partner attempts to escape, focus on hip pressure and leg control, 5 reps per side, 50% resistance.
    4. Flow drill: Transition between Side Control, Knee-on-Belly, and Mount with a partner, focusing on smooth transitions and maintaining control, 5 minutes, 60% resistance.
    5. Positional sparring: Start in Side Control, aim to achieve Mount. Opponent defends Mount and tries to escape. Focus on maintaining position. 5 rounds x 2 minutes, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Incorporate the Mount position into your rolling. Aim to secure and maintain Mount, or transition to submissions from Mount. 5 rounds x 3 minutes, 90% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After successfully passing the opponent's guard and achieving a dominant top position like Side Control or Knee-on-Belly.
    • When the opponent attempts a submission from guard that you can defend and exploit to move to a superior position.
    • Following a successful sweep where you land on top in a position that allows for an easy transition to Mount.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Bridge and Shrimp: When the attacker's hips are too high, the defender bridges their hips up, lifting the attacker, then shrimps their hips out to create space and escape.
    • Elbow Escape: If the attacker's knees are too far apart, the defender places an elbow on the mat and shrimps their hips to create space and recover guard.
    • Hip Escape to Guard Recovery: If the attacker's weight is too far forward, the defender uses their feet to push off the mat and shrimp their hips back, regaining guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to stay in Mount BJJ?

    Your back pain is likely caused by an anterior pelvic tilt, meaning your hips are sticking out behind you. To fix this, consciously drive your hips forward and down towards your opponent's hips, engaging your glutes. This creates a stable base and reduces the strain on your lower back by aligning your spine.

    Q: How do I maintain Mount against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on minimizing the space between your hips and theirs. Keep your knees tucked tightly to their sides and your hips low and forward. Utilize your forearms to frame against their biceps and shoulders to prevent them from posturing up and using their weight effectively.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Side Control to Mount in BJJ?

    The optimal time to transition from Side Control to Mount is when your opponent is actively trying to regain guard or turn away from you. Use their movement against them by driving your hips forward and switching your legs to secure the Mount before they can re-establish their defensive structure.
