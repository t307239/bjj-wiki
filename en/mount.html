Mount is achieved when you are on top of your opponent, straddling their torso, with your knees and feet off the mat. It offers superior control and access to submissions.

White belts often struggle with mount by being too high, too low, or losing base, allowing their opponent to escape easily.

The core principle is maintaining a stable base by keeping your weight forward and hips low, creating a crushing pressure that limits your opponent's movement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, transition your hips forward, bringing your knee closest to your opponent's head towards their armpit.
    2. Drive your other knee across their torso, aiming to place it on the opposite side of their body, perpendicular to their spine.
    3. As your second knee clears their hips, immediately sit your hips down, lowering your center of gravity.
    4. Extend your legs, creating a 'figure-four' or 'grapevines' by hooking your feet behind your opponent's ankles or heels.
    5. Grip your opponent's upper thighs with your forearms, pressing them into their hips to prevent them from bridging.
    6. Maintain a slight forward lean, driving your chest towards their chest, keeping your hips low and tight.
    7. Ensure your knees are wide enough to prevent your opponent from easily bringing them together, but not so wide that you lose balance.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing knees too high on the opponent's torso: This strains the opponent's lumbar spine and can lead to acute lower back injuries (disc herniation or facet joint sprain) for them. Keep knees lower, closer to their hips.
    • Allowing opponent to bridge with your hips elevated: This can cause hyperextension of your own lower back if you are not braced, leading to muscle strains or ligament sprains. Keep hips low and drive weight forward.
    • Gripping opponent's biceps or shoulders instead of thighs: This gives the opponent more leverage to shrimp or bridge, and can cause shoulder impingement or elbow hyperextension injuries to your own arms if they resist strongly. Grip their thighs firmly.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side control to mount, focusing on hip movement and knee placement. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner lies flat. Practice establishing mount and holding it for 10 seconds. (20 reps, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to your transition. Focus on maintaining base. (20 reps, 25% resistance).
    4. Controlled transition drill: Start in side control. Attempt to mount; partner may try to prevent it but not actively escape. (15 reps, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled mount maintenance: Start in mount. Partner attempts to escape using basic bridging or shrimping. You focus on maintaining base. (15 reps, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Focus on achieving and maintaining mount for short periods during sparring. (3 rounds, 90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After successfully passing the guard and achieving a dominant top position like side control.
    • When your opponent attempts to shrimp or escape from side control, creating an opening for you to advance.
    • When your opponent is flat on their back and you have established a strong top control, making it difficult for them to create frames.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Bridge and roll: The opponent bridges hard, lifting their hips and rolling you over. This requires timing and a strong grip on the legs to prevent the roll.
    • Shrimp (hip escape): The opponent uses their hips to create space and move away, often aiming to get their knee inside. This is countered by maintaining tight hips and forward pressure.
    • Elbow escape: The opponent brings their elbow to the inside, creating a frame to push you away and potentially transition to guard. This is countered by controlling the elbow and maintaining chest-to-chest pressure.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to hold Mount in BJJ?

    This is often due to having your hips too high and your weight too far back. Your lower back is then carrying the load, creating strain. To fix this, focus on driving your hips down towards your opponent's hips and keeping your chest connected to their chest. This transfers the weight to your legs and opponent, reducing back pressure.

    Q: How do I maintain Mount against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary focus must be on controlling their hips and maintaining a low base. Use 'grapevine' hooks by wrapping your feet around their ankles or heels to prevent them from bridging. Keep your weight distributed over their hips, not their chest, making it harder for them to use their size to buck you off. Think about controlling their base with your legs.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a submission from Mount in BJJ?

    The best time to attack from Mount is *after* you have secured a stable position and your opponent is reacting defensively. Look for opportunities when they are trying to defend a specific limb or are struggling to escape. For instance, if they lift an arm to defend a choke, that arm becomes a submission opportunity (like an Armbar). Don't rush submissions; establish control first.
