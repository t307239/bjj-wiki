Your First BJJ Mount: A White Belt's Essential Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Mount is a dominant top position achieved after successfully passing your opponent's guard and establishing control from above.

White belts often struggle with Mount due to a lack of body awareness, leading to poor weight distribution and easy escapes.

The key is to create a stable base by connecting your hips to their hips and controlling their posture to prevent them from bridging or shrimping out.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a passed guard position, drive your hips down towards your opponent's hips, creating a tight connection.
    2. Place your knees on either side of their torso, keeping your feet flexed and off the mat.
    3. Your shins should be angled slightly forward, creating a 'seatbelt' effect with your legs.
    4. Distribute your weight evenly over their chest and hips, avoiding sitting too far back or forward.
    5. Maintain a strong upper body base by keeping your chest close to their chest, preventing them from posturing up.
    6. Secure one of your opponent's arms with a "s-grip" (thumb wrapped around the wrist, fingers gripping the forearm) to control their posture and prevent bridging.
    7. Simultaneously, secure the opposite side collar with a "collar grip" (thumb inside the collar, fingers outside) to further break down their posture and maintain control.
    8. Your hips should be angled slightly to one side, allowing you to "hip-in" and maintain pressure, preventing them from "shrimping" away.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing knees too wide apart: This can strain the adductor muscles (groin) and create a gap for your opponent to "shrimp" their hips through, leading to loss of position.
    • Sitting too far back on the opponent's hips: This encourages them to "bridge" upwards, potentially injuring your lower back or knees as you are pulled into an awkward position.
    • Letting your feet touch the mat: This provides your opponent with a "frame" and an easy way to "hook" your legs, leading to sweeps or guard recovery, and can also put undue pressure on your ankle ligaments.
    • Overextending your arms to "push" the opponent down: This can lead to losing your base and can strain your elbow joints, making you susceptible to "armbar" attempts.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Connect & Weight Transfer: Practice sitting down from a passed guard position, connecting your hips to an imaginary opponent's hips, and transferring your weight forward. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Knee Placement & Shin Angle: Practice placing your knees on either side of a dummy or training partner's torso and angling your shins forward. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Grip Simulation: Practice grabbing a collar and controlling an arm with an s-grip on a dummy or stationary partner. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    4. Mount Stabilization Drill: From a passed guard, get to Mount and hold for 10 seconds. Partner provides minimal resistance (light pushing). (10 reps, 25% resistance)
    5. Mount Control Drill: Get to Mount and maintain control for 30 seconds while the partner actively tries to escape (shrimping, bridging). You focus on maintaining base and weight. (10 reps, 50% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling - Mount Focus: During rolling, prioritize achieving and maintaining Mount for at least 30 seconds before attempting submissions or switching positions. (5 rounds, 75-90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After successfully passing your opponent's guard (e.g., from a knee-slice pass or torreando pass).
    • When your opponent is on their back and their legs are extended or their guard is broken.
    • As a transition from side control when your opponent attempts to "bridge" or "buck" you off.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Shrimping": When the opponent attempts to "shrimp" their hips away, "hip-in" with your hips towards them and drive your knees further apart to maintain pressure.
    • "Bridging": If the opponent "bridges" upwards, "posture up" slightly, keeping your chest close to theirs, and "hip-in" to their hips to break their bridge momentum.
    • "Elbow-Knee Escape": If the opponent tries to bring their elbow and knee together to create space, "smash" your knee down on their hip and "cut" across their body to maintain a tight chest-to-chest connection.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent always escape Mount so easily when I get there?

    This is common for white belts. You're likely not connecting your hips to their hips firmly enough, creating space for them to shrimp. Ensure your weight is distributed over their chest and hips, and actively "hip-in" to their body. Also, check that your feet are not flat on the mat, which gives them a frame to push off.

    Q: How do I maintain Mount against a much larger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on "cutting" your hips in and "smashing" your weight down onto their chest. Use your "collar grip" and "arm control grip" to break their posture and prevent them from using their size to "bridge" effectively. Think of connecting your sternum to their sternum.

    Q: The best time is when you have established a strong, stable Mount and your opponent is actively defending by trying to escape, rather than posturing up. Look for opportunities when their arm is extended or their neck is exposed, often after they have failed to bridge or shrimp effectively. Don't rush; solidify your position first.
