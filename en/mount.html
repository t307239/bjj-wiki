Mount: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Mount is a dominant top position where you are seated on your opponent's torso, controlling their hips and upper body.

White belts often struggle with maintaining mount due to improper weight distribution, leading to easy escapes.

The key to secure mount is anchoring your base directly over your opponent's center of mass, creating a stable platform.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, transition to a "grapevine" by placing your shins across your opponent's inner thighs.
    2. Drive your hips down and forward, ensuring your knees are wider than your opponent's shoulders.
    3. Secure "S-Mount" by bringing one knee (e.g., right knee) to their hip crease, perpendicular to their spine.
    4. Swing your other leg (left leg) over their torso, placing your left foot on the mat behind their head.
    5. Your left shin should be across their upper chest, creating a "frame" with your knee and foot.
    6. Distribute your weight by driving your hips down and slightly forward, directly over their sternum.
    7. Grip their collar with your right hand, pulling their head down towards your chest for control.
    8. Grip their far sleeve (left sleeve) with your left hand, pulling it across their body towards your right hip.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Sitting too high on the opponent's chest with knees close together: This allows the opponent to bridge and roll you, potentially causing knee hyperextension or ACL tears.
    • Gripping opponent's arms too tightly without maintaining hip pressure: This can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears if the opponent forces an escape.
    • Allowing opponent to turn their hips into you: This creates a "cheese grater" effect on your shins and can lead to shin splints or knee ligament strain; maintain a wide base and drive your hips forward.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo movement: Practice transitioning from side control to S-mount, focusing on hip movement and leg placement (5 reps).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): From side control, have a partner lie still and practice mounting, focusing on hip pressure and weight distribution (10 reps).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Execute the mount transition with a partner offering minimal resistance, focusing on maintaining position against a soft body (15 reps).
    4. Partner drill (controlled resistance): Practice mounting against a partner who actively tries to prevent it but without aggressive escapes (20 reps).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Spar with a partner, focusing on achieving and maintaining mount for at least 10 seconds (5 rounds, 50% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (full): Spar with a partner, aiming to secure mount and hold it for as long as possible, simulating a real match (5 rounds, 75-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After successfully passing the guard and establishing side control.
    • When your opponent attempts a submission from guard and you can shrimp or step over their legs.
    • When you have achieved a dominant top position like knee-on-belly and can transition upwards.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): If the opponent is trying to mount you from a compromised position, immediately drive your hips away and create space to recover guard.
    • Bridge and Roll: If the opponent has mount but is not controlling your hips effectively, bridge your hips sharply and attempt to roll them to the side, creating an escape opportunity.
    • Elbow Escape: If the opponent has mount and their weight is not distributed correctly, use your elbow to create a frame against their hip and shrimp out.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do my shins hurt so much when I try to do Mount?

    Shin pain in mount often occurs when your shins are pressing directly against your opponent's hips or ribs without proper framing. Ensure your knees are wide and your shins are angled across their torso, not digging into bone. Distribute your weight onto your hips and chest, not solely your shins, to alleviate pressure.

    Q: How do I get to Mount against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on speed and precision of your transition rather than brute force. Use their size against them by quickly transitioning to S-mount, placing your knee on their hip bone and swinging your other leg over their shoulder. Drive your hips down and forward, aiming to sit on their sternum to establish a stable base, preventing them from using their weight to crush you.

    Q: When should I stop trying to get to Mount and just pass the guard?

    You should prioritize guard passing if your opponent has a very strong Closed Guard or is actively defending your transition attempts with significant pressure. If you find yourself struggling to establish a stable position to begin your mount transition, or if they are consistently shutting down your path with framing and hip movement, it's often more efficient to reset and attempt a different guard pass.
