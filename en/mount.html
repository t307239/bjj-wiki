The Essential Mount Position in BJJ

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Mount is a dominant grappling position where you control your opponent from on top, facing their torso. It offers excellent control and numerous submission opportunities.

Many white belts struggle by placing their weight too high or too low, allowing their opponent to bridge or shrimp out easily.

The key is to maintain a low center of gravity, connect your hips to their hips, and control their upper body base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, transition your hips forward, bringing your knee closest to their head over their hip bone. Maintain a wide base with your supporting leg.
    2. Drive your hips down and slightly forward, creating a connection between your pelvis and their pelvis. Your chest should be heavy on their chest.
    3. Place your knee from the bridging leg onto the mat, creating a stable tripod with your feet and the knee on the mat.
    4. Bring your second leg over their body, typically tucking your shin across their hip or belly, ensuring your heel is near their knee.
    5. Establish a strong gable grip (interlock fingers) around their neck, or secure a high collar grip with both hands, thumbs inside.
    6. Keep your elbows tight to your body, preventing them from creating space to bridge or shrimp.
    7. Distribute your weight primarily through your hips and chest, with a slight forward pressure, keeping your base wide and stable.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Placing knees too close together or too far apart: This compromises your base and allows the opponent to use their legs to create space, potentially leading to knee ligament strain (ACL/MCL) when attempting to bridge or shrimp.
    • Lifting hips too high off the opponent: This removes your base of support and weight, allowing the opponent to bridge and roll you, risking shoulder dislocation or neck injury from impact.
    • Gripping too loosely or on the arms: This gives the opponent opportunities to escape or counter, and can lead to wrist sprains or finger injuries if they explosively pull away or twist.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning from side control to mount on an empty mat, focusing on hip connection and weight distribution. (20 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Execute the mount transition with a compliant partner, focusing on the mechanics. (10 reps)
    3. Light pressure drilling (25% resistance): Partner attempts to lightly resist the transition, focusing on maintaining control despite minor movement.
    4. Controlled transition (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the mount but without explosive movements or counters. Focus on maintaining position.
    5. Simulated rolling (75% resistance): Partner attempts to actively defend and escape, but the focus is on maintaining mount for a set time (e.g., 30 seconds) before transitioning.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Implement the mount transition during live sparring, aiming to achieve and maintain the position.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After successfully passing guard and establishing side control.
    • When the opponent is turtled and you have control of their hips.
    • Following a sweep where you end up in a dominant top position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Bridge and Roll: The opponent attempts to bridge their hips into you, lifting you off balance and trying to roll you over. Requires a strong base and control of their upper body to prevent.
    • Shrimp (Hip Escape): The opponent uses their legs to create space between your hips and theirs, attempting to regain guard. Requires maintaining hip connection and potentially adjusting your body to block their escape.
    • Elbow Escape: The opponent tries to bring their elbow to the inside of your thigh to create space and re-establish guard. Requires maintaining pressure and potentially switching your hips to prevent their elbow from getting inside.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent always bridge and escape when I try to mount?

    This usually happens because your hips are too high off their body, creating a gap. You need to drive your hips down and forward, connecting your pelvis to theirs. Imagine trying to sit on their sternum while keeping your knees wide and your feet planted for a stable base.

    Q: How do I mount a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maintaining a low center of gravity and using your body weight strategically. Instead of trying to lift them, drive your hips into them and use your chest pressure. Secure a strong gable grip or collar grip to control their head and prevent them from posturing up.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to mount from side control?

    The ideal moment is when your opponent's hips are relatively still and they haven't established strong frames. Look for opportunities after they've attempted an escape that you've successfully defended, or when they are slightly off-balance. Drive your knee over their hip decisively.
