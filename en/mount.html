Mount: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

Mount is a dominant top position where you straddle your opponent's torso, facing their head. It allows for control and submission opportunities, effectively neutralizing their guard.

Many white belts struggle with mount by either "sitting" too high, allowing escapes, or "slouching" forward, losing structural integrity and risking sweeps.

The key insight is maintaining a "hip-to-hip" connection with your opponent while driving your weight forward, creating a stable base and limiting their movement.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure your opponent's hips by placing your shins across their torso, ideally just below their hip crease. Your knees should be pointed slightly outwards to create a stable base.
    2. Drive your hips down towards your opponent's hips, aiming for a "hip-to-hip" contact. This transfers your weight directly onto their pelvis, not their stomach.
    3. Maintain a wide base by keeping your knees spread wider than your opponent's shoulders. This prevents them from bridging effectively.
    4. Keep your chest pressed against your opponent's chest, creating a "chest-to-chest" connection. This prevents them from creating space to shrimp out.
    5. Your arms should frame their upper body, either by gripping their opposite shoulders (collar grips are advanced and not recommended for white belts) or by placing your hands on their upper chest/collarbone area.
    6. Continuously adjust your weight forward and backward to counter their movements. If they try to bridge, lean back slightly; if they try to shrimp, drive forward.
    7. Ensure your feet are tucked behind their hips or are used to "hook" their legs if they attempt to bring their knees to their chest. This traps their lower body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Sitting too high on the opponent's chest: This allows the opponent to bridge and roll you by creating an "arch" with their back. The spinal erector muscles and lumbar discs are vulnerable to hyperextension and shear forces.
    • Slouching forward with rounded shoulders: This creates a "hollow" back, allowing the opponent to "hip escape" by shrimping. The cervical spine (neck) is at risk of hyperextension if the opponent frames and pushes your head away.
    • Allowing opponent to get knees inside: If your knees are too close together, the opponent can "reguard" by bringing their knees between your legs. This strains the adductor muscles of the groin and can lead to knee valgus injuries.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drills: Practice achieving mount from side control or knee-on-belly without a partner. Focus on hip placement and weight distribution. (50 reps)
    2. Static Mount Hold: Once in mount, hold the position against a compliant partner for 30 seconds. Focus on maintaining hip-to-hip and chest-to-chest pressure. (10 reps)
    3. Mount Stabilization Drill: Start in mount. The opponent attempts to escape (e.g., bridge, shrimp). You focus solely on maintaining the position without attempting submissions. (50% resistance, 2 minutes per round, 5 rounds)
    4. Mount to Submission Entry: From mount, your partner offers light resistance to your transition. Practice moving from mount to an armbar or collar choke setup. (75% resistance, 3 minutes per round, 3 rounds)
    5. Flow Rolling with Mount Focus: Engage in light rolling where the primary goal is to achieve and maintain mount, then transition to a submission. The opponent can actively defend but not aggressively attack. (90% resistance, 5 minutes per round, 3 rounds)
    6. Live Rolling: Full resistance rolling, focusing on implementing the mount position and its subsequent attacks. (100% resistance, 5 minutes per round, 3 rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • After successfully passing the opponent's guard and settling into a dominant top position like side control or knee-on-belly.
    • When the opponent "gives" you the position by turning away or exposing their back during a scramble.
    • After a successful sweep where you land directly in a stable mount rather than a less dominant position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Bridge and Roll: As the opponent settles into mount, actively bridge your hips upwards and to one side, creating an "arch" to unseat them. Maintain contact with your feet to "hook" their legs.
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): If the opponent is too high or not driving their weight forward, use your feet to frame against their hips and push away, then bring your knee inside their leg to create space and recover guard.
    • Technical Stand-up: If the opponent is loose in mount, create space with your hands on their hips and perform a technical stand-up, driving your feet into the mat and rising to your feet while keeping your hips low.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I am in mount?

    Your back likely hurts because you are slouching forward with a rounded spine, creating hyperextension in your lumbar region. To fix this, focus on driving your hips down towards your opponent's hips and maintaining a "chest-to-chest" connection, which naturally straightens your spine and engages your core for support.

    Q: How do I maintain mount against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your weight distribution is key. Instead of trying to "sit" on them, drive your hips low and "hip-to-hip" with theirs. Use your knees to create a wide, stable base, and keep your chest pressed firmly against their chest to prevent them from using their size to create space and escape.

    Q: When should I transition from mount to a submission?

    Transition from mount to a submission when you have established a secure, stable mount with good hip-to-hip pressure. If the opponent is actively defending by bridging or shrimping, it's often best to stabilize your position first. Once they are relatively still, you can begin to set up your Armbar or choke.
