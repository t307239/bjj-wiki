Open Guard is a defensive BJJ position where the practitioner is on their back, using their legs to control distance and create attacking opportunities. It's a dynamic space, unlike the static nature of Closed Guard.

White belts often struggle with Open Guard due to a lack of control over their limb placement and an over-reliance on pushing rather than framing. This leads to a loss of posture and immediate pressure passes.

The core principle of Open Guard is creating and maintaining a 'frame' with your legs and arms, using biomechanical leverage to prevent your opponent from collapsing your guard and establishing dominant positions.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From your back, knees bent and feet flat on the mat, initiate by extending one leg towards your opponent's hip crease. Grip their pant leg just above the ankle with your corresponding hand (e.g., right leg extended, right hand grips right pant leg).
    2. Simultaneously, use your other hand to establish a sleeve grip (e.g., left hand grips opponent's left sleeve). Your hips should be slightly elevated off the mat, creating a base.
    3. Maintain a slight bend in your extended leg's knee, preventing a hyperextension. Your gripping arm should be straight, creating a rigid frame.
    4. As your opponent attempts to close the distance, use your extended leg to push their hip away, maintaining the frame. Your body pivots slightly on your hips.
    5. Your free hand (not on the sleeve) can actively frame against their bicep or shoulder to further control posture and distance. Ensure your elbows are tucked.
    6. When attacking, use your gripping leg to 'hook' their leg or hip, while simultaneously driving your hips forward and slightly to the side to create an angle.
    7. Maintain constant tension and subtle adjustments in your grips and leg extensions to keep your opponent off-balance and prevent them from establishing a dominant base.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Hyperextension: When extending your leg to create distance, a white belt might fully straighten their knee while the opponent applies forward pressure. This can cause a hyperextension injury to the knee joint, potentially damaging the ACL or meniscus. The correct movement involves maintaining a micro-bend in the knee, acting as a shock absorber.
    • Wrist Sprains/Fractures: Gripping the opponent's gi too loosely or with an open palm while they attempt to break your grip can lead to wrist sprains or fractures. The opponent's rotational force can easily overwhelm a weak grip. Always maintain a firm, closed-fist grip, and use your entire forearm to create a strong lever.
    • Lower Back Strain: When trying to shrimp or adjust your hips from the guard, a white belt might lift their hips too high without proper core engagement. This can lead to a strain in the lower back muscles as they try to support excessive weight. Engage your core strongly and focus on controlled hip movement, keeping your back relatively flat.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Leg Extension & Frame Drill: Practice extending one leg, gripping your own pant leg, and holding a static frame. Repeat 20 times per leg. Resistance: 0%.
    2. Solo Hip Elevation & Frame: From your back, practice lifting your hips and extending one leg while maintaining a sleeve grip with the opposite hand. Hold for 5 seconds. Repeat 20 times. Resistance: 0%.
    3. Partner Drill - Static Guard Retention: Your partner stands in front of you, not moving. Practice establishing your open guard grips and maintaining frames. They can gently push. Repeat for 1 minute, then switch. Resistance: 25%.
    4. Partner Drill - Controlled Guard Retention: Your partner takes small steps, trying to advance. You practice extending legs and using grips to maintain distance and prevent them from reaching your hips. Repeat for 2 minutes. Resistance: 50%.
    5. Partner Drill - Basic Sweep Attempt: Your partner attempts to pass. You practice initiating a simple sweep (e.g., tripod sweep) from open guard, focusing on grip control and hip movement. Repeat for 3 minutes. Resistance: 75%.
    6. Live Rolling - Open Guard Focus: Engage in rolling with a partner, specifically focusing on maintaining and utilizing your open guard for at least 50% of the round. Prioritize positional control over submission. Resistance: 90-100%.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up and trying to establish heavy pressure.
    • When you have successfully broken your opponent's posture and they are off-balance.
    • When your opponent is attempting to establish a knee-slide or toreando pass.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The 'Spider Guard' Grip: When the opponent is inside your guard, use your hands to grip their sleeves just above the elbow. Extend your legs to push their hips away, creating a frame. This prevents them from collapsing your guard and allows for sweeps or transitions.
    • The 'De La Riva Hook': Place the sole of one foot on the outside of the opponent's lead leg, just above the knee. Use your hands to control their upper body (collar or sleeve). This creates a strong lever to off-balance and sweep them.
    • The 'Lasso Guard': Wrap one of your legs around the opponent's arm and secure a grip on their far side collar. This isolates their arm and allows for control, sweeps, or submissions like the triangle choke.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do Open Guard?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are gripping the opponent's gi with an open palm or a weak grip, especially when they are trying to break your hold. When they apply pressure or try to escape, this force is directly transferred to your delicate wrist joint. Always maintain a firm, closed-fist grip, and use your forearm as a rigid lever to absorb and redirect force, rather than relying solely on your wrist.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, your primary focus must be on distance management and leverage. Use your legs to create frames and push their hips away constantly, preventing them from closing the gap. Employ grips that allow you to control their posture and balance, such as sleeve grips or pant grips. Think of your legs as springs, always ready to push and create space, and your arms as anchors to control their upper body.

    Q: Is Open Guard only for attacking, or can I use it to defend?

    Open Guard is a highly versatile position that serves both offensive and defensive purposes. Defensively, it's used to maintain distance, prevent your opponent from passing your guard, and stop them from establishing dominant positions like Side Control or mount. By creating frames with your legs and arms, you can effectively 'wall off' your opponent and stall their progress, giving you time to set up your own attacks or transition to a more advantageous position.
