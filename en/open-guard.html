Open Guard allows you to control distance and angles from your back, preventing opponents from settling into dominant positions like Side Control or mount.

Many white belts struggle because they treat it like a static wrestling hold, losing the dynamic interplay of frames and hip movement.

The key is using your legs as flexible frames to create space and off-balance your opponent, enabling sweeps and submissions.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From your back, lift your hips slightly off the mat. Your knees should be bent to approximately 90 degrees at the hip and knee joints.
    2. Place the soles of your feet on your opponent's hips, maintaining a slight bend in your knees. This is your primary frame.
    3. Grip your opponent's sleeves or collar (e.g., right sleeve, left collar) with your hands. Keep your elbows tucked close to your body.
    4. To create space, extend one leg while simultaneously pulling the opponent's corresponding arm closer to your body using your grip.
    5. As you extend, use your hips to shrimp laterally, moving your base away from the opponent's center of gravity.
    6. Simultaneously, use the other leg's foot on the hip to push and angle your opponent, disrupting their posture and weight distribution.
    7. Maintain constant tension in your frames; do not let your legs go completely straight or your arms go limp.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Hyperextending your knees: When pushing an opponent's hip, if your knee joint goes beyond its natural range of motion, you risk tearing the ACL or PCL.
    • Correct: Maintain a slight bend in the knee, using your hamstrings and quadriceps to control the extension, never forcing the joint past its limit.
    • Gripping too wide or loosely: If your hands are far from your body or your grip is weak, the opponent can easily break your posture and pass your guard.
    • Correct: Keep your elbows tight to your ribs and secure strong grips on sleeves or collars, using your back muscles to maintain tension.
    • Allowing opponent to pin your legs: If your opponent flattens your legs against your chest, you lose your ability to create frames and space.
    • Correct: Actively use your feet on the hips to push and shrimp, never letting your legs become completely immobilized.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice shrimping and extending legs while holding a pillow or yoga block between your knees for hip mobility awareness. 20 reps each side.
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): Partner stands, you are on your back. Practice establishing frames on hips and pulling sleeves/collars. No movement from partner. 5 mins.
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner gently tries to step forward. You practice pushing hips and creating space with leg extensions. Focus on maintaining frames. 5 mins.
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner attempts to close distance and posture up. You practice shrimping and framing to prevent them from settling. Focus on hip movement. 5 mins.
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner attempts basic guard passes (e.g., torreando). You focus on using your frames to disrupt their attempt and recover guard. 3 mins.
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Apply Open Guard principles during sparring. Focus on maintaining frames and creating opportunities for sweeps, not just survival. 3 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent is attempting to pass your guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • When opponent is postured up and trying to establish heavy pressure from the top.
    • When you have successfully reset to your back after a failed submission or sweep attempt.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Spider Guard grip on sleeves and foot on hip: Use your foot on the opponent's hip to push and create an angle, while pulling their sleeve to break their posture. This sets up sweeps like the triangle choke or omoplata.
    • Lasso Guard grip and foot on hip: Wrap one arm around the opponent's arm and secure a grip on their collar or shoulder. Use your free foot on their hip to control distance and create angles for sweeps or submissions.
    • De La Riva Guard: Hook one of the opponent's legs with your foot (e.g., right leg hooks opponent's left leg behind the knee). Use your other foot on their hip or thigh to control distance and off-balance them, leading to sweeps like the trip or kimura.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to do Open Guard in BJJ?

    Your back pain is likely due to improper hip engagement and a lack of core stability. Instead of flattening your back on the mat, actively engage your glutes and core to lift your hips slightly. This creates a more neutral spine and allows your legs to function as effective frames, reducing the strain on your lower back.

    Q: How do I use Open Guard effectively against a much bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus intensely on maintaining frames and distance. Use your feet on their hips with strong, consistent pressure to prevent them from closing the gap. Prioritize breaking their posture with sleeve and collar grips, and look for opportunities to off-balance them using hip movement and leg angles.

    Q: When is the right time to switch from Closed Guard to Open Guard in BJJ?

    The optimal time to transition from Closed Guard to Open Guard is when your opponent is postured up and attempting to break your guard, or when you recognize an opportunity to create space for a sweep or submission. Actively opening your guard by extending your legs and placing your feet on their hips allows you to regain control and dictate the engagement.
