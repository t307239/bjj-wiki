Open Guard is a dynamic BJJ position where you control distance and angles with your legs extended, typically from your back. It allows for sweeps and submissions against opponents who are postured up or attempting to pass your guard.

White belts often struggle with Open Guard by becoming rigid or reaching too far, losing their base and inviting passes. They focus on holding rather than actively controlling space and leverage.

The core principle of Open Guard is creating frames with your shins and feet to manage distance and off-balance your opponent, enabling you to attack their base and posture.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From your back, extend your legs upwards, creating a base with your hips slightly elevated off the mat. Keep your knees bent at approximately 90 degrees.
    2. Establish grips: Secure an overhook grip on the opponent's right arm with your left hand (collar or sleeve) and an underhook grip on their left arm with your right hand (collar or sleeve).
    3. Position your feet: Place the sole of your left foot on the opponent's right hip joint, ensuring your ankle is directly over their hip bone.
    4. Place the sole of your right foot on the opponent's left bicep, creating a strong pushing surface.
    5. Maintain a slight gap: Keep your hips a fist's distance away from the opponent's hips to prevent them from easily collapsing your base.
    6. Create frames: Actively push with your shins and feet to create space. Your left shin acts as a barrier against their right hip, and your right shin against their left arm.
    7. Adjust angles: Slightly tilt your torso to your left, allowing your right leg to swing around and potentially attack their left leg for sweeps.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too tightly with your hands on the opponent's collar while simultaneously pushing hard with your feet: This can hyperextend your wrists, leading to wrist sprains or ligament tears. The correct movement is to maintain firm but not rigid grips, and use your legs to create the primary control and distance.
    • Extending your legs fully to push an opponent who is aggressively postured forward: This can put excessive strain on your hamstrings and lower back, potentially causing pulls or strains. Instead, bend your knees and use your shins to create a solid frame against their hips and thighs.
    • Allowing your opponent to get their hips inside your extended legs: This compromises your guard and can lead to them easily passing or achieving dominant positions. Maintain active leg pressure and adjust your hip angle to prevent this penetration.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Leg Extension and Frame Drill: Practice extending your legs and creating frames against an imaginary opponent. Focus on hip elevation and shin placement. (10 reps per leg, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo Grip and Frame Drill: Add collar and sleeve grips, practicing to hold them while maintaining leg frames. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner Drill - Static Guard Retention: Partner lies on their back, you practice Open Guard grips and leg frames without any movement from the partner. (20 reps, 10% resistance)
    4. Partner Drill - Controlled Posture Up: Partner attempts to posture up slightly, you practice pushing them away with your frames and re-establishing grips. (15 reps, 25% resistance)
    5. Partner Drill - Basic Sweep Attempt: Partner attempts a controlled pass, you execute a simple sweep attempt (e.g., scissor sweep). Partner offers minimal resistance. (10 reps per sweep type, 50% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling - Guard Retention Focus: Roll with a partner, focus on maintaining Open Guard and preventing them from passing for 30 seconds at a time, then reset. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent has established a strong posture, making it difficult to control them from Closed Guard.
    • Opponent is attempting to pass your guard and you want to create distance and angles to counter.
    • You are on your back and need to create space to initiate sweeps or submissions.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent attempts to stack pass: Use your left foot on their hip to push them away and your right foot on their arm to prevent them from collapsing your guard. Immediately transition to a sweep or a submission like a triangle choke.
    • Opponent tries to step around your legs: Extend your right leg and place your right foot on their left bicep to create a frame and push them back. Simultaneously, use your left foot on their hip to maintain distance and off-balance them.
    • Opponent tries to grab your ankles to break your guard: Maintain active pressure with your shins. If they get a grip, use your opposite hand to grip their wrist and pull it away while pushing with your leg to create space and reset.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do Open Guard?

    Wrist pain in Open Guard often stems from gripping too tightly on the opponent's collar or sleeve while simultaneously applying excessive, rigid force with your legs. This creates a torque on your wrist joints. To fix this, loosen your grip slightly, focusing on maintaining control rather than brute force. Use your legs to create the primary distance and leverage, allowing your hands to guide and secure grips more gently.

    Q: How do I use Open Guard effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, Open Guard relies heavily on precise framing and distance management. Use your shins and feet to create a strong, active barrier against their hips and thighs, preventing them from closing the distance and using their weight. Focus on off-balancing them by pushing and pulling with your legs and controlling their posture with your grips, looking for sweep opportunities rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: When is the best time to switch from Closed Guard to Open Guard?

    The optimal time to transition from Closed Guard to Open Guard is when your opponent is postured up and trying to break your guard by standing or driving forward aggressively. This is when your opponent's weight is most advantageous for you to create space and angles. By opening your guard, you can use your legs to push them away, control their distance, and set up sweeps or submissions that are difficult to achieve from a tightly closed guard.
