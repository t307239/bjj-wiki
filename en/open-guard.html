Open Guard Fundamentals for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Open Guard is a dynamic BJJ position where you break the Closed Guard and use your legs to control distance and create attack angles.

White belts often struggle due to a passive mindset and a lack of understanding of leverage, leading to them being easily passed.

The key insight is using your legs as levers to maintain frames and off-balance your opponent, preventing them from establishing solid pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard, break opponent's posture by gripping their collar with both hands, pulling them forward.
    2. Simultaneously, drive your hips slightly forward and up, creating a small gap between your knees.
    3. Insert one knee deep into the opponent's hip crease, while the other leg extends to create a frame.
    4. Grip opponent's sleeve or wrist with the hand on the same side as the extended leg, creating a strong forearm frame.
    5. With the other hand, grip the opponent's opposite collar or lapel, controlling their posture and upper body.
    6. Maintain a 'hip angle' by keeping your hips slightly turned away from the opponent, creating leverage.
    7. Distribute your weight by keeping your base wide and your core engaged, preventing them from collapsing your guard.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to 'hold on' by squeezing with your legs: This strains the adductors and hip flexors, leading to groin pulls. Instead, use your shins and feet to create frames and push against their hips.
    • Letting your knees collapse inwards: This exposes your inner thighs and knees to potential knee bars or sweeps, damaging the MCL. Keep your knees wide and knees pointing slightly outwards.
    • Extending your arms fully without framing: This puts your elbows and wrists at risk of hyperextension or armbars. Always maintain a slight bend in your elbows and use your forearms to push against their biceps or shoulders.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice opening your guard and establishing leg positions (knee-on-hip, extended leg frame) without a partner. 50 reps.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Partner stands or kneels, you practice opening guard and establishing grips and frames. 25% resistance, 5 reps each side.
    3. Static guard retention: Partner attempts to pass, you focus solely on maintaining your frames and hip angle. 50% resistance, 3 reps.
    4. Introduction to sweeps: Partner applies light pressure, you practice initiating simple sweeps from open guard. 75% resistance, 5 reps.
    5. Guard retention against basic passes: Partner attempts torreando or knee-slice passes with controlled movement. 90% resistance, 3 reps.
    6. Light rolling: Focus on maintaining open guard and attempting submissions or sweeps against a resisting opponent. 100% resistance, 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent has established a strong closed guard, and you need to create space for sweeps or submissions.
    • When opponent is posturing up in your guard, creating an opportunity to break their posture and establish leg control.
    • When opponent is attempting to pass your guard and you can intercept their hips with your legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Against a knee-slice pass: Maintain a strong frame with your extended leg against their hip, push them away, and bring your other knee to their hip to re-establish guard. Use your collar grip to pull them into your guard.
    • Against a torreando pass: Keep your hips mobile and use your extended leg to create distance. Grip their arm and pull them into a position where you can either transition to a sweep or re-establish guard.
    • Against a stack pass: Drive your hips forward and use your legs to push their shoulders away. Grip their collar and pull them forward to break their base, creating an opportunity for a sweep or submission.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to open guard and grip their sleeves?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you grip the sleeve and extend your arm fully without proper forearm framing. Instead of a straight arm grip, bend your elbow slightly and use your forearm to create a rigid frame against their bicep or shoulder. This transfers the pressure to your stronger forearm bones and muscles, protecting your wrist joint.

    Q: How do I use Open Guard effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on using your legs to create frames and off-balance their weight, rather than trying to muscle through. Keep your hips mobile and use your extended leg to maintain distance and control their base. Gripping their collar and one sleeve can help you dictate their posture and pull them into less advantageous positions for them.

    Q: When should I transition from Closed Guard to Open Guard?

    You should transition from Closed Guard to Open Guard when your opponent is posturing up heavily, making it difficult to maintain control or initiate sweeps. It's also beneficial when they are actively trying to break your guard, as opening allows you to control the distance and angles more effectively. The goal is to prevent them from settling into a dominant passing position.
