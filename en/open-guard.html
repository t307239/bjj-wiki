Open Guard: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Open Guard starts from a seated or supine position, using your legs to control distance and create attacking angles. It allows for sweeps and submissions when your opponent is postured up or trying to pass.

White belts often struggle with Open Guard due to a lack of body awareness and a tendency to grip too loosely or too tightly, leading to loss of control or injury.

The single most crucial mechanical insight is maintaining frames with your shins and forearms to prevent your opponent from collapsing your guard and establishing dominant pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a seated position with your hips slightly elevated off the mat, knees bent at approximately 90 degrees.
    2. Place the soles of your feet on your opponent's hips, maintaining a slight bend in your knees and ankles.
    3. Establish a "Spider Guard" grip: grip your opponent's right sleeve with your left hand and their left sleeve with your right hand. Keep your elbows tucked close to your body.
    4. Create a frame by extending your right shin across your opponent's left hip and your left shin across their right hip. Your heels should be pointed towards the ceiling.
    5. To sweep, shift your weight slightly to your left, extending your right leg to push their left hip away while simultaneously pulling their right sleeve with your left hand.
    6. As they fall, transition your right foot to their left hip and adjust your grip on their left sleeve with your right hand, preparing for a sweep or submission.
    7. Maintain tension in your legs and core to prevent your opponent from closing the distance or passing your guard. Your hips should always be mobile.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping sleeves too tightly without maintaining frames: This can lead to wrist hyperextension or sprains if the opponent resists forcefully, damaging the carpal bones or ligaments. Always maintain active shin frames to control distance.
    • Letting knees collapse inwards when opponent applies pressure: This allows the opponent to pass easily and can cause knee valgus, stressing the MCL and ACL. Keep your shins actively pushing outwards to create space.
    • Extending legs completely straight without control: This leaves your ankles vulnerable to attacks like heel hooks and can lead to ankle ligament damage if the opponent applies a sudden torque. Maintain a slight bend in your knees and ankles.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice assuming the Open Guard position, establishing grips, and extending/retracting legs for 5 minutes.
    2. Partner drill - static position (25% resistance): Partner stands in front, you establish Open Guard grips. Practice pushing and pulling with legs against minimal resistance for 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill - controlled movement (50% resistance): Partner takes a step forward. You practice framing and adjusting your guard. 5 reps per side.
    4. Sweep attempts (75% resistance): Partner attempts to maintain balance. You execute a sweep attempt. 5 reps per side.
    5. Guard retention drill (90% resistance): Partner attempts to pass. You focus on keeping your legs between you and them, re-establishing grips. 3 minutes.
    6. Live rolling (100% resistance): Implement Open Guard techniques during rolling, focusing on control and safe execution. 2 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up and trying to establish control over your legs.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard from a distance.
    • When you have successfully created space and have control of at least one of your opponent's sleeves.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Spider Guard": Grip opponent's sleeves, place soles of feet on hips, extend shins to create frames. Use this to control distance and set up sweeps or attacks.
    • "De La Riva Guard": Hook opponent's leg with one foot, maintain grip on sleeve with opposite hand. Use this to off-balance and attack the hooked leg or transition to other guards.
    • "Lasso Guard": Wrap one arm around opponent's neck and secure their opposite sleeve with your hand. This creates strong control and can lead to sweeps or submissions like the triangle choke.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I do Open Guard and try to grab their sleeves?

    Your wrist pain is likely due to hyperextension caused by grabbing too far up the sleeve or without maintaining a strong frame. Instead of just grabbing, use your grip to pull their elbow towards you while simultaneously extending your shin into their hip. This creates a biomechanical advantage, making the grip more effective and reducing strain on your wrist.

    Q: Against a bigger opponent, distance management is paramount. Utilize your legs like springs, constantly pushing their hips away to prevent them from smothering you. Focus on grips that control their posture, like sleeve grips in Spider Guard, and use your frames (shins on hips) to maintain that crucial space. Sweeps become about unbalancing their larger mass rather than brute force.

    Q: Is Open Guard only for attacking, or can I use it to defend?

    Open Guard is a powerful defensive tool. The frames created by your legs (shins on hips or opponent's legs) act as a barrier, preventing your opponent from collapsing your guard or driving forward. Maintaining these frames and actively controlling their posture allows you to reset, create space, and defend against passes while looking for opportunities to counter-attack.
