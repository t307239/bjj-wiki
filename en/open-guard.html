Open guard involves maintaining distance from your opponent using your legs to control their posture and base. It's a defensive position that creates offensive opportunities by hindering their ability to pass.

White belts often struggle with Open Guard by collapsing their structure, allowing easy passes, or becoming too rigid, losing the dynamic control needed.

The core principle is creating a strong, flexible frame with your legs and hips to dictate range and angle, preventing them from establishing forward pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a supine position, extend your legs, creating a base wider than your opponent's hips.
    2. Grip both of your opponent's sleeves with your hands, thumbs inside the fabric, palms facing up.
    3. Simultaneously, establish a cross-collar grip with your opposite hand, thumb inside the collar.
    4. Angle your hips slightly to one side, creating a lean that prevents them from stacking you directly.
    5. Keep your elbows tucked to your ribs, maintaining a tight frame against their chest or shoulders.
    6. Use your feet to push against their hips or thighs, controlling the distance and preventing them from closing the gap.
    7. As they pressure forward, shift your weight onto your hips and use your legs to create angles, aiming to sweep or transition to a submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Extending arms fully to push opponent's shoulders: This hyper-extends the elbow joint, risking an elbow hyperextension injury. Keep elbows bent and tucked to your ribs.
    • Allowing opponent to grip your ankles directly: This compromises your leg structure and allows them to break your guard easily, leading to a potential knee or ankle torque injury. Maintain active foot grips on their sleeves or pants.
    • Slouching your upper back and neck: This creates a weak posture and can lead to neck strain or disc compression injuries. Maintain a tall spine and engage your core.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice opening your guard, establishing sleeve and collar grips, and creating leg frames against an imaginary opponent (5 minutes, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice opening your guard, establishing grips, and maintaining frames for 10 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner drill (light pressure): Partner applies minimal forward pressure. Focus on maintaining frames and preventing them from closing the distance (5 minutes, 25% resistance).
    4. Guard retention drill: Partner attempts to pass. You focus solely on keeping your guard closed and preventing any penetration (5 minutes, 50% resistance).
    5. Sweep attempt drill: Partner maintains base. You practice initiating a sweep from Open Guard, focusing on hip movement and leg extension (5 minutes, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Focus on maintaining Open Guard, preventing passes, and looking for sweep opportunities (3 rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent attempts a double-leg takedown while you are on your back.
    • Opponent is postured up, creating space between your legs.
    • Opponent is attempting to pass your guard by walking around.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Torreando Pass: Opponent attempts to circle around your legs. Grip their far arm and sleeve, drive your hips to the side, and use your leg to push their hip away, creating an angle for a sweep.
    • Knee Slice Pass: Opponent drives their knee down your centerline. Extend your legs to create space, grip their knee, and use your hips to shrimp away, re-establishing your guard.
    • Stack Pass: Opponent attempts to drive their weight directly down on you. Grip their shoulders or collar, push them away with your legs, and use your hips to create an angle to escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to grip their sleeves in Open Guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping too tightly with a bent wrist, creating excessive pressure. Ensure your wrist is straight and neutral when establishing sleeve grips. Focus on gripping the fabric with your fingers and thumb, maintaining a strong forearm connection rather than just a finger grip. This distributes the force more evenly and protects your wrist joint.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, your primary goal is to use your legs to create and maintain distance, preventing them from collapsing your structure. Focus on strong frames with your shins against their hips and thighs. Use your foot grips on their sleeves to control their posture and break their forward momentum. Shrimp your hips away to create angles and off-balance them, making their weight less of a factor.

    Q: Transitioning to Open Guard is beneficial when your opponent is postured up, creating space between your legs, or when they are actively trying to break your Closed Guard by standing up. It's also a good option if you want to create more dynamic sweeping opportunities by controlling the distance and angles with your legs. Essentially, if your Closed Guard is becoming ineffective or is about to be broken, Open Guard is a logical next step.
