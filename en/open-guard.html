Open Guard begins from a seated or supine position, aiming to control distance and create angles to attack or sweep. It replaces the tight control of Closed Guard with active leg and arm entanglements.

White belts often struggle by passively letting their legs be limp or by over-extending, creating openings for passes. They also tend to grip too loosely, negating the control needed.

The core principle is creating strong frames with your legs and arms to manage your opponent's base and posture, using your hips to generate leverage for sweeps and submissions.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a seated or supine position with knees bent, feet flat on the mat if possible, or hips elevated.
    2. Establish grips: Sleeve grips on opponent's gi sleeves, or wrist control if no gi. Aim for grips at the elbow or forearm to prevent them from posturing up.
    3. Create a frame: Extend one leg, placing the sole of your foot on the opponent's hip bone, perpendicular to their torso. Keep your knee slightly bent, not locked out.
    4. Secure the second leg: Bring your second foot to the opponent's opposite hip or thigh, creating a V-shape with your legs. This leg can be used to control distance or to hook their leg.
    5. Hip angle: Rotate your hips slightly away from the opponent, creating an angle of approximately 45 degrees. This elongates your body and makes it harder for them to stack you.
    6. Weight distribution: Keep your weight centered over your hips. Avoid leaning too far back, which makes you vulnerable to being flattened.
    7. Active control: Continuously adjust your frames and grips to maintain distance and control. Use your hips to bridge or shrimp subtly to maintain your base and angles.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Hyperextended Knees: Gripping too tightly with straight legs and allowing the opponent to drive forward. This can tear the ACL or MCL by forcing the knee beyond its natural range of motion. Always maintain a slight bend in the knee when framing.
    • Wrist Sprains/Fractures: Gripping opponent's gi sleeves too tightly with a straight wrist and allowing them to suddenly pull their arm out, twisting your wrist. Maintain a neutral wrist, and if they pull, absorb the force by bending your elbow.
    • Lower Back Strain: Trying to shrimp or bridge with a rounded lower back while the opponent is posturing heavily. This can lead to muscle pulls or disc issues. Engage your core and maintain a posterior pelvic tilt when moving your hips.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Escapes and Framing: Practice shrimping and bridging with correct hip angles and leg frames. 10 reps each side, 0% resistance.
    2. Partnered Framing and Distance Control: Partner stands, you practice framing with legs on hips, keeping them at bay. 10 reps, 25% resistance.
    3. Gripping and Leg Frame Drills: Partner attempts to break your grips and pass. You focus on re-establishing grips and maintaining leg frames. 10 reps, 50% resistance.
    4. Basic Sweep Entry: Practice initiating a sweep (e.g., Hip Bump Sweep) from your open guard. Partner resists lightly. 5 reps each side, 75% resistance.
    5. Controlled Open Guard Sparring: Spar with a partner focusing only on maintaining open guard and attempting sweeps/submissions. No passing allowed for the opponent. 3 rounds x 3 minutes, 90% resistance.
    6. Live Rolling with Open Guard Focus: Incorporate open guard into your regular sparring, aiming to utilize it whenever applicable. 5 rounds x 5 minutes, 100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is in your guard, and you want to prevent them from establishing a strong, controlling guard pass like a knee slice or stack pass.
    • When you have just been swept or lost control of your guard, and need to re-establish a defensive position with offensive potential.
    • When your opponent is posturing up significantly, creating an opportunity to use your legs to disrupt their balance and attack.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Pass Defense: If opponent tries to stack you, widen your knee angle (like a butterfly guard setup) and bridge your hips into their chest, creating space to recover closed guard or move to an angle.
    • Guard Pass Attempt (e.g., Torreando): If opponent tries to circle around your legs, extend the leg on the side they are moving towards, using your foot on their hip to push them away and simultaneously turn your hips to face them.
    • Posture Control: If opponent tries to break your grips and posture up, use your leg frames to push their hips away, and simultaneously pull down on their sleeves to break their posture and bring them back into your guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to control my opponent's sleeves in Open Guard?

    Your wrist is likely hurting because you are gripping with an extended or flexed wrist, and the opponent's pull is forcing it into an unnatural position. To fix this, ensure your wrist stays neutral and your elbow is bent, absorbing the force. Imagine your forearm and hand forming a straight line, with the elbow acting as a hinge.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, your open guard is about creating distance and angles, not about overpowering them. Use your legs as strong frames to keep their weight off you. Focus on precise foot placement on their hips and use your hips to generate leverage for sweeps. Prioritize mobility and maintaining your base over static grips.

    Q: The ideal time to transition from Closed Guard to Open Guard is when your opponent is posturing up significantly, making it difficult to maintain a tight closed guard, or when they are attempting to break your guard. This transition allows you to regain control of distance and create opportunities for sweeps or submissions.
