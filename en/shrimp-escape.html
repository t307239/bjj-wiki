The Essential Shrimp Escape for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★☆☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Shrimp Escape is a fundamental BJJ movement used to create space when an opponent has established control, most commonly from guard or Side Control. It allows you to disengage and re-establish a safer position or attempt a sweep.

White belts often struggle by trying to push or pull with raw strength rather than using precise body mechanics. This leads to getting stuck, losing balance, or even injury, as they aren't generating efficient leverage.

The core principle of the Shrimp Escape is leveraging your hips and feet to create a controlled, sliding motion away from pressure. It's about hip dissociation and coordinated limb movement, not brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a position of disadvantage (e.g., opponent in closed guard), ensure your knees are tucked towards your chest.
    2. Place the sole of one foot (e.g., right foot) flat on the mat, with the knee bent at approximately 90 degrees, creating a stable base.
    3. Shift your weight slightly onto your planted foot and simultaneously lift your hips off the mat by extending your planted leg.
    4. With your hips elevated, slide your planted foot directly away from your opponent's center of mass, angling your pelvis perpendicular to their hips.
    5. As you slide, bring your other knee (e.g., left knee) towards your chest, creating space between your hips and your opponent's.
    6. Continue sliding your planted foot and bringing your free leg's knee forward until you have created sufficient distance to transition.
    7. Aim to create a gap of at least one arm's length or enough to re-establish your guard or move to a safer position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee hyperextension: Attempting to push off the opponent's hip with a straight leg can cause hyperextension of the knee joint, potentially tearing the ACL or PCL. The correct movement involves planting the foot flat and driving through the hip of the planted leg, keeping a slight bend.
    • Lower back strain: Arching the lower back excessively while trying to lift the hips can lead to muscle strains or disc compression. The hips should be lifted by engaging the glutes and core, not by arching the spine.
    • Ankle and foot injury: Planting the foot at an awkward angle or rolling the ankle while sliding can cause sprains or fractures. Ensure the sole of the foot is flat on the mat and the ankle is in a neutral position throughout the movement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Escape (No Opponent): Practice lifting hips and sliding foot away, focusing on the hip angle. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Hip Escape with Knee Tuck: Add the knee tuck motion to the previous drill. (50 reps)
    3. Partnered Static Shrimp Escape: Partner lies on their back, you are on top in side control. Practice shrimp escape to create space. Partner provides no resistance. (20 reps per side)
    4. Partnered Light Resistance Shrimp Escape: Partner in closed guard. You practice creating space to open guard. Partner offers light hip pressure. (25% resistance, 30 reps)
    5. Partnered Moderate Resistance Shrimp Escape: Partner in closed guard. You practice shrimp escape to get to half guard or better. Partner offers moderate hip pressure. (50% resistance, 40 reps)
    6. Live Rolling Shrimp Escape: In live rolling, consciously look for opportunities to shrimp escape from disadvantageous positions. (100% resistance, integrated into rolling time)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent has secured a strong closed guard and you cannot break their posture.
    • When you are stuck in side control and need to create space to escape to guard or knee-on-belly.
    • When an opponent is attempting a submission from guard and you need to disengage.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The Hip Bump Sweep: As you begin to shrimp, instead of sliding away, drive your hips upwards and to the side, using your planted foot as a pivot to sweep your opponent over.
    • The Knee-to-Elbow Escape: If the opponent is holding on tightly, instead of a full shrimp, bring your knee to your elbow on the same side, creating a tight frame to push their hip away and create a smaller, controlled space.
    • The Underhook and Bridge: If you can establish an underhook as you shrimp, use that arm to pull your opponent closer and simultaneously bridge your hips, disrupting their balance and creating an opportunity to escape or improve position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to Shrimp Escape from bottom of closed guard?

    Your back is likely hurting because you are arching your lower back excessively to lift your hips, rather than engaging your glutes and core muscles. This puts undue stress on your lumbar spine. To fix this, focus on squeezing your glutes and drawing your belly button towards your spine as you lift your hips. This will create a more stable and powerful hip extension, protecting your back.

    Q: How do I Shrimp Escape effectively against a much bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, brute force shrimp escapes are ineffective and can lead to injury. Focus on precision and leverage. Ensure your planted foot is flat and drive through that hip, angling your body to create a perpendicular line between your hips and theirs. The goal is to create a tiny bit of space to begin your escape, not to push them off entirely. Think of it as a controlled slide, not a push.

    Q: When is the best time to Shrimp Escape versus trying to pass guard?

    The Shrimp Escape is primarily a defensive or positional repositioning tool, not a guard pass. You would use it when you are stuck in a disadvantageous position (like being controlled in Closed Guard or side control) and need to create space to either re-establish your own guard, escape to a neutral position, or improve your control. Guard passing techniques are initiated when you have already achieved a dominant top position and are actively trying to advance past the opponent's legs.
