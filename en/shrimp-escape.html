The Shrimp Escape is a fundamental BJJ movement used to create space and escape unfavorable positions, most commonly from the bottom of Closed Guard or side control. It allows you to re-establish guard or move to a safer position.

White belts often struggle with the Shrimp Escape due to a lack of understanding of leverage and weight distribution. They tend to push with their arms or legs instead of using their hips and core effectively.

The key mechanical insight is to use your hips as a lever, pivoting on your shoulders and feet to slide your hips away from the pressure, creating a dynamic space to move into.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From bottom of closed guard, establish opponent's collar grip with your right hand and sleeve grip with your left hand. Opponent's weight is forward.
    2. Flex your knees, bringing your heels towards your glutes, creating a stable base with your feet flat on the mat. Your hips should be slightly elevated.
    3. Simultaneously, drive your left shoulder into the mat and push off with your right foot. Your hips will rotate to your left.
    4. As your hips move left, extend your right leg straight out, creating a wedge between you and the opponent's hip. Your left leg remains bent.
    5. Continue sliding your hips away from the opponent, using your right foot as a pivot and your left leg to maintain distance. Your torso should remain relatively upright.
    6. Once you have created sufficient space (approximately 1-2 feet), bring your left knee back in between you and the opponent to re-establish guard.
    7. If escaping side control, place your forearm on the mat for support and drive your hips away, pivoting on your shoulder and opposite foot.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly pushing with the arms: This can lead to elbow hyperextension, damaging the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) and posterior capsule. The correct movement is to drive hips away, using arms minimally for framing.
    • Lifting the hips too high and arching the back: This strains the lumbar spine and can lead to disc compression or facet joint injury. The correct movement involves a controlled hip pivot, keeping the spine relatively neutral.
    • Not creating enough space before moving legs: This can result in your knees getting trapped or your opponent maintaining superior control, risking knee joint hyperextension or ligament damage. The correct movement emphasizes creating space with the hips first, then repositioning the legs.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Movement: Practice the hip pivot and extension motion on your own, focusing on the feeling of sliding your hips away. 50 reps.
    2. Assisted Hip Movement: Have a partner lie on top of you in a static position (e.g., closed guard). Practice the shrimp motion, focusing on creating space with your hips against their static weight. 25% resistance. 10 reps per side.
    3. Guard Re-establishment Drill: From closed guard, practice the shrimp escape and immediately transitioning back to closed guard. Focus on smooth transitions. 50% resistance. 15 reps.
    4. Side Control Escape Drill: Have a partner practice a loose side control position. Perform the shrimp escape to create space and attempt to get back to guard. 75% resistance. 10 reps per side.
    5. Flow Rolling (Controlled): Engage in light, controlled rolling, focusing on using the shrimp escape whenever you feel your guard is being passed or you are in a compromised position. 90% resistance. 5 minutes.
    6. Live Rolling: Apply the shrimp escape during live rolling as needed, focusing on timing and execution. 100% resistance. 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent has established a strong closed guard and is beginning to break your posture.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard from side control and you feel them moving to your hip.
    • When you are stuck in a bad position and need to create immediate space to reset.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent attempts to follow your hips: If your opponent tries to step over your leg as you shrimp, maintain your frame and use your bent leg to push their hip away, then continue the shrimp.
    • Opponent tries to flatten you out: As you shrimp, if your opponent tries to flatten you, use your free arm to frame against their shoulder or head, preventing them from collapsing your posture. Then continue your escape.
    • Opponent moves to a knee-slice pass: If your opponent transitions to a knee-slice, use the space created by your shrimp to bring your knee inside and establish half-guard or re-guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Shrimp Escape?

    Your lower back pain likely stems from over-arching your spine to create space. Instead of lifting your hips excessively, focus on a controlled hip pivot, using your shoulders and feet as points of leverage. Imagine sliding your hips sideways like a crab, rather than trying to lift your entire torso off the mat.

    Q: How do I Shrimp Escape against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maximizing the leverage from your hips and using your feet as anchors. Ensure your heels are close to your glutes, creating a strong base. Drive your hips away with force, using your arms for framing only when necessary to prevent them from collapsing your posture. The goal is to create a small, dynamic window of space to move into.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Shrimp Escape in BJJ?

    The Shrimp Escape is most effective when your opponent has established a dominant top position and is actively pressuring you, threatening a Guard Pass or submission. It's ideal when you feel them closing the distance and their weight is on top of your hips, making it difficult to move. Use it to disrupt their structure and create the space needed to regain guard or improve your position.
