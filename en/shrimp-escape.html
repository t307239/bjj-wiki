Shrimp Escape: The Essential BJJ Technique for White Belts

WHITE★☆☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Shrimp Escape begins when your opponent has established a dominant position, most commonly Closed Guard or side control, and aims to create space to re-establish guard or escape. White belts often struggle because they try to push away with their legs instead of using their hips and core. The key mechanical insight is to use your hips as a lever, pivoting on your feet and shoulders to 'swim' your hips away.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, ensure your opponent's hips are close to yours, ideally with their chest on your chest. Your knees should be tight to your chest.
    2. Place the foot on the side of your opponent's hip (e.g., right foot if they are on your right) flat on the mat, with the knee pointing towards the ceiling.
    3. Place the opposite foot (e.g., left foot) flat on the mat, with the knee pointing towards your opponent's hip. This creates your base.
    4. Simultaneously, place your hands on your opponent's hips or biceps, creating a frame to prevent them from collapsing your posture.
    5. Initiate the movement by driving your heel of the grounded foot (e.g., right heel) into the mat, while simultaneously lifting your hips off the mat slightly.
    6. Execute a powerful hip extension by pushing your hips away from your opponent, pivoting on your grounded foot and the opposite shoulder. Imagine 'swimming' your hips sideways.
    7. As your hips clear your opponent's hips, extend your legs and create distance, returning to a guard position or standing up.
    8. Maintain your frames with your hands throughout the movement to prevent re-engagement by your opponent.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to push your opponent away with straight legs: This places excessive stress on the knee joint, potentially leading to ACL or MCL tears as the knee buckles under direct anterior-posterior or medial-lateral force. The correct movement is hip extension, not leg pushing.
    • Arching your back excessively to create space: This can lead to lumbar spine hyperextension injuries and disc compression. The movement should originate from the hips, not the spine. The correct action is to drive the hips away with core engagement.
    • Keeping your feet too close to your body during the shrimp: This limits the range of motion and leverage, often resulting in the opponent maintaining control. It can also lead to ankle hyperextension if you try to push off with insufficient base. Ensure your feet are flat on the mat with knees bent to create a stable pivot point.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Escape (No Partner): Practice the hip movement without resistance. Focus on the foot placement, hip extension, and pivoting action. 50 reps.
    2. Partner Assisted Hip Escape (0% Resistance): Have a partner lie on their back and lightly hold your hips. Practice the shrimp escape, focusing on creating space. 5 sets of 10 reps.
    3. Partner Assisted Hip Escape (25% Resistance): Your partner provides light pressure, attempting to maintain their position. Focus on driving your hips away effectively. 5 sets of 10 reps.
    4. Guard Retention Drill (50% Resistance): From closed guard, your partner attempts to posture up. Use the shrimp escape to create space and re-close your guard. 5 sets of 10 reps.
    5. Side Control Escape Drill (75% Resistance): Your partner is in side control. Practice the shrimp escape to create space to get your knees inside and establish guard. 5 sets of 10 reps.
    6. Live Rolling (90% Resistance): Apply the shrimp escape during live rolling when you find yourself in a compromising position like being stuck in closed guard or losing side control.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When trapped in your opponent's closed guard with their hips too close.
    • When your opponent has established side control and you cannot create space with other methods.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard and you need to create distance to re-establish.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Against Pressure Passing: If your opponent is 'smashing' you down, use the shrimp to create space and bring your knees back between you. The pivot on your shoulder and foot prevents them from collapsing your base.
    • Against Posture Control: If your opponent is controlling your posture and preventing you from attacking, the shrimp allows you to break their connection and create an opportunity to attack their arms or re-establish guard.
    • To Escape Pins: When stuck in side control or mount and unable to bridge or buck effectively, the shrimp is a fundamental escape to create space and begin the process of regaining guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Shrimp Escape?

    Your lower back pain is likely due to over-reliance on spinal extension rather than hip articulation. When you arch your back excessively, you put undue stress on your lumbar spine. Focus on engaging your glutes and core to drive your hips away, using your feet and shoulders as pivots, rather than just bending backward. This ensures the power comes from the correct muscle groups.

    Q: How do I Shrimp Escape against a much bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paraMount. Ensure your foot placement is precise: one foot flat on the mat with the knee up, the other foot also flat with the knee pointing towards their hip. Use your hands to create a strong frame on their hips or biceps. Initiate the movement with a powerful hip extension, visualizing 'swimming' your hips away. Don't try to push their weight; instead, use their weight against them by creating a pivot point and moving your hips out of the line of force.

    Q: When should I shrimp versus just trying to push my opponent off with my legs?

    You should always prioritize the shrimp escape over simply pushing with your legs. Pushing with straight legs often fails to create meaningful space and can lead to injury. The shrimp is a biomechanically sound movement that uses your hips as a lever to generate significant distance. It's effective from various positions, including being stuck in guard or Side Control, and is the foundation for creating space to re-establish your guard or escape pins.
