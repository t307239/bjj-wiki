Knee Slice Pass: Your First Guard Pass in BJJ

This pass starts from a standing or kneeling position against an opponent's guard, aiming to establish Side Control. It's a fundamental control-based pass that bypasses the legs.

White belts often fail because they rush the hip movement or rely on upper body strength, neglecting the core mechanics. This leads to them getting stuck or swept.

The key insight is using your knee as a wedge to break the opponent's hip structure, allowing your own hip to drive through and establish dominant control.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from a closed guard, establish strong grips: either two collar grips or one collar and one sleeve grip. Your knees are inside the opponent's thighs, hips slightly elevated.
    2. Drive your hips forward and slightly down, creating a tight base with your knees on the mat, just outside the opponent's hips.
    3. Select one leg (e.g., your right leg) and begin to drive its knee forward, aiming to place it on the mat directly beside the opponent's hip bone.
    4. Simultaneously, begin to swing your hips forward and to the side, pivoting on your left knee which remains planted.
    5. As your right knee makes contact, use it to 'slice' through the opponent's hip joint, creating pressure and breaking their posture.
    6. Drive your right shoulder into the opponent's chest or shoulder, maintaining downward pressure.
    7. Continue the hip pivot and forward drive, bringing your left leg forward to match your right, establishing a knee-on-belly or side control position.
    8. Release collar grips if needed and secure an arm-and-head control (e.g., cross-face) to prevent them from reguarding.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly driving your knee directly onto the opponent's knee or shin can hyperextend their knee joint, potentially tearing the ACL or MCL. Always aim for the hip bone.
    • Rushing the hip rotation and letting your base widen too much can lead to your own knee or ankle being twisted outwards, causing ligament damage. Keep your supporting knee firmly planted and your hips driving forward.
    • Neglecting to maintain chest-to-chest pressure and instead pushing with your hands can allow the opponent to shrimp or bridge effectively, leading to a loss of position and potential shoulder or elbow strain from awkward escapes.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip rotation and knee slice motion without a partner, focusing on the smooth transition from guard entry to the side control base. 50 reps.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice the entire sequence from guard entry to side control, focusing on correct hip angle and knee placement. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice the hip break and forward drive. Focus on breaking their posture. 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance): Partner actively tries to maintain guard and create frames. Focus on maintaining pressure and hip drive. 3 reps per side.
    5. Flow drill: Integrate the Knee Slice Pass into a series of guard passes with a partner who is allowed to defend but not actively attack. Focus on smooth transitions. 5 minutes.
    6. Live rolling (90% intensity): Attempt the Knee Slice Pass against a resisting opponent, focusing on correct biomechanics and not getting discouraged by failures. 2 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent has their knees too close together, making it difficult to pass between their legs.
    • When opponent attempts a weak closed guard sweep or submission, leaving an opening.
    • When opponent is in half-guard and their top leg is not effectively controlling your hip.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Pummel: If opponent tries to bring their free leg over your shoulder to establish an X-guard or sweep, pummel your leg back and re-establish a solid base.
    • Hip Escape and Re-guard: If you fail to break the hip structure, the opponent will often try to shrimp their hips back. Escape your hips and immediately try to re-establish guard or move to a different pass.
    • Framing and Bridging: A well-timed frame with the forearm against your chest and a bridge can disrupt your balance and create space to recover guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Knee Slice Pass?

    Your knee might hurt because you're applying pressure directly onto the bone or joint instead of the fleshy part of the opponent's hip. Ensure your knee is slicing next to their hip bone and that your supporting leg is stable with your shin vertical, not at an acute angle. This protects your own knee and transfers force efficiently.

    Q: How do I Knee Slice Pass a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus heavily on creating a deep wedge with your slicing knee and driving your chest into their chest. Use your shoulder to control their head and prevent them from posturing up. Your hip drive is crucial; think of it as a ram, not a push, using your body weight to compress their hips.

    Q: When should I stop trying the Knee Slice Pass and switch?

    You should switch if the opponent successfully frames your face or shoulder, preventing you from driving forward, or if they manage to bring their bottom leg across your body to trap your leg. Continuing without adjustment will likely lead to you getting stuck or swept, so be ready to transition to a Leg Drag or a double-under pass.
