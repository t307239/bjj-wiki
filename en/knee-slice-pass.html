Knee Slice Pass: A Biomechanically Precise BJJ Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Knee Slice Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a Closed Guard or half guard. Its objective is to break the opponent's guard and establish side control.

White belts often fail by attempting to force the pass with raw strength, neglecting proper body mechanics and weight distribution.

The key mechanical insight is using your shin as a wedge to displace their hips, while your chest pressure drives through their core to create space.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a deep cross-collar grip with your right hand, palm facing away from you, and a far-side pant grip (e.g., outside of their left knee) with your left hand. Maintain a slightly upright posture, knees bent, feet hip-width apart.
    2. Drive your right knee forward, aiming to place it just outside and slightly in front of the opponent's left hip bone. Your left foot remains planted, providing a stable base.
    3. As your right knee makes contact, begin to drive your chest forward and down, applying pressure directly onto their hip and thigh. Simultaneously, angle your hips to the left, creating a perpendicular line between your torso and their legs.
    4. Initiate a subtle hip rotation, driving your right hip forward and slightly down, while your left hip pivots backward. This rotation helps to 'slice' your knee through their guard.
    5. Use your collar grip to pull their upper body towards you, keeping them tight. Your pant grip helps to control their leg and prevent them from reguarding.
    6. Continue driving your right knee forward and across their body, creating a wedge that lifts and displaces their left hip. Your weight should be distributed over your left foot and your right knee.
    7. As their left hip is displaced, your right leg will 'slice' past their guard. Immediately secure a cross-face grip with your right arm and drive your right shoulder into their neck/chin area.
    8. Drive your hips low and forward, settling into side control. Your left foot will move to establish a stable base, and your right knee will typically be placed on the mat for stability. Maintain chest-to-chest pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect knee placement: Driving your knee directly into the opponent's shin bone instead of their hip. This can cause hyperextension of your knee or a sprain of your medial collateral ligament (MCL) as your knee buckles outward.
    • Letting your hips rise too high: When your hips are elevated, you lose the ability to drive forward effectively, and your knee can easily get trapped. This often leads to your own knee being vulnerable to a knee bar if the opponent rotates.
    • Relying on arm strength: Pulling excessively on the collar grip without driving your body weight forward. This strains your shoulder joint, leading to rotator cuff impingement or tears, and fails to displace the opponent's hips.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip rotation and knee-driving motion on an empty mat. Focus on the feeling of your shin 'slicing' past an imaginary hip. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner lies on their back with legs extended. You practice the grip setup and the entire knee slice motion, focusing on driving your knee past their hip and establishing side control. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and the movement. Focus on maintaining your base and driving through. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance - 50%): Your partner actively tries to keep their guard closed or prevent the pass, but without full commitment. Focus on breaking their posture and displacing their hips. (5 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Your partner actively defends, attempting to re-guard or create frames. You must work to maintain pressure and adjust your angles. (3 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Attempt the Knee Slice Pass during sparring, focusing on applying the learned mechanics. Do not get discouraged if it doesn't work every time; the goal is to integrate it. (Duration of rolling rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent attempts to close their guard and their hips are relatively flat on the mat.
    • When the opponent is in half guard and their knee is trapped, but their hips are not yet fully squared.
    • When you have successfully broken the opponent's posture and they are leaning forward slightly.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Reguarding: As your knee slices, the opponent can bring their free leg back over your hip to re-establish guard. To counter, maintain tight chest pressure and use your free hand to block their hip or knee from coming over.
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): If you apply the pass too slowly, the opponent can shrimp their hips away to create space. To counter, maintain constant forward pressure with your chest and hips, and keep your knee close to their body.
    • Knee Shield/Frame: The opponent can use their free leg to create a frame against your chest or shoulder. To counter, use your collar grip to pull their head down and your cross-face grip to control their head, breaking down their frame.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Knee Slice Pass?

    This often happens when you're driving your knee directly into the opponent's shin or ankle bone, or when your knee is forced outwards due to poor hip positioning. Ensure your knee aims for the side of their hip and that your hips are angled perpendicularly to their legs, allowing your knee to slice past their hip bone, not their lower leg. Keep your supporting leg's knee bent and your weight balanced.

    Q: How do I Knee Slice Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing become even more critical. Instead of trying to force their hips open with pure pressure, focus on using your grips to subtly off-balance them and create a small opening. Use your collar grip to pull their head down and their upper body towards you, which will often cause their hips to shift. Then, drive your knee through that created space, rather than trying to break their guard directly.

    Q: When should I transition from a failed Knee Slice Pass to another technique?

    If your Knee Slice Pass is being consistently defended, look for opportunities based on their reaction. If they are shrimping away, you might be able to transition to a Leg Drag pass by securing their ankle. If they are framing strongly, you might be able to switch to a stack pass or an x-pass by adjusting your angle and grip. Always be ready to adapt based on their defensive mechanics.
