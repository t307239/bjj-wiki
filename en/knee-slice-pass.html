The Knee Slice Pass is a fundamental guard pass initiated from a top-side position, aiming to bypass the opponent's legs and secure Side Control.

White belts often struggle due to relying on brute force rather than leverage, leading to stalled passes and vulnerability.

The key is to use your knee as a wedge, driving through their hip line while maintaining chest-to-chest pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand (if passing on their right side) and a cross-sleeve grip with your left hand.
    2. Base: Establish a wide, stable base with your feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent.
    3. Hip Angle: Drive your left hip towards their right hip, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and their legs.
    4. Knee Placement: Slide your right knee along the mat, aiming to place it directly on their hip crease or slightly above.
    5. Pressure: Simultaneously, drive your chest into their chest, maintaining a tight connection.
    6. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight forward onto your right knee and left foot, lifting your hips slightly off the mat.
    7. Slice Motion: Drive your right knee forward and under their legs, 'slicing' through their guard.
    8. Completion: Once your knee is past their hip, step your left foot forward and secure side control by pinning their far arm and hip.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Error: Trying to force the pass by lifting your hips too high and swinging your leg over.

    Risk: Your knee joint (ACL/MCL) can be hyperextended or torn as their legs can easily trap and twist it.

    Correction: Keep your hips low and drive your knee *through* their hip line, not over it.

    • Error: Letting your opponent bridge or shrimp effectively by not maintaining chest-to-chest pressure.

    Risk: Your lumbar spine can be excessively flexed or extended, leading to muscle strains or disc issues.

    Correction: Maintain constant, firm chest-to-chest contact and use your frames to control their hips.

    • Error: Crossing your legs in your base while attempting the pass, creating a narrow platform.

    Risk: You can easily lose balance and fall to the side, potentially twisting your ankle or knee.

    Correction: Maintain a wide, stable base with your feet parallel and shoulder-width apart.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Footwork and Knee Placement: Practice the hip angle, base, and knee-driving motion without a partner. (10 reps per side)
    2. Controlled Partner Entry (0% Resistance): Partner lies flat on their back, you practice the grips, hip angle, and knee placement, gently sliding your knee through. (10 reps per side)
    3. Controlled Partner Entry (25% Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and hip movement. (10 reps per side)
    4. Half-Guard Simulation (50% Resistance): Partner establishes half-guard, you practice the knee slice entry and initial drive. (10 reps per side)
    5. Full Guard Entry (75% Resistance): Partner attempts to defend with a full guard, you focus on maintaining pressure and slicing through. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling (90-100% Resistance): Integrate the Knee Slice Pass into sparring, focusing on correct execution and adaptation. (3 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is flat on their back with their knees close together.
    • When they attempt to close their guard but leave a small gap between their knees.
    • When you have successfully established side control but they are trying to recover guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shrimping and Re-guarding: If the passer's hip pressure is weak, the defender can shrimp their hips away and bring their knee back to create a half-guard or full guard.
    • Knee Shield/Posting: The defender can place their shin across the passer's chest or hip to create space and prevent the knee from slicing through.
    • Stacking: If the passer commits too heavily and brings their weight too far forward, the defender can attempt to 'stack' them by bringing their hips over the passer's shoulders and standing up.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Knee Slice Pass?

    Knee pain often stems from improper knee placement and excessive outward rotation. If you're trying to force your knee over their leg instead of driving it *through* their hip line, you're putting shearing forces on your knee joint. Ensure your knee slides along the mat, aiming for their hip crease, and maintain a strong chest-to-chest connection to control their base.

    Q: How do I Knee Slice Pass against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on superior leverage and timing rather than pure strength. Use your grips to control their posture and break their base before initiating the pass. Drive your chest into their chest to limit their movement, and use your hip angle to create the opening for your knee to slice through. Don't try to power through; be precise with your pressure and hip movement.

    Q: When should I use the Knee Slice Pass versus a Torreando Pass?

    The Knee Slice Pass is generally more effective against opponents who keep their knees relatively close together or are trying to close their guard. It relies on driving *through* their legs. The Torreando Pass, on the other hand, is better when your opponent's knees are spread wider, allowing you to circle around their legs. Choose the pass that best exploits the current positioning of your opponent's legs.
