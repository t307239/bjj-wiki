This pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a guard player, aiming to establish Side Control.

White belts often struggle by allowing the opponent to maintain hip connection and frame effectively.

The key mechanical insight is using your knee as a wedge to displace their hips, creating a clear path for your body.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a cross-sleeve grip with your left hand (if opponent is on their back).
    2. Step your right foot forward, placing it outside their left hip, knee bent at 90 degrees, shin perpendicular to the mat.
    3. Drive your right knee down and slightly forward, aiming to make contact with the mat just outside their hip bone.
    4. Simultaneously, drive your chest down towards their chest, creating a "smash" pressure.
    5. Pivot your left foot back, bringing your left hip closer to their right hip, creating a tight base.
    6. Slice your right knee across their hips, forcing their legs to separate and their hips to turn away from you.
    7. Maintain head control by pressing your forehead into their cheek or forehead, preventing them from turning back in.
    8. Drive your weight forward and down, clearing their hips and settling into side control, securing an underhook with your right arm.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect knee placement: Driving your knee directly onto the opponent's hip bone can cause acute patellar tendonitis or tibial plateau fractures. The correct movement is to drive the knee towards the mat beside their hip.
    • Over-extending the spine: Arching your back excessively to create pressure strains the lumbar spine and can lead to disc herniation. Maintain a neutral spine and use hip drive and chest pressure.
    • Neglecting hip connection: Allowing your hips to stay too far away from the opponent's hips enables them to shrimp and re-establish guard. Keep your hips tight and drive them forward.
    • Releasing grips too early: Dropping your collar or sleeve grips allows the opponent to create frames and recover guard. Maintain strong grips until side control is established.
    • Insufficient hip turn: Not pivoting your hips and turning your body to face the opponent's hip allows them to maintain their guard structure. Drive your hips forward and turn your torso.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the footwork, knee placement, and hip pivot motion without a partner. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (static): Opponent lies on their back, you practice the pass mechanics, focusing on grip and knee placement. (25 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Opponent offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the hip displacement. (25 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance): Opponent actively tries to frame and prevent the pass, but not aggressively. (25 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the pass against a resisting opponent in a controlled sparring session. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full intensity): Execute the pass in live rolling with full resistance. (10 rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent has their knees tucked towards their chest in a closed guard.
    • When the opponent is attempting to transition from open guard to a more defensive position.
    • When you have established a strong grip on their pants or collar, controlling their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): The opponent shrimpes their hips away as your knee comes across, creating space to re-establish guard. The counter is to maintain chest pressure and drive your hips forward.
    • Knee Shield: The opponent places their knee between your chest and hip to create distance. The counter is to "smash" that knee down with your shoulder and drive your slicing knee further across.
    • Underhook Recovery: The opponent attempts to secure an underhook as you pass. The counter is to fight for the underhook yourself or immediately secure a cross-face.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Knee Slice Pass?

    This is likely due to improper knee placement. You are probably driving your knee directly onto the opponent's hip bone, which can lead to patellar tendonitis or a tibial plateau contusion. Instead, aim to drive your knee down towards the mat just outside their hip bone, using it as a pivot point and wedge, not a direct impact point.

    Q: How do I Knee Slice Pass against a bigger opponent who has strong grips?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and pressure are key. Focus on breaking their posture first with strong collar and sleeve grips, pulling them towards you and slightly off-balance. Then, use your hips to drive into them as you execute the knee slice, ensuring your weight is distributed forward and down to negate their size advantage.

    Q: When should I abandon the Knee Slice Pass and switch to another pass?

    You should consider switching passes if the opponent successfully creates a strong knee shield that you cannot smash through, or if they consistently shrimp out and re-establish guard effectively. If they manage to secure an underhook and turn into you, it's also a signal to disengage and reset or transition to a different pass like a Leg Drag.
