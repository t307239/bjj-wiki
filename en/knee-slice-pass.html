Knee Slice Pass: The White Belt's Biomechanically Accurate Guide

This pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a guard player, aiming to establish Side Control. Its goal is to break down the opponent's legs and isolate their hips.

White belts often struggle because they lack the precise hip pressure and body alignment needed to prevent the opponent from re-guarding or creating space.

The key mechanical insight is driving your knee *through* the opponent's hip line, not just *over* it, creating a "slice" that breaks their base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand (palm facing up) and a same-side sleeve grip with your left hand. Your opponent is in closed guard.
    2. Hip Angle: Lower your hips towards the mat, aligning your pelvis perpendicular to your opponent's hips. Your right hip should be directly in front of their left hip.
    3. Knee Placement: Drive your right knee forward, aiming to place it directly on the mat *inside* the opponent's left thigh, creating a wedge.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift 70% of your body weight onto your lead knee and hip, pressing down and forward.
    5. Head Position: Keep your head up and slightly to your left, creating a strong base and preventing your head from being trapped.
    6. Leg Action: Extend your left leg back, keeping your foot on the mat for base, while simultaneously driving your right knee forward and across.
    7. Pressure: Continue to drive your right knee and hip into the opponent's hip socket, forcing their legs open and isolating their upper body.
    8. Completion: Once the opponent's legs are separated, transition to side control by bringing your left leg to a stable base and securing your opponent's far arm and head.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee hyperextension: Driving the knee directly down with excessive force without proper hip alignment can lead to hyperextension of the knee joint, potentially tearing the ACL or PCL.
    • Ankle sprain: Planting the plant foot at an awkward angle while trying to drive forward can cause the ankle to roll inwards or outwards, leading to a ligamentous sprain.
    • Lower back strain: Rounding the back and attempting to force the pass with upper body strength alone, rather than using hip pressure and core engagement, can result in lumbar disc compression or muscle strains.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Mobility Drill: Perform 20 hip circles in each direction, focusing on driving the knee across the body. (0% resistance)
    2. Shadow Pass Drill: Practice the full Knee Slice Pass motion against an imaginary opponent, focusing on precise hip angles and knee placement. 10 reps per side. (0% resistance)
    3. Partnered Static Pass: With a partner lying on their back, practice driving your knee across their hip line and establishing side control without any active guard retention. 10 reps per side. (25% resistance)
    4. Partnered Guard Retention Drill: Your partner attempts to keep closed guard. You focus on executing the initial steps of the Knee Slice Pass (hip angle, knee drive). They can offer light resistance to prevent the pass. 10 reps per side. (50% resistance)
    5. Controlled Pass with Light Guard Retention: Your partner actively tries to retain guard. You execute the Knee Slice Pass with controlled pressure, aiming to break their guard and achieve side control. They can attempt basic sweeps. 5 reps per side. (75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling Application: Integrate the Knee Slice Pass into sparring rounds, attempting it when the opportunity arises. Focus on technique over winning. 3 x 3-minute rounds. (90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is in closed guard and you have established a strong cross-collar and sleeve grip, breaking their posture.
    • Opponent has opened their guard and is attempting to establish a spider or lasso guard, creating space you can exploit.
    • Opponent is in half-guard and you have secured a strong cross-face and hip control, preventing them from establishing a strong base.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Reguarding with the far leg: As you drive your knee across, the opponent can bring their far leg (the one not being sliced) up and over your hip to re-establish guard. To counter, ensure your slicing knee drives *through* their hip line, not just over it, and maintain tight head and shoulder pressure to prevent them from creating space.
    • Hip Escape and Re-guard: The opponent can use a hip escape to create space and bring their legs back into a guard position. To counter, maintain constant downward pressure with your slicing knee and hip, keeping your base wide and your head low to prevent them from generating momentum for the escape.
    • Stacking: The opponent can try to stack your weight by bridging and pushing your upper body towards your head. To counter, ensure your base is wide and stable, and drive your hips forward and down, rather than leaning back. Keep your head up and your core engaged to maintain your structure.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Knee Slice Pass?

    Your knee might hurt due to improper alignment. Instead of driving your knee directly down, you should be driving it *across* the opponent's hip, using it as a wedge to break their guard. Ensure your hip is angled perpendicular to theirs, and your weight is distributed over your hip and thigh, not solely on the knee joint itself. This biomechanical shift protects your knee and leverages your hip for pressure.

    Q: How do I Knee Slice Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage your hips and core more effectively. Focus on getting your hip *inside* their hip line and driving your slicing knee with the power generated from your glutes and hamstrings, not just your quadriceps. Maintain tight head and shoulder pressure, almost like a 'smash pass,' to keep them from using their size to create space. Don't try to muscle through; use precise angles and consistent pressure.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Knee Slice Pass from closed guard?

    The optimal time to attempt a Knee Slice Pass from Closed Guard is *after* you have successfully broken the opponent's posture and established a dominant grip (e.g., cross-collar and sleeve). Look for the moment their hips are slightly off the mat or when they attempt to open their guard. Initiating the pass prematurely or with poor posture will allow them to easily re-establish their guard or initiate a sweep.
