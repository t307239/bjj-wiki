The De La Riva Guard is an open guard position designed to control distance and off-balance an opponent, leading to sweeps or submissions. It starts from a seated or supine position with one leg hooked behind the opponent's leg.

White belts often struggle because they lack body awareness, leading to an unstable base and easily broken grips. They try to muscle positions rather than using leverage.

The key insight is using your hooked leg as a lever and your free leg to create frames, constantly adjusting your hip angle to maintain balance and control.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or supine position, secure a standard collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on the opponent's left sleeve with your left hand.
    2. Place the sole of your right foot on the opponent's left hip crease, keeping your knee slightly bent and your hip angled towards your right.
    3. Hook your left foot behind the opponent's left knee pit, ensuring your shin is perpendicular to their thigh and your ankle is flexed.
    4. Drive your right hip into the opponent's left thigh, creating a wedge and preventing them from easily passing.
    5. Use your left foot (hooked leg) to apply inward pressure, subtly drawing the opponent's weight onto their left leg.
    6. Simultaneously, extend your right leg slightly, pushing off their hip to create space and maintain your guard.
    7. Adjust your hip angle by shifting your weight to your left side, creating an opening for a sweep or transition.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly hooking the opponent's leg too high (around the thigh) can lead to your own knee hyperextension if they bridge or drive forward, damaging the ACL. Always aim for the knee pit.
    • Gripping the opponent's pants too tightly without controlling their sleeve or collar allows them to break your grip and pass easily, potentially leading to wrist sprains. Maintain sleeve and collar control.
    • Allowing your hooked leg to become too straight or limp makes you vulnerable to their passing and can strain your ankle and calf muscles. Keep your ankle flexed and shin actively engaged.
    • Failing to post your free leg (the one not hooking) on their hip creates a weak base, allowing them to step over and pass. Maintain a strong frame with your posting leg.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg hook and hip placement with a partner holding still (0% resistance). Focus on grip placement and hip angle. 10 reps each side.
    2. Partnered drilling (no resistance): Execute the full De La Riva setup, focusing on weight transfer and frame creation. Partner offers no resistance. 5 reps each side.
    3. Light resistance drilling: Partner attempts to step out or posture up slightly. You focus on maintaining the hook and frame. 5 reps each side, 25% resistance.
    4. Sweep attempt drilling: Partner maintains a stable base. You attempt a sweep from De La Riva (e.g., basic scissor sweep setup). Partner offers minimal defense. 5 reps each side, 50% resistance.
    5. Controlled rolling: Roll with a partner who understands De La Riva and tries to pass your guard. You focus on maintaining the guard and attempting sweeps. 3 minutes, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Execute De La Riva Guard in a live sparring session, working on maintaining position, sweeping, and transitioning. 5 minutes, 100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is posturing up and trying to apply pressure from a standing or kneeling position.
    • When the opponent has a wide base and is trying to pass your guard by walking around.
    • When you are looking to create dynamic movement and off-balance your opponent for a sweep.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Standing base adjustment: If the opponent tries to step their outside leg across, quickly bring your inside leg (the one not hooking) to their hip to create a frame and push them away.
    • Grip break and re-engagement: If they break your sleeve grip, immediately transition to controlling their wrist or elbow to prevent them from straightening their arm and passing.
    • Leg re-hook: If they pull their leg out of your hook, swiftly re-establish the hook on the same leg or switch to hooking their other leg, maintaining hip connection.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do De La Riva Guard?

    Your ankle pain likely stems from a lack of ankle flexion and a straight leg when hooking. The De La Riva hook requires your ankle to be flexed, with your shin actively pressing into the opponent's knee pit. A straight, limp leg puts excessive strain on your ligaments and tendons. Focus on actively flexing your ankle and engaging your calf muscle to create a strong, dynamic hook rather than passively draping your leg.

    Q: How do I use De La Riva Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary goal is to use the De La Riva to disrupt their base and prevent them from using their weight effectively. Focus on the hip angle: drive your right hip into their thigh to create a wedge and prevent them from squaring up. Use your hooked leg to continuously pull their weight onto their supporting leg, creating an imbalance. Your free leg should actively frame their hips or knees to maintain distance and control.

    Q: When should I switch from De La Riva Guard to another guard?

    You should consider switching guards when the opponent successfully stabilizes their base, making sweeps difficult, or when they manage to pass your collar and sleeve grips. If they are effectively pressuring forward and you cannot create space, it might be time to transition. Also, if they threaten a pass that is particularly dangerous from De La Riva, like a strong knee slice, disengage and transition to a more stable guard like Closed Guard or half guard.
