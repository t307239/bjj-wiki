De La Riva Guard: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The De La Riva Guard is an open guard position where one leg hooks the opponent's thigh, creating distance and control. It aims to disrupt the opponent's base and create sweep or submission opportunities.

White belts often struggle by stiff-legging their hooking leg or allowing their hips to collapse, negating the leverage. They also tend to overcommit to grips without establishing a solid base.

The core principle is using your hooking leg as a lever to control hip distance and off-balance, while your other leg maintains framing and base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated open guard, establish an underhook on the opponent's right arm with your left arm, gripping their collar at the sternum. Your right arm grips their right sleeve at the elbow joint.
    2. With your left leg, extend and hook the outside of your opponent's right thigh, just above the knee. Your left foot should be flexed, toes pointing upwards.
    3. Simultaneously, bring your right leg up and place your right foot on the opponent's left hip crease, fingers pointing towards their pelvis. This is your base leg.
    4. Shift your hips to your left, angling your torso slightly away from the opponent. Your right knee should point diagonally forward, away from the opponent.
    5. Use your underhook and sleeve grip to pull the opponent forward and slightly to their right, creating tension in your hooking leg.
    6. Drive your right foot into their hip to maintain space and prevent them from collapsing your guard. This creates a frame.
    7. Actively flex your left calf and hamstring, using your hooked leg to pull their thigh towards your torso, disrupting their balance. Your left knee should point slightly forward.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Stiff-legging the hooking leg: This puts excessive strain on the knee joint, specifically the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and medial collateral ligament (MCL), leading to tears. The correct action is to maintain a slight bend in the knee and use calf and hamstring tension.
    • Allowing the base leg (right foot on hip) to collapse: This leads to the opponent easily passing your guard and can result in ankle or foot sprains if the opponent steps on your foot incorrectly. Maintain a strong frame by keeping your right knee directed forward and your foot actively pushing.
    • Over-extending the hooking leg: This leaves your hip exposed and can lead to the opponent easily stepping over your leg for a pass, potentially causing knee hyperextension or groin strains. Keep the hook tight and controlled, with your knee slightly bent.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice transitioning into the De La Riva Guard from a seated position. Focus on the leg hook and base leg placement. (50 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner stands in front of you. Practice establishing the grips and leg hook, then maintaining the frame with your base leg. (25 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (light control): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice pulling them forward and maintaining your base. Focus on hip angle and leg tension. (20 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Sweep attempt drill: With light resistance, your partner attempts to posture up. Practice executing a basic sweep from De La Riva. (15 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Guard retention drill: Your partner attempts to pass your guard. Focus on maintaining your frames and leg hook to prevent the pass. (10 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (limited): Apply De La Riva Guard against a resisting opponent, focusing on establishing the position and one sweep attempt. (5 rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up and within arm's reach, but not too close.
    • When you have established a strong collar and sleeve grip on your opponent's arm.
    • When your opponent is attempting to establish side control or knee-on-belly from your guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Inside Pass: Opponent steps their right leg inside your hooking leg and drives forward. Defend by bringing your right knee to your chest and switching to a closed guard or half guard.
    • Toreando Pass: Opponent circles their right leg around your hooking leg. Defend by using your base leg to push their hip away and re-establishing your frames, or by transitioning to a Berimbolo.
    • Stacking: Opponent drives their weight forward and down onto your guard. Defend by actively pushing their hips away with your base leg and using your underhook to create space.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right knee hurt when I try to put my foot on my opponent's hip in De La Riva Guard?

    This often happens when you're not properly angling your hips and directing your knee. Instead of pushing straight forward, angle your right knee diagonally forward and away from your opponent. Ensure your foot is on their hip crease, not their stomach, and that your left leg is actively hooking their thigh to control their base.

    Q: How do I use De La Riva Guard against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maintaining maximum distance with your base leg and using your hooking leg as a precise lever. Instead of pulling them forward, use your Underhook to guide their weight to one side, creating an opening for a sweep. Prioritize strong grips to prevent them from easily closing the distance.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from De La Riva Guard to attack?

    The optimal time to attack from De La Riva is when your opponent is off-balance, usually after you've pulled them forward or to the side. Look for moments when their weight is not evenly distributed, making them susceptible to a sweep or a submission like a triangle or Armbar. Don't force attacks when they are stable.
