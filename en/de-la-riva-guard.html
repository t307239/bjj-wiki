De La Riva Guard: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The De La Riva Guard is an open-guard position used to control an opponent's base and create sweep or submission opportunities.

White belts often struggle by overextending their limbs or failing to establish a strong base, leading to lost positions and potential injury.

The core principle is using your leg as a hook to disrupt their balance and create angles for attack, not just to entangle.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated open guard, establish a pant grip on the opponent's right leg with your left hand, aiming for the ankle or shin area. Maintain a straight arm frame.
    2. With your right leg, hook the opponent's right hip crease or the back of their right knee, creating a 'DLR hook'. Keep your knee pointed towards the ceiling.
    3. Your left foot remains on the opponent's hip or thigh, acting as a base and distance setter. This prevents them from easily closing the distance.
    4. Shift your weight onto your left hip, angling your torso approximately 45 degrees away from the opponent. This creates space and leverage.
    5. Use your left hand on their hip or thigh to control their posture and prevent them from posturing up. Your right arm can frame their knee or thigh.
    6. When initiating a sweep, drive your left foot into their hip while simultaneously pulling their right leg slightly towards you with your left hand.
    7. As they step to re-balance, your right hook (the DLR hook) will begin to lift their leg, initiating the sweep motion. Maintain your hip angle.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Wrapping your foot around the opponent's ankle with your knee pointing outwards: This puts excessive stress on the knee joint, risking ACL or MCL tears. The correct movement is to hook the hip crease or knee with your knee pointing upwards.
    • Extending your hooking leg straight out: This makes your leg vulnerable to being trapped and passed, and also compromises your base, risking ankle sprains from an uncontrolled fall. Keep a slight bend in your hooking knee and use your foot to actively pull or push.
    • Relying solely on the pant grip without hip control: This allows the opponent to easily step out or posture up, leading to a loss of control and potential for armbar or other attacks. Always maintain a secondary control point like a hip or thigh grip with your free hand.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice entering the De La Riva Guard from various starting positions (e.g., seated guard, on your back), focusing on establishing the correct grips and hook placement. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Mirror drill: With a partner, practice the entry and establishing the hook, with the partner offering minimal resistance. Focus on hip angle and weight distribution. (5 minutes per side, 10% resistance)
    3. Sweep entry drill: Practice the initial sweep mechanics (hip drive, pulling action) without the partner actively resisting the sweep itself. (5 minutes per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Sweep attempt drill: The partner offers light resistance to the sweep, allowing you to feel the timing and pressure needed to complete the sweep. (5 minutes per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Guard retention and sweep drill: The partner attempts to pass, and you focus on maintaining the De La Riva Guard and transitioning to sweep attempts. (5 minutes per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Implement the De La Riva Guard during rolling, focusing on entering the position, maintaining it, and attempting sweeps or submissions. (3 rounds, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured up and has their weight forward, creating an opening to establish the hook.
    • When the opponent is trying to pressure pass or knee-slice, allowing you to use their forward momentum against them.
    • When you are on your back with your opponent in your guard, and they are attempting to establish a base or pass.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Knee-slice pass defense: If they attempt a knee-slice, maintain your left foot on their hip and actively push away, creating space to transition to a different guard or sweep.
    • Torreando pass defense: If they try to circle out, use your left hand to control their hip and your right hook to prevent them from fully disengaging. Be ready to switch to a single leg or butterfly guard.
    • Stack pass defense: If they try to stack you, drive your left foot into their hip and use your right leg to push their leg away, creating an angle to shrimp out or transition to a technical stand-up.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the De La Riva Guard?

    Your knee might hurt because you are externally rotating your femur or placing excessive valgus (inward) stress on the knee joint when hooking. Ensure your hooking knee points upwards towards the ceiling, not outwards. Keep a slight bend in the knee and use your foot to secure the opponent's hip crease or upper thigh, not their lower shin, to minimize rotational stress.

    Q: How do I effectively use De La Riva Guard against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on using your hips and angles to create leverage rather than pure strength. Your left foot on their hip is crucial for maintaining distance and preventing them from crushing you. Use your right hook to disrupt their balance and create angles for sweeps like the Scissor Sweep or tripod sweep by pulling their leg across their body.

    Q: When is the best time to switch from De La Riva Guard to another guard if my opponent is defending well?

    If your opponent is effectively neutralizing your right leg hook and keeping their hips away, it's a good time to consider transitioning. Look for opportunities when they step their base in to defend, which creates an opening to switch to a Butterfly Guard by bringing your left foot to their hip and underneath them, or to transition to a Z-guard by placing your left foot on their hip and stepping your right foot inside their leg.
