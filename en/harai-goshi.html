Harai Goshi is a dynamic hip throw originating from standing positions, aiming to off-balance and sweep an opponent to the mat. White belts often struggle by trying to muscle the throw rather than using proper leverage and body mechanics.

The key to a successful Harai Goshi lies in generating rotational force through your hips and then using your entire body as a lever, not just arm strength.

This technique requires precise hip alignment and a controlled sweep of the leg to create a powerful, efficient takedown.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong right lapel grip with your left hand, fingers inside the collar, and a right sleeve grip with your right hand, thumb inside the sleeve cuff. Your feet should be shoulder-width apart, facing your opponent.
    2. Step your left foot forward, angling your body approximately 45 degrees to your opponent's right side. Your left knee should track over your left foot.
    3. Turn your hips inward, bringing your right hip into contact with your opponent's lower abdomen and hip area. Your back should be relatively straight, not rounded.
    4. As you turn your hips, simultaneously pull your opponent forward and slightly to their left with your lapel grip, while driving your right shoulder into their chest.
    5. Sweep your right leg upward and outward, aiming to contact your opponent's right thigh or hip area with the back of your thigh, just above your knee.
    6. Continue the hip rotation and leg sweep, driving your opponent over your right hip as your right leg acts as a fulcrum. Your left leg provides a stable base.
    7. As your opponent falls, maintain control with your grips and follow them to the mat, aiming for a dominant position like side control or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to lift your opponent with your arms instead of rotating your hips: This can lead to a strained lower back due to excessive lumbar flexion and hyperextension.
    • Stepping too far forward or in the wrong direction: This compromises your base and can result in your opponent easily countering or you losing balance, potentially spraining an ankle or knee.
    • Sweeping your leg too low or too early: This can cause your opponent to step over it, leading to a trip or fall for yourself, and risking knee ligament damage from an awkward twist.
    • Not turning your hips into the opponent: This results in a weak throw and can cause strain on your hip joint as you try to force rotation without proper alignment.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo shadow drilling: Practice the footwork, hip rotation, and leg sweep motion without a partner. Focus on smooth transitions and body alignment. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner static drilling (no resistance): With a willing partner standing, practice the grips and the entire Harai Goshi motion, with the partner offering minimal to no resistance. Focus on feeling the hip contact and leg sweep. (25 reps, 25% resistance)
    3. Partner controlled drilling (light resistance): The partner offers light resistance and helps you feel the off-balancing points and the sweep. Focus on maintaining control and executing the throw with proper body mechanics. (25 reps, 50% resistance)
    4. Partner controlled drilling (moderate resistance): The partner actively resists, allowing you to practice generating power and overcoming slight opposition. Focus on timing and commitment to the throw. (20 reps, 75% resistance)
    5. Live drilling (controlled): Execute Harai Goshi in a live rolling scenario, but with the agreement to reset if the throw is not completed cleanly or if there's risk of injury. Focus on applying the technique under dynamic pressure. (10 reps, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt Harai Goshi during regular sparring. Focus on recognizing opportunities and executing the technique with full commitment, while being mindful of your partner's safety. (5 minutes, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Opponent is leaning forward, creating an overextended posture.
    • Opponent is posturing up after a failed guard pass attempt.
    • During standing exchanges when you have established strong grips and control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Uchi Mata (Inner Thigh Throw): If the attacker attempts Harai Goshi, you can step your leg inside their sweeping leg and drive forward with your hip, simultaneously lifting their leg with yours.
    • Osoto Gari (Major Outer Reaping): As the attacker commits to their hip turn, step your outside leg back and reap their attacking leg with your own, driving your hips forward to unbalance them.
    • Counter-Sweep with a Leg Trip: If the attacker's leg is low, you can step over their sweeping leg and apply a trip with your shin or foot, driving your weight into them to cause them to fall.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to do Harai Goshi?

    Your lower back likely hurts because you are trying to lift your opponent with your arms and upper body, rather than rotating your hips. This puts excessive strain on your lumbar spine. Focus on turning your hips sharply into your opponent, allowing your hips to do the work of the throw, and keep your back relatively straight.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Instead of trying to overpower them, focus on getting your hip deeper and higher on their body, making them feel lighter. Use their forward momentum against them by pulling them into your hip rotation. A strong lapel grip helps control their posture and draw them in.

    Q: The ideal time to attempt Harai Goshi is when your opponent is slightly off-balanced or leaning forward, creating an opening. This often happens after they attempt a guard pass and overextend, or when they are posturing up from your guard. Look for moments where their weight is not fully committed to their base.
