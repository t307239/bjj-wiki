Harai Goshi: A White Belt's Guide to the Sweeping Hip Throw

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Harai Goshi is a dynamic hip throw originating from a standing clinch, aiming to unbalance your opponent and sweep them to the mat.

White belts often fail by initiating the throw with arm strength alone, neglecting the crucial hip and footwork components.

The key mechanical insight is using your body's rotation and hip contact to create a lever that lifts and sweeps the opponent's base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grips: Secure a right-hand lapel grip (thumb-in) on your opponent's left lapel, and a left-hand sleeve grip on their right sleeve, pulling their elbow towards your chest.
    2. Stance and Entry: Step your left foot forward, angling your body 45 degrees to your opponent's right, placing your left foot slightly behind their right foot.
    3. Hip Contact: Drive your right hip forward and inward, making firm contact with the front of your opponent's right hip/pelvis area.
    4. Torso Rotation: Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, bringing your right shoulder towards your opponent's chest, initiating the pulling action with your arm grips.
    5. Leg Sweep: As you rotate and your hip connects, swing your right leg in a sweeping arc, from back to front, aiming to catch your opponent's right leg just above the ankle or at the shin.
    6. Weight Transfer: Simultaneously transfer your weight from your back foot onto your front (left) foot and then onto your right hip as you complete the sweep.
    7. Completion: Continue the rotation and sweep, driving your opponent over your right hip and leg, finishing in a controlled landing on the mat, maintaining your grips.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Knee Flexion: Bending only the knees without proper hip contact and torso rotation leads to a weak attempt and potential knee hyperextension. The knee joint can be overstretched, damaging the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) or meniscus. Instead, ensure your hip is the primary point of contact and rotation driver.
    • Over-reliance on Arm Pulling: Yanking with the arms without generating power from the hips and legs causes shoulder strain and ineffective throws. The rotator cuff muscles and ligaments can be torn or sprained. Focus on driving your hips and using your body's momentum.
    • Stepping Too Far or Too Close: Stepping too far forward with your left foot leaves you off-balance, while stepping too close prevents effective hip contact. This can lead to your own ankle or knee buckling under pressure, causing sprains or fractures. Ensure your left foot is positioned to allow for a solid base and hip engagement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling (0%): Practice the footwork, hip movement, and arm positioning without a partner. Focus on the 45-degree angle and hip drive. (20 reps)
    2. Partner Static Mirroring (25%): With a compliant partner, practice the entry, hip contact, and leg sweep motion without any resistance. Partner stands still. (15 reps)
    3. Controlled Grip Fighting & Entry (50%): Practice securing grips and executing the entry and hip contact. Partner offers minimal resistance to grip fighting. (10 reps)
    4. Assisted Sweep (75%): Partner provides a light forward or backward lean to help you feel the sweep. Focus on the full motion and weight transfer. (10 reps)
    5. Dynamic Entry & Sweep (90%): Partner actively resists grips but allows the throw to be attempted. Focus on timing the sweep after hip contact. (5 reps)
    6. Live Rolling Application (100%): Attempt Harai Goshi during live rolling, focusing on proper mechanics and timing. Do not force the throw if the opportunity isn't there. (5 attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is leaning too far forward, creating an overextended base.
    • When you have established strong lapel and sleeve grips and can control their posture.
    • When your opponent attempts a forward-moving attack, creating an opening for a counter-throw.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Osoto Gari: If your opponent blocks your Harai Goshi sweep with their leg, you can transition to a powerful reaping throw by driving your right leg back and sweeping their left leg.
    • Ankle Pick: If your opponent hops or pulls their leg away from your sweep, drop your level and secure a grip on their ankle, driving forward to finish a single-leg takedown.
    • Snap Down: If your opponent anticipates the throw and tries to stiff-arm or create distance, use your sleeve grip to snap their head down and follow up with a guard pass or another takedown.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to Harai Goshi?

    Wrist pain during Harai Goshi often stems from gripping too tightly with just the fingers or gripping the sleeve with an open palm. This puts excessive strain on the wrist joint. Ensure your grip is firm but controlled, using your entire forearm and palm to support the sleeve. Also, avoid pulling directly upwards; use your body's rotation to generate the leverage for the throw.

    Q: How can I use Harai Goshi effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on disrupting their base and posture before initiating the throw. Use your grips to pull them slightly off-balance forward. Your hip contact needs to be exceptionally firm, driving into their center of gravity. Crucially, utilize your body's rotation and leg sweep to create the leverage, rather than trying to overpower them with pure strength. Timing and precision become paraMount.

    Q: I keep falling over when I attempt Harai Goshi, what am I doing wrong?

    Falling over typically means your base is compromised and your weight transfer is incorrect. Ensure your left foot is positioned correctly behind their right foot to provide a stable pivot point. When you drive your hip in, you must simultaneously transfer your weight onto that hip and your front (left) foot, not remain balanced on your back foot. Practice the weight transfer in isolation to develop the correct motor pattern.
