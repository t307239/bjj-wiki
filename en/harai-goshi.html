Harai Goshi is a dynamic hip throw initiated from standing, aiming to unbalance your opponent and take them to the mat.

White belts often struggle due to insufficient hip rotation and poor weight transfer, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The core principle is to use your hip as a fulcrum and sweep their leg with your own, creating a powerful lever for the throw.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Right-handed attacker. Establish a strong right lapel grip at the opponent's collarbone and a left sleeve grip at their wrist.
    2. Entry: Step your left foot forward, angling your body 45 degrees to the right of your opponent's center of mass.
    3. Kuzushi (Unbalancing): Pull their left sleeve towards your right hip while simultaneously pushing their right lapel away with your left hand, creating a diagonal pull.
    4. Hip Placement: Rotate your hips 90 degrees clockwise, placing your right hip bone firmly against the front of the opponent's right hip, below their belt line.
    5. Foot Placement: Your left foot should be positioned slightly behind and to the side of the opponent's right foot, creating a stable base.
    6. Body Alignment: Tuck your chin to your chest and maintain a straight back. Your body should form a stable pivot point.
    7. Sweep and Lift: As you rotate your hips, sweep your right leg upwards and outwards, contacting the opponent's right thigh or calf.
    8. Execute the Throw: Simultaneously, drive forward with your hips and use your upper body to guide their fall, maintaining control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to throw by merely pulling the opponent forward without hip engagement: This can strain your lower back ligaments (e.g., lumbar sprain) due to excessive hyperextension and shear forces. The correct action is to insert your hip deeply and use hip rotation.
    • Lifting the opponent solely with your arms without hip power: This risks shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears from the excessive load. Focus on using your entire kinetic chain, powered by your hips and legs.
    • Incorrect foot placement leading to your own imbalance: Stepping too close or too far can cause your base to collapse, leading to a fall for yourself and potential knee ligament damage (e.g., ACL strain) if your knee buckles under unexpected load. Ensure your feet are positioned to support your weight and the throw.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling: Practice the footwork, hip rotation, and arm movements without a partner. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Mirror Drilling (No Resistance): Stand facing a partner. Practice the grips, entry, and hip insertion. Partner remains passive. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Assisted Entry Drill: Partner offers slight resistance to the kuzushi. You focus on hip insertion and sweep. (10 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled Throwing Practice: With a cooperative partner, execute the full Harai Goshi. Focus on safe landing and control. (10 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Dynamic Entry and Kuzushi: Partner applies light resistance to your initial pull and push. Focus on generating the unbalancing force. (10 reps, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Takedown Situations: Attempt Harai Goshi during live rolling, starting with limited grappling exchanges. (5 successful attempts, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is leaning forward, creating an opening for kuzushi.
    • During standing exchanges when you have established strong collar and sleeve grips.
    • When you feel the opponent's base is unstable or they are recovering from a previous exchange.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Leg Catch (Single Leg Takedown): As the attacker commits to the throw, the defender can quickly drop their hips, catch the attacker's leg, and transition to a single leg takedown attempt.
    • Osoto Gari Counter: If the attacker's sweep is weak, the defender can use their own hip to block the sweep and immediately counter with an Osoto Gari (major outer reaping).
    • Forward Roll (Ukemi): A skilled defender can perform a forward breakfall (mae ukemi) to safely absorb the impact and reset to a standing position, negating the throw.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right hip hurt when I try to do Harai Goshi?

    Your hip pain likely stems from not fully rotating your hips and instead trying to force the throw with your back. When you don't achieve a 90-degree hip turn, your right hip bone will be jamming into your opponent's side rather than acting as a stable pivot. This misalignment can cause muscular strain or ligamentous stress in your hip joint. Ensure you complete the full hip rotation and insert your hip bone deeply into your opponent's side.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, focus intensely on the kuzushi (unbalancing) phase. You need to break their posture and weight distribution significantly before committing to the hip insertion. Use a strong diagonal pull on the sleeve and a push on the lapel to create a strong pulling force that shifts their weight onto their toes. Then, ensure your hip is placed lower than theirs and you drive upwards with your legs as you sweep their leg.

    Q: I keep getting stuck in a stalemate when I try Harai Goshi, what am I doing wrong?

    A stalemate usually means your kuzushi is insufficient, or your hip insertion is not deep enough. If you're not effectively unbalancing your opponent, they can easily maintain their base. You must create a strong pulling and pushing action that forces their weight forward and to the side. If your hip isn't deep enough, it won't be a stable fulcrum. Aim to get your hip bone firmly against their hip bone, below their center of gravity, before initiating the sweep.
