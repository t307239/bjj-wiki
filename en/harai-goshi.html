Harai Goshi: The Sweeping Hip Throw for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Harai Goshi is a dynamic hip throw initiated from a standing clinch, aiming to sweep your opponent off their feet and onto their back.

White belts often struggle with Harai Goshi due to insufficient hip engagement and a reliance on upper body strength, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is driving your hips *under* your opponent's center of gravity while simultaneously using your sleeve grip to create a pulling lever.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grips: Establish a standard Gi grip: Right hand on opponent's left lapel (collar grip), left hand on opponent's right sleeve (sleeve grip).
    2. Stance & Footwork: Adopt a slightly staggered stance, feet shoulder-width apart. Step your right foot forward and slightly to the side of your opponent's right foot.
    3. Hip Rotation: Rotate your hips counter-clockwise, turning your back towards your opponent. Your right hip should be angled towards their left hip.
    4. Lowering Center of Gravity: Bend your knees deeply, lowering your hips below your opponent's waistline. Your right hip should be in contact with their right hip or slightly below.
    5. Driving Force: Drive forward with your hips, pushing them into your opponent's midsection. This creates an unstable base for them.
    6. Sleeve Pull & Arm Action: As you drive your hips, pull sharply with your left sleeve grip, drawing their arm across your body and forward.
    7. Sweeping Action: Simultaneously, lift and sweep your right leg upwards in an arc, aiming to catch their right leg behind the knee or on the calf.
    8. Completion: Continue the hip drive and sleeve pull as your right leg sweeps their leg, initiating their rotation over your hip and down to the mat.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Hip Insertion: Attempting to throw by pushing your hips *away* from the opponent instead of driving them *under* their center of gravity. This can strain the lower back ligaments (lumbar sprain) by hyperextending the spine.
    • Jerking Sleeve Grip: Pulling the sleeve grip with excessive force using only the arm, without hip drive. This can cause a rotator cuff tear in your shoulder or a biceps tendonitis due to the sudden, isolated tension.
    • Leg Collision/Rebound: Sweeping your leg too low or with insufficient upward force, causing your leg to collide with your opponent's shin or foot. This can lead to a knee ligament sprain (MCL/ACL) if your knee is forced into an unnatural angle upon impact.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Body Movement (0%): Practice the hip rotation, lowering of the center of gravity, and leg sweep motion without a partner. Focus on hip-to-hip contact and leg arc. (20 repetitions)
    2. Solo Grip & Movement (0%): Attach a training dummy or a sturdy belt to a post. Practice establishing the grips and performing the hip and leg movement. (20 repetitions)
    3. Controlled Partner Movement (25%): With a compliant partner standing, practice the footwork, hip insertion, and the initial pull of the sleeve grip. Partner offers minimal resistance. (10 repetitions each side)
    4. Controlled Throw Attempt (50%): Perform the Harai Goshi with a partner who understands the technique and offers slight resistance, allowing for a controlled throw. Focus on hip contact and the sweep. (10 repetitions each side)
    5. Semi-Live Takedown Practice (75%): In a standing drill, attempt Harai Goshi when the opportunity arises, with your partner actively defending. Focus on timing and commitment. (5 minutes)
    6. Live Rolling Takedown Attempt (90-100%): During live rolling, look for opportunities to set up and execute Harai Goshi. Focus on integrating the technique into your overall game. (Until fatigue or successful attempt)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From a standing clinch when your opponent is postured slightly forward.
    • When you have established strong lapel and sleeve grips.
    • When your opponent takes a weight-forward step, creating an opening for a sweep.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Defensive Posture & Base: If the opponent initiates Harai Goshi, immediately widen your base, lower your hips, and resist the hip insertion by keeping your feet firmly planted.
    • Uchi Mata/O Soto Gari Transition: If you feel the sweep coming, you can transition by stepping your leg *inside* their sweeping leg (Uchi Mata) or by countering with your own sweeping leg (O Soto Gari) as they attempt theirs.
    • Breaking Grips & Creating Distance: Actively work to break your opponent's sleeve and collar grips by prying them open with your free hand and creating space to step back and disengage.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to Harai Goshi?

    Lower back pain during Harai Goshi typically stems from incorrect hip mechanics. Instead of driving your hips *under* your opponent's center of gravity, you might be pushing your hips *away* or leaning back excessively. This places undue stress on your lumbar spine. Focus on bending your knees deeply and rotating your hips to create a solid connection and leverage point, rather than relying on a hyperextension of your back.

    Q: How can I use Harai Goshi against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your advantage lies in speed and technique, not brute force. Focus on getting *under* their weight by bending your knees significantly and using your hip rotation to get your hip bone to their hip bone. Your sleeve grip is crucial here; use it to pull their weight forward and off balance *before* you initiate the hip drive and sweep. Timing and a deep hip insertion are paraMount.

    Q: The opportune moment for Harai Goshi is when your opponent is slightly off-balance, perhaps leaning forward to attack or after they've just taken a step. Look for moments where their weight is not perfectly distributed. Establishing strong grips first is vital; attempt the throw only after you have control of their upper body and can initiate a pulling motion to help break their base.
