Mastering the Double Leg Takedown in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Double Leg Takedown initiates from a standing or clinch position, aiming to secure both of an opponent's legs to bring them to the mat. White belts often struggle by relying on brute force instead of precise body mechanics.

The key insight is understanding that the takedown is a kinetic chain transfer of momentum, not just a pulling motion.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Stance: Feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, hips low. Maintain a balanced, athletic posture with your center of mass below your opponent's.
    2. Entry: Step forward with your lead leg, placing it outside your opponent's lead foot. Simultaneously, lower your level by bending your knees and hips, bringing your torso upright.
    3. Penetration Step: Take a second, deeper step with your rear leg, driving it forward so your lead knee is behind your opponent's thigh. Your hips should be lower than theirs.
    4. Grip: Secure a "double-under" grip by wrapping your arms around your opponent's thighs, interlocking your fingers or cupping your hands just above their knees.
    5. Head Position: Drive your head into your opponent's chest or shoulder, ideally on the same side as your lead leg. This creates a strong structural connection.
    6. Drive and Lift: Initiate the drive by extending your legs and pushing off the mat with your rear foot. Simultaneously, use your hips to drive forward and slightly upward, lifting your opponent's base.
    7. Finish: Continue driving forward and slightly to the side. As your opponent's base breaks, guide them to the mat by continuing the forward momentum, transitioning to a dominant top position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly dropping your hips too low and rounding your back: This places extreme stress on the lumbar spine, leading to potential disc herniation or facet joint sprains. The correct movement involves bending the knees and hips while maintaining a neutral spine.
    • Lunging forward with your head down and arms extended: This exposes your neck to potential chokes and your head to impact, risking concussion. The head should be used as a structural point against the opponent's body.
    • Attempting to lift with your arms only: This strains the biceps and shoulder joints, risking tears or dislocations. The power comes from the drive of your legs and the pivot of your hips.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Shadow Drilling (0% resistance): Practice the steps of the Double Leg Takedown in the air, focusing on footwork, hip position, and grip. (10 reps)
    2. Partner Static Drill (0% resistance): Stand opposite a compliant partner. Practice the entry and grip-securing phase, ensuring correct body alignment and hip height. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner Controlled Entry (25% resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance. Focus on the penetration step and securing the double-under grip. (10 reps per side)
    4. Partner Controlled Drive (50% resistance): After securing the grip, your partner offers slight resistance to the drive. Focus on leg drive and hip pressure. (10 reps per side)
    5. Live Grip Fighting and Entry (75% resistance): Engage in grip fighting and practice entering for the Double Leg. Your partner will defend actively but not fully resist the takedown itself. (5 minutes)
    6. Live Rolling with Takedown Focus (90-100% resistance): Integrate the Double Leg Takedown into live rolling, attempting it when opportunities arise. Focus on execution and transitioning to top control. (2 x 5-minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up in your guard, creating a gap at their hips.
    • When your opponent attempts a snap-down or forward pressure, creating an opening for a low-level entry.
    • When clinching, if you can establish a dominant underhook or secure a collar tie that allows you to lower your level.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Whizzer (Overhook): If the attacker penetrates, immediately secure an overhook on their lead arm and drive your shoulder into their upper back, preventing them from driving through. This shifts their weight and disrupts their base.
    • Sprawl and Hip Escape: As the attacker drives, quickly sprawl your legs back and extend your hips away. Simultaneously, hip escape to create distance and break the grip. This forces the attacker to lose their connection.
    • Foot Sweep/Trip Counter: If the attacker is too upright, use your foot to sweep their lead leg as they penetrate, or as they drive, use a small trip motion with your foot to disrupt their balance and create an opportunity to disengage or counter.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to do a Double Leg Takedown in BJJ?

    Lower back pain during the Double Leg Takedown often stems from rounding your spine during the penetration step and drive. Instead of bending your knees and hips while keeping your back straight, you might be hinging at the waist. This puts excessive strain on your lumbar discs. Focus on keeping your chest up and driving through your legs, not your lower back.

    Q: How can I successfully perform a Double Leg Takedown against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. Instead of trying to lift, focus on driving their weight into you and then using their momentum against them. Secure the double-under grip low on their thighs. Drive your forehead into their chest and use your legs to propel yourself forward, aiming to off-balance them and bring them down through sheer forward momentum, rather than vertical lifting.

    Q: When is the best time to shoot for a Double Leg Takedown in BJJ if I'm starting from standing?

    The optimal time to shoot for a Double Leg Takedown from standing is when your opponent commits their weight forward or presents a clear opening at their hips. This could be when they are posturing up, attempting to pass your guard in a clinch, or even as a reactive counter to their forward pressure. Avoid shooting when they are perfectly balanced or have their weight heavily on their back leg.
