The double leg takedown is initiated from a standing position, aiming to secure both of an opponent's legs and drive them to the mat.

White belts often fail by "chasing" the legs without proper body connection or weight transfer.

The key insight is to use your head and shoulders to drive forward while your legs secure the tackle.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent stands square, you face them. Your lead foot is approximately one foot-length from their lead foot. Your lead shoulder is slightly lower than theirs.
    2. Secure a collar grip with your lead hand, pulling their head down and slightly to your side, creating a slight forward lean in their posture.
    3. Simultaneously, secure a pant grip on their lead leg's thigh with your trail hand, gripping the fabric just above the knee.
    4. Lower your level by bending your knees and hips, bringing your lead knee to the mat directly behind their lead foot. Ensure your back is straight, not rounded.
    5. Drive your head into their chest or shoulder on the side of your lead leg. Your head should be posting, not leading the charge.
    6. Explosively drive your hips forward and upward, using your legs as the primary power source. Imagine lifting their base.
    7. Maintain the pant grip and continue driving forward, forcing them to fall backward or to the side as you follow them down.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Rushing the takedown by lunging forward with your upper body: This strains the cervical spine and can lead to neck injuries (e.g., whiplash) as your head absorbs impact. The correct action is to drive with your legs and hips, keeping your head posted.
    • Bending your back like a question mark during the shot: This puts immense pressure on the lumbar spine and can cause disc herniation. Maintain a neutral spine, driving from your hips and glutes.
    • Gripping too low on the pants (shin/ankle) and not driving your hips: This leads to a "sloppy" takedown, often resulting in a knee hyperextension injury for the attacker if the opponent pivots or falls awkwardly. Secure the grip higher on the thigh and drive the hips.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Shadow drilling (0% resistance): Practice the footwork, level change, and head post 10 times per side.
    2. Partnered drilling (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the takedown sequence from standing. Focus on grip acquisition and driving. 5 sets of 5 reps.
    3. Controlled drilling (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to focus on the mechanics of the shot and driving. 5 sets of 5 reps.
    4. Cooperative drilling (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend but allows the takedown to be completed. Focus on maintaining connection and driving power. 5 sets of 5 reps.
    5. Live wrestling entry (75% resistance): Initiate the takedown during a live roll, but the primary goal is completion, not necessarily winning the exchange. 5 minutes.
    6. Full live rolling (100% resistance): Attempt the double leg takedown during regular sparring. Focus on timing and execution against a resisting opponent. 3 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent overextends their base or takes a large step forward.
    • When they are leaning forward, creating an opportunity for you to lower your level.
    • When you have secured a strong collar grip and can pull them slightly off-balance.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Sprawling: Opponent immediately extends their legs backward, flattening their hips to the mat, preventing your legs from securing theirs. Your response is to drive forward and around their hips.
    • Whizzer: Opponent secures an overhook on your lead arm and uses their body weight to push your head away, breaking your connection and often allowing them to circle out. Your response is to drive your head into their chest and circle with them, or secure a second leg.
    • Foot Sweep/Trip: As you shoot, the opponent uses their free leg to trip your lead leg or sweep your base, causing you to fall. Your response is to maintain your posture and hip drive, or transition to a single leg if possible.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try a double leg takedown in BJJ?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are not driving with your hips and legs, instead relying on your upper body and lunging. This can cause hyperextension or awkward twisting forces on the knee joint. Ensure your lead knee lands behind your opponent's foot and drive your hips forward and up, keeping your back straight.

    Q: How can I do a double leg takedown against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on speed, angles, and proper hip drive. Lower your level significantly and get your head to the correct posting position before driving. Use their size against them by driving them backward or sideways, forcing them to fall under their own weight. Secure strong grips and explode with your legs.

    Q: Should I shoot for a double leg takedown when my opponent has me in closed guard in BJJ?

    No, a double leg takedown is not typically initiated from Closed Guard. The double leg is a standing technique. Once on the ground in closed guard, you would focus on guard passing techniques. Attempting a takedown from guard would be ineffective and likely put you in a disadvantageous position.
