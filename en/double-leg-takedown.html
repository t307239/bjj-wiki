BJJ Double Leg Takedown: A White Belt's Precise Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Double Leg Takedown initiates from a standing position, aiming to secure both of the opponent's legs to drive them to the mat. White belts often hesitate, fearing the commitment required, which allows opponents to react effectively.

The core mechanical insight is to use your hips and driving force, not just arm strength, to lower your center of gravity and drive through your opponent's base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Stance: Assume a balanced athletic stance, knees slightly bent, feet shoulder-width apart. Opponent is also standing.
    2. Initial Contact: From approximately 3-4 feet away, step forward with your lead leg. Simultaneously, reach your hands to grip the opponent's thighs, just above the knees. Your grip should be a " Gable grip" (palms facing each other, fingers interlocked) or "palm-to-palm" grip.
    3. Level Change: Immediately lower your hips by bending your knees and sinking your weight. Your lead knee should bend to approximately 90 degrees, with your lead shin vertical. Your rear leg remains bent.
    4. Penetration Step: Drive your rear leg forward, stepping it past your lead leg's position. Your hips should be lower than the opponent's hips.
    5. Head Position: Drive your head into the opponent's torso, on the side of your lead leg, creating a "head-to-body" connection. Your head should be up, not tucked.
    6. Drive and Lift: With your head and chest driving into the opponent's core, use your legs and core to drive forward and slightly upward. Imagine driving through them.
    7. Completion: Continue driving until the opponent's base is compromised and they fall to the mat. Maintain your grip and drive throughout the movement.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Head Position: Driving your head down into the opponent's knee or shin can cause a concussion or facial injury. The correct movement is driving your head into their torso, looking up.
    • Over-Reaching with Arms: Relying solely on arm strength to pull the opponent down will lead to shoulder strain and ineffective takedowns. The correct movement utilizes leg drive and hip pressure.
    • Standing Too Tall: A high center of gravity makes you easy to off-balance and prone to injury. The correct technique requires a deep "level change" to get your hips below the opponent's.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling (0% resistance): Practice the steps (stance, grip, level change, penetration, drive) without a partner. Focus on smooth transitions and hip height. 10 reps.
    2. Partnered Entry and Grip (25% resistance): Partner stands passively. Practice your penetration step and securing the thigh grips. Partner offers minimal resistance to grip acquisition. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partnered Level Change and Drive (50% resistance): Partner stands passively. Execute the full takedown motion, focusing on the level change and driving force, but partner offers no defense. 10 reps.
    4. Partnered Controlled Takedown (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to maintain balance but doesn't actively defend. Execute the takedown with committed drive. Partner allows the takedown. 5 reps per side.
    5. Live Takedown Attempts (90% resistance): Partner actively resists the takedown attempt, trying to sprawl or circle out. Focus on timing and commitment. Takedown may not always succeed. 5 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (100% resistance): Integrate the double leg takedown into live sparring, attempting it when the opportunity arises. Focus on chaining techniques and recognizing openings. 5-minute rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured slightly forward, presenting a good target for a low penetration.
    • When the opponent is reaching for a collar or sleeve grip, creating a momentary opening.
    • When you have secured a dominant underhook or collar tie, allowing you to control their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Sprawl: When you feel the penetration step, immediately "sprawl" by extending your legs back and lowering your hips to the mat, creating a strong base and preventing the opponent from securing your legs. Your chest should remain pressed into their back.
    • Whizzer: As the opponent penetrates, "whizzer" by placing your arm over their back, hooking their hip or waist. Simultaneously, drive your shoulder into their neck/shoulder area and use your hips to push them away or off-balance.
    • Circle Out: If the opponent secures the leg, immediately begin to circle your hips away from their driving force. This can disrupt their balance and create an angle to disengage or transition to a different attack.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try a double leg takedown in BJJ?

    Knee pain during a double leg takedown often stems from driving your lead knee directly into the mat or slamming it down at an incorrect angle. This puts excessive shear force on the knee joint. To fix this, ensure your lead knee bends to approximately 90 degrees with your shin vertical, and the impact is absorbed by your driving leg's hip and ankle, not directly by the knee cap.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Focus on a deep "level change" to get your hips significantly lower than theirs. Utilize a powerful penetration step and drive forward with your entire bodyweight, aiming to get underneath their center of gravity. Don't try to lift them directly; instead, drive them backward or sideways to disrupt their base.

    Q: The optimal time to attempt a double leg takedown is when your opponent exhibits a forward lean or a slight posture break. This often occurs when they are initiating a grip fight, reaching for a collar, or pushing forward themselves. Look for moments where their weight is momentarily on their front foot, making them susceptible to a low penetration and drive.
