Mastering the Double Leg Takedown in BJJ for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

This technique begins from a standing position, aiming to take your opponent to the mat by securing both of their legs.

White belts often fail by relying on brute force instead of proper leverage and body mechanics.

The core principle is driving your hips forward and down while maintaining a strong, upright posture.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Initiate with a wide, stable base, feet shoulder-width apart. Maintain a slight forward lean, hips angled towards your opponent.
    2. Secure a Collar Grip with your right hand, pulling their head slightly down and to your left. Simultaneously, secure a Pants Grip (outside of the thigh) with your left hand.
    3. Step your left foot forward, placing it beside your opponent's right foot, creating a staggered stance.
    4. Drive your right knee to the mat, landing it slightly behind your opponent's right knee. Ensure your thigh is parallel to the mat.
    5. Simultaneously, drive your hips forward and slightly down, tucking your chin to your chest. Your chest should be in contact with their torso.
    6. Grip the far leg (their left leg) with your left arm, securing a deep grip around the ankle or calf.
    7. Explode forward and upward by extending your hips and driving through your legs. Maintain your grip and head position to control their balance.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly dropping your head too low: This can lead to neck hyperextension, risking cervical spine strain or injury.

    The correct movement is to tuck your chin to your chest, maintaining a neutral neck alignment.

    • Lunging forward with straight legs: This puts excessive stress on the knees, risking ligament tears (ACL, MCL) due to uncontrolled hyperextension.

    The correct movement involves a deep knee bend and driving with the hips.

    • Gripping too loosely on the legs: This allows the opponent to easily peel your hands off, leading to a loss of control and potential for them to counter.

    The correct grip is a firm, locked grip around the ankle or calf, using your forearms and hands to secure it.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo walk-throughs: Practice the footwork and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the depth of your knee drop and hip drive. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner assisted entry: With a compliant partner standing, practice initiating the grip and stepping in. Partner offers minimal resistance to your entry. (20 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Controlled takedown drills: Work with a partner who understands the drill. Focus on securing the grips and driving forward without fully completing the takedown. (30 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    4. Takedown completion drills: Execute the full takedown with a partner who allows you to finish the movement. Focus on driving and maintaining control. (20 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    5. Flow rolling (Takedown focus): Incorporate takedowns into light sparring where the goal is to chain techniques. Takedowns are initiated from a standing start. (5 rounds, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (Takedown attempts): Full sparring where takedowns are a valid starting point. Focus on timing and execution under pressure. (5 rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is postured high and off-balancing forward.
    • Opponent is reaching for your collar or sleeves, creating an opening.
    • Opponent takes a wide stance and is static, not actively defending.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Sprawling: Opponent attempts to drive forward. You immediately drop your hips and extend your legs backward, pushing your hips to the mat to block their head and chest.
    • Whizzer (Overhook): Opponent secures a double leg. You secure an overhook on their arm and drive your shoulder into their head, creating a strong frame to prevent them from driving through.
    • Footwork/Angle Change: Opponent attempts the double leg. You quickly pivot your hips and step your foot out to the side, creating an angle and breaking their connection to your legs.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I practice the Double Leg Takedown in BJJ?

    Knee pain during the double leg takedown often stems from dropping your knee directly onto the mat without proper cushioning or driving through your hips. This can cause direct impact trauma or ligament strain. To prevent this, ensure your knee lands with your thigh parallel to the mat, and focus on driving your hips forward and upward, using your legs and core for power, not just your knee as a pivot point.

    Q: How can I perform a Double Leg Takedown against a significantly larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. Focus on using a strong collar grip to break their posture and pull them slightly forward before initiating. When you step in, ensure your base is wide and your drive is explosive, using your hips to unbalance them rather than trying to lift them. A low center of gravity and driving your shoulder into their hips will help you move their mass.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Double Leg Takedown from the clinch in BJJ?

    The optimal time to attempt a double leg takedown from the clinch is when your opponent is slightly off-balance forward or has committed to a forward movement. Look for moments when their weight is shifted onto their lead leg, or when they are reaching for your collar, creating an opening. A strong collar grip to pull them forward can also help create the necessary imbalance to initiate the takedown effectively.
