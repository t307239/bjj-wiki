Double Leg Takedown: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Double Leg Takedown is initiated from a standing position, aiming to bring your opponent to the mat by securing both of their legs.

White belts often fail by relying on brute strength, poor hip positioning, or failing to commit to the shot.

The key insight is using your hips to drive forward and upward, leveraging your entire body's mass for a powerful, controlled takedown.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Stance: Adopt a staggered stance, lead leg forward, knees slightly bent, maintaining a hip-width base.
    2. Penetration Step: Step forward with your lead leg, bringing your trail leg to a position behind your lead knee, creating a deep lunge.
    3. Head Position: Drive your head to one side, outside of your opponent's torso, typically to their hip line. This prevents them from posting on you.
    4. Grip: Secure a "collar-and-bicep" grip on your opponent's right side (if your lead leg is right) - your left arm grips their collar, and your right arm grips their right bicep.
    5. Hip Angle: Simultaneously, drive your hips forward and downward, tucking your pelvis underneath your opponent's center of mass.
    6. Weight Transfer: Initiate a powerful forward drive using your legs and core, transferring your weight from your rear foot to your lead foot.
    7. Drive: Maintain forward pressure and elevate your opponent's legs by continuing to drive your hips forward and slightly upward.
    8. Finish: As they lose balance, drive them to the mat, ideally to their side, and transition to a dominant position like side-control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Head First Dive: Lunging forward with your head down and forward, attempting to grab legs without stepping in properly. This can lead to neck hyperextension or concussion if the opponent sprawls effectively. The correct movement is to step in and keep your head up and to the side.
    • Knee Impact: Dropping your trail knee directly onto a hard mat without proper cushioning or while your opponent is low. This can cause patellar tendonitis or direct knee cap impact injuries. The correct movement is to land with your lead foot firmly planted and your trail knee bent, not taking direct impact.
    • Arm Snatching: Trying to pull the opponent's legs with only arm strength and a weak grip. This strains your shoulders and elbows, leading to rotator cuff tears or elbow hyperextension. The correct movement is to use your hips and body weight to drive and lift.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling (0%): Practice the footwork, penetration step, and hip drive motion without a partner. Focus on hip angle and head position. (20 reps)
    2. Partner Static Setup (25%): Partner stands passively. You practice the penetration step, head position, and grip placement. No resistance. (15 reps)
    3. Partner Controlled Entry (50%): Partner offers light resistance to your entry. You focus on driving through their initial defense and securing the grip. (10 reps per side)
    4. Controlled Drive (75%): Partner actively resists your drive with light pressure. You focus on maintaining hip connection and driving forward. Partner helps to a controlled fall. (10 reps per side)
    5. Live Entry & Drive (90%): Full speed entry and drive, but the partner allows the takedown to be completed without actively trying to defend the transition to the mat. (5 reps per side)
    6. Full Live Takedown (100%): Attempt the double leg takedown in a sparring situation, focusing on proper execution and commitment. (5-10 attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up from a guard or half guard, creating space.
    • When your opponent is reaching for grips or trying to establish a front headlock.
    • When your opponent is moving backward, creating distance that you can close.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Sprawl: If the opponent shoots, immediately drop your hips to the mat, extending your legs back and flattening your body. This negates their leg control and creates a strong base to defend.
    • Whizzer: As the opponent attempts to drive in, circle your arm over their back and hook your forearm on their hip or back. This allows you to control their posture and prevent them from driving forward, often leading to a tie-up or counter-takedown.
    • Head Inside Single Leg Defense: If they secure one leg, immediately circle your head to the inside of their body and use your arm to block their hip. This prevents them from driving and allows you to establish a strong base to stand or transition.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do my knees hurt when I try to do a double leg takedown?

    Knee pain often stems from improper knee placement during the penetration step and drive. If you are landing your trail knee directly on the mat or if your knees are collapsing inward due to weak hip abductors, you're at risk. Ensure your penetration step lands with your trail leg behind your lead knee, not directly under it, and focus on keeping your knees tracking over your toes, engaging your glutes for stability.

    Q: How can I do a double leg takedown against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, brute force is ineffective. You must rely on speed, leverage, and proper biomechanics. Focus on a deep penetration step that gets your hips underneath their center of gravity. Instead of trying to lift them vertically, drive your hips forward and slightly upward with a powerful forward momentum, using their weight against them as you drive them off balance. Grip control is paraMount; secure a tight collar-and-bicep grip to prevent them from posturing up.

    Q: When is the best time to shoot for a double leg takedown in BJJ?

    The optimal time to shoot for a double leg is when your opponent is slightly off-balance or has committed to a forward movement that you can exploit. This includes moments when they are posturing up from your guard, reaching for grips, or moving backward. Avoid shooting when they are stable and have their weight distributed evenly, as this makes them harder to move and increases your risk of being countered.
