Half guard is a defensive and offensive position where one opponent controls the opponent's leg with their own leg, creating a strong base.

White belts often struggle by keeping their hips too close or too far, neglecting their frames, and allowing easy passes.

The core principle is controlling the opponent's hip and knee line with your own shin and knee, creating a wedge to prevent them from advancing.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start from a supine position, opponent kneeling or standing over you.
    2. Bring your right shin across the opponent's right thigh, just above the knee.
    3. Hook your left foot behind the opponent's right ankle or calf.
    4. Grip the opponent's left pant leg at the knee or thigh with your left hand.
    5. Grip the opponent's right collar or shoulder with your right hand, creating a frame.
    6. Drive your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle to the opponent's hips.
    7. Keep your right knee tight to your chest, preventing the opponent from stacking you.
    8. Your left elbow should be on the mat, creating a solid base and preventing them from posturing up.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Allowing the opponent to stack you by not keeping your hips angled and your knee tucked: this can cause lumbar spine compression injuries.
    • Overextending your arm while framing, especially when the opponent drives forward: this puts your shoulder joint at risk of dislocation or ligamentous tears.
    • Trying to 'muscle' your way out by pulling with your arms only: this can lead to wrist sprains or elbow hyperextension injuries.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice entering Half Guard from various starting positions (e.g., failed sweep, guard recovery) for 10 reps per side.
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): Your partner stands passively, allowing you to establish grips and frames. Practice 10 reps per side.
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner offers light pressure, simulating a basic Half Guard entry. 10 reps per side.
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner attempts a basic guard pass (e.g., knee slice). You focus on maintaining your Half Guard. 10 reps per side.
    5. Partner drilling (75% resistance): Partner applies more pressure and attempts common passes. Focus on maintaining the position and preventing the pass. 5 reps per side.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Integrate Half Guard into positional sparring, focusing on retention and transitioning. 3 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When an opponent passes your full guard and settles into a kneeling or standing position over your legs.
    • When you attempt a sweep or submission from closed guard and the opponent defends by posturing up and stepping out.
    • During guard recovery situations where you can intercept a leg to prevent a full pass.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): Drive your hips to your left, creating space to bring your right knee back across the opponent's body to re-establish full guard or transition to another guard.
    • Leg Pummeling: Actively move your trapped leg in and out, creating opportunities to either secure a better Half Guard grip or escape the position entirely.
    • Frame and Bridge: Use your right arm to frame against the opponent's shoulder or hip, and bridge your hips upwards to create space and off-balance them.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I'm in Half Guard?

    Knee pain in Half Guard often stems from poor hip positioning and lack of a proper frame. If your hips are too far back, your knee is taking excessive pressure from the opponent's weight. Ensure your knee is tucked towards your chest and your hips are angled at 45 degrees to the opponent's, creating a solid base and distributing weight through your pelvis, not solely your knee joint.

    Q: How do I maintain Half Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paraMount. Focus on controlling their hip line with your shin and knee, creating a strong 'wall'. Use your grips on their pants and collar to prevent them from generating forward pressure. Actively shrimp your hips away to create space, then use that space to re-engage your frame or transition, rather than trying to match their strength.

    Q: Is Half Guard a good position to attack from for a white belt?

    While Half Guard is excellent for defense and control, its offensive potential for white belts is best explored after mastering the foundational mechanics. Focus initially on retaining the position and preventing Guard Passes. Once comfortable with the defensive aspects, you can begin to learn sweeps like the hip bump or tripod sweep from this position, but prioritize positional safety first.
