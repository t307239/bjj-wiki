Your First Steps in Half Guard BJJ

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Half Guard is a fundamental BJJ position where one of your legs is trapped between your opponent's legs. It aims to stabilize your opponent and create opportunities for sweeps or submissions from a defensive posture.

White belts often struggle by simply lying flat, offering no frames or hip pressure, allowing easy passes. They also tend to extend their trapped leg, creating vulnerability.

The core principle is creating a strong, active frame with your free leg and hips, controlling distance and preventing your opponent from flattening you out.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a seated, slightly on-your-side position. Your opponent is likely in your guard, attempting to pass.
    2. Trap your opponent's right leg with your left leg, placing your left shin across their hip bone and your left foot on the outside of their thigh.
    3. Your right leg will be free. Position your right shin vertically, parallel to your opponent's body, with your right foot planted firmly on the mat or on their hip.
    4. Create a frame with your left forearm on your opponent's hip, ensuring your elbow is tucked and your forearm is perpendicular to their torso.
    5. Drive your hips towards your opponent, creating a 'quarter-guard' angle, rather than lying flat on your back.
    6. Secure a grip on your opponent's collar (e.g., cross-collar grip with your right hand) or sleeve (e.g., right hand on their left sleeve).
    7. Use your free right leg to push into their hip or thigh, creating space and preventing them from collapsing your guard further.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Extending your trapped leg straight: This exposes your knee and ankle ligaments (ACL, MCL, ankle syndesmosis) to hyperextension or twisting injuries, especially during leg lock attempts. Keep your trapped knee bent and protected.
    • Lying flat on your back: This removes your ability to generate hip pressure and frames, making your knee and ankle joints susceptible to compression and shear forces from stacking or pressure passes. Maintain a strong, on-your-side posture.
    • Overextending your arms to frame: This can lead to elbow hyperextension or shoulder dislocations if the opponent applies sudden pressure or transitions. Keep your frames tight and supported by your torso and hips.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg trapping and frame creation motion with no resistance. 10 repetitions per side.
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): Your partner stands or kneels passively. Focus on establishing the correct leg position and forearm frame. 5 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner gently tries to step their leg over your trapped leg. Focus on maintaining your frame and hip pressure. 5 repetitions per side.
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner attempts a basic guard pass (e.g., knee slice). Focus on creating space with your free leg and maintaining your frame. 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner applies moderate pressure and attempts a guard pass. Focus on retaining the half guard and preventing the pass. 3 repetitions per side.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Attempt to maintain half guard and work towards sweeps or transitions, while your partner attempts to pass. Focus on applying the learned mechanics and safety principles.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent has successfully isolated one of your legs and is postured up, attempting to pass.
    • When you are on your back and have an opportunity to trap an opponent's leg as they attempt a pass.
    • When transitioning from a failed sweep or guard recovery, and an opponent's leg gets caught.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Frame Retention: Keep your trapped leg bent and your shin across your opponent's hip. Use your free leg to push their thigh away, creating space and preventing them from flattening you. This maintains your base.
    • Hip Pressure and Angle: Drive your hips towards your opponent, creating a quarter-guard angle. This makes it difficult for them to flatten you out or generate significant stacking pressure. It also sets up hip escapes.
    • Forearm Frame: Use your free forearm to create a strong frame on your opponent's hip or bicep. This prevents them from collapsing your guard completely and allows you to shrimp or create space for transitions.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to keep my leg in Half Guard?

    Your knee pain likely stems from either overextending your trapped leg or a lack of proper hip engagement. When you extend your leg, your knee joint is vulnerable to shear forces. Instead, keep your trapped knee bent, with your shin across your opponent's hip, and focus on driving your hips into them to create a stable base and protect the joint.

    Q: How do I maintain Half Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your frames and hip pressure become critical. Use your forearm on their hip and your free leg to push their thigh away, creating space and preventing them from collapsing your guard. Focus on keeping your hips angled towards them, rather than lying flat, to minimize their weight advantage and make it harder for them to flatten you out.

    Q: You should transition when your opponent has stabilized their pass and you can no longer create effective frames or hip pressure. Look for opportunities when they overcommit to a pass, creating a moment of imbalance. A common transition is to shrimp your hips away and attempt to re-establish closed guard or transition to butterfly guard if you can free your trapped leg.
