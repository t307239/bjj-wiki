Mastering Half Guard in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu: A White Belt's Blueprint

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Half Guard is a defensive position achieved when an opponent has passed one of your legs, securing control of your hips while you retain one leg between you and them.

White belts often struggle by creating a flat, static base, allowing easy passes and neglecting active hip movement.

The core principle is using your trapped leg as a lever to create space and re-establish guard or achieve sweeps, driven by active Hip Escapes.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent passes one leg, establishing side control or knee-on-belly. Your goal is to bring your free leg's knee towards your chest, creating a frame.
    2. Secure an underhook on the opponent's far arm (away from your body) with your near arm, gripping their bicep or tricep. Your elbow should be tight to your body.
    3. With your free hand, establish a grip on the opponent's collar (lapel grip) or their far sleeve. This prevents them from posturing up.
    4. Drive your hips away from the opponent, creating space. Your trapped leg's knee should be angled slightly towards the opponent's hip.
    5. Use your underhook and collar/sleeve grip to pull the opponent's upper body towards you, collapsing their posture.
    6. Simultaneously, shrimp your hips laterally (away from the opponent's chest) and drive your trapped knee forward, aiming to get your trapped foot to the mat.
    7. Once your trapped foot is on the mat, your goal is to establish your full guard or transition to a sweep by driving your hips upwards and rotating your body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your trapped leg straight: This can hyperextend the knee joint, leading to ACL or meniscus tears. Maintain a bent knee and use it as a control point.
    • Allowing the opponent to flatten your hips to the mat: This compromises your ability to shrimp and create space, potentially leading to shoulder or neck injuries from pressure passes. Keep your hips elevated by driving your feet into the mat and using your frames.
    • Gripping too loosely on the opponent's arm or collar: This allows the opponent to easily break your grips and advance their pass, potentially causing wrist or elbow strain from uncontrolled movements. Maintain tight, active grips with your thumb wrapped.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip escape (shrimping) motion and bringing your trapped knee towards your chest, focusing on the mechanics of creating space. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (static): Partner lies on their back, you are in half guard. Practice securing the underhook and collar/sleeve grip, then shrimping out to create space. Partner offers minimal resistance. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner attempts a basic knee-slice pass. You focus on framing with your free leg and securing your grips to prevent the pass and create space to recover guard. (25% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (controlled resistance): Partner attempts a knee-slice or pressure pass. You actively use your grips and hip escapes to prevent the pass and work towards a sweep or guard recovery. (50% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (intermediate resistance): Partner attempts a more dynamic pass. You focus on executing the half guard mechanics to defend and look for sweep opportunities. (75% resistance, 3 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling: Implement half guard defensively and offensively during rolling, prioritizing correct mechanics and space creation over submission attempts. (100% resistance, 5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent has successfully passed one of your legs and is establishing side control.
    • When an opponent is attempting a knee-slice pass and you can intercept their leg.
    • When you are recovering guard from a failed sweep or submission attempt.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Recovery (Full Guard): Use your trapped leg to hook behind the opponent's knee and your free leg to come over their head, re-establishing full guard. This requires actively shrimping and driving your hips forward.
    • Sweep (Lockdown Sweep): Secure a lockdown grip with your trapped leg (foot hooked behind the opponent's ankle) and use your underhook to pull them off balance, sweeping them to your guard side. This involves strong hip drive and rotation.
    • Sweep (Inside Half Guard Sweep): Transition to an inside half guard position by bringing your trapped leg underneath the opponent's legs, then use your underhook and hip pressure to sweep them over.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to get out of Half Guard?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are extending your trapped leg straight, putting hyperextension pressure on the joint. Instead of straightening, keep a slight bend in your knee. Use your foot to hook the opponent's ankle or shin, and drive your knee forward to create space, rather than pushing your leg away.

    Q: How do I use Half Guard effectively against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, focus on creating space and disrupting their base. Use your Underhook to keep them close and prevent them from posturing up. Actively shrimp your hips to create a frame with your free leg, preventing them from flattening you. Prioritize escapes and sweeps that use leverage rather than brute strength.

    Q: When should I try to sweep from Half Guard versus trying to recover Full Guard?

    Try to sweep when you have a strong underhook, have created significant space with your hips, and the opponent's weight is distributed in a way that makes them vulnerable to being unbalanced. If the opponent is too heavy, has a strong base, or is effectively controlling your hips, prioritize recovering Full Guard by bringing your free leg over their head and re-establishing a Closed Guard structure.
