Half Guard is a defensive and offensive position where one of your legs is between your opponent's legs, controlling one of their hips. It's a crucial stepping stone from Closed Guard, offering more mobility and transition opportunities.

White belts often struggle by being too passive, allowing their opponent to establish dominant pressure and pass. They also tend to flail their free leg, losing the necessary structure and leverage.

The core principle is using your trapped leg to create a frame and wedge, preventing your opponent from establishing a solid base while simultaneously creating space for your hips to move and attack.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a bottom position, when your opponent attempts a guard pass, secure your left leg over your opponent's right thigh, hooking their inner thigh with your left foot. Simultaneously, secure your right leg under their right thigh, hooking their outer thigh with your right foot. This forms the 'half guard' entanglement.
    2. Establish a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand on your opponent's gi, pulling them slightly towards your left side. Your left arm secures a sleeve grip (e.g., opponent's right sleeve) or wraps around their waist.
    3. Drive your left hip into your opponent's right hip, creating a wedge. Your pelvis should be angled approximately 45 degrees relative to your opponent's body.
    4. Keep your trapped left knee pointed towards the ceiling, preventing your opponent from stacking you directly. This knee acts as a vital structural element.
    5. Maintain a base width with your feet approximately shoulder-width apart, providing stability on the mat.
    6. When initiating a sweep or transition, drive your hips *away* from your opponent's base, using the leg frame to create space. This forward hip thrust is critical.
    7. Ensure your grips are tight and your body is coiled, ready to generate power from your core and hips, not just your arms.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your trapped leg straight, leaving your knee vulnerable to hyperextension when your opponent applies pressure. This can damage the ACL or MCL. The correct action is to keep your knee bent and actively drive your hip into your opponent.
    • Allowing your opponent to flatten you out by not creating a hip frame. This can lead to shoulder impingement or neck strain as your opponent stacks. Always maintain your hip angle and drive your hip into their base.
    • Releasing your grips prematurely or using weak grips. This allows your opponent to easily break your structure and pass. Maintain active, strong grips on the collar and sleeve/waist throughout the sequence.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg entanglement and hip frame movement without a partner. Focus on the hip angle and knee position. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner stands passively. Practice securing the half guard, establishing grips, and driving your hip. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drilling (light pressure): Your partner applies minimal forward pressure, allowing you to practice maintaining your frame and hip angle. (30 reps)
    4. Partner drilling (controlled pass attempt): Your partner attempts a simple guard pass (e.g., knee slice). You focus on establishing and maintaining half guard. (40 reps)
    5. Live rolling (focus on retention): During rolling, prioritize maintaining your half guard against basic passes, not attempting sweeps. (3 rounds, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (integrated sweeps): Begin attempting basic sweeps from half guard while maintaining retention. (5 rounds, 75-90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent successfully passes your Open Guard or Butterfly Guard and settles into a position where one of your legs is trapped between their legs.
    • When you are defending a guard pass from Closed Guard and your opponent establishes a strong base, making it difficult to maintain a full guard.
    • When you are on your back and your opponent is postured up, attempting to break your guard, and you can strategically trap one of their legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Frame and Inside Control: Actively drive your hip into your opponent's hip to create space. Simultaneously, secure an inside grip on their thigh or pants with your arm, preventing them from easily advancing their hips.
    • Leg Over and Sweep Preparation: Once your hip frame is established, bring your trapped leg over your opponent's hip and hook their far leg. This sets up various sweeps by creating leverage and disrupting their base.
    • Underhook and Transition: Secure an underhook on your opponent's far arm or waist. This allows you to pull them closer, break their posture, and transition to offensive positions like sweeps or back takes.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to get into Half Guard?

    Your knee might hurt if you are overextending your trapped leg or allowing your opponent to drive their weight directly onto it. Ensure you maintain a bent knee position and actively drive your hip into your opponent's hip to create a stable frame. This hip pressure, not direct knee force, is what secures the position and protects your joint.

    Q: How do I set up a sweep from Half Guard when my opponent is much bigger than me?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. Focus on using your hip frame to create a small pocket of space, then drive your hips *away* from their base. Secure a deep collar grip and pull them down as you shrimp your hips. This disrupts their weight distribution and allows you to use your legs to elevate their hips for the sweep.

    Q: Should I always try to put my leg over their hip in Half Guard?

    Not always, but it's a very common and effective progression. The initial half guard setup prioritizes the hip frame and inSide Control to prevent the pass. Once that's secure, bringing your leg over their hip (often called 'locking up' half guard) creates a stronger entanglement and opens up more sweep and submission opportunities. Master the basic frame first, then progress to the leg over.
