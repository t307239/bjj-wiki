Half Guard: A White Belt's Biomechanical Foundation

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Half Guard is a fundamental defensive and offensive position initiated when an opponent passes one of your legs, leaving you with one leg inside their guard and one outside.

White belts often struggle by being too passive or by trying to force submissions without establishing control, leading to positional disadvantages.

The core principle is to use your inside leg to control the opponent's hip and your outside leg to create distance and prevent them from settling into a dominant pass.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a bottom position, as the opponent attempts to pass your guard, identify the leg they have bypassed.
    2. With your inside leg, hook behind the opponent's knee on the same side as your inside leg, ensuring your shin is perpendicular to their thigh.
    3. Secure a "collar grip" on the opponent's gi at the neck or upper chest with your corresponding arm, keeping your elbow tucked.
    4. With your outside leg, extend it to create a "frame" against the opponent's hip or thigh, preventing them from collapsing onto you.
    5. Keep your hips slightly elevated and angled away from the opponent's chest, creating a wedge with your inside leg's knee.
    6. Maintain pressure with your forearm on the opponent's bicep or shoulder on the side of your inside leg to prevent them from posturing up or driving forward.
    7. Your shoulder should be pressed into the opponent's hip on the side of your outside leg to limit their forward momentum and control their base.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to "lock" your legs around the opponent's waist without proper hip angle: This can hyperextend your knee joint, causing MCL or LCL tears due to excessive valgus stress.
    • Reaching for "armbar" submissions prematurely from Half Guard without establishing control: This leaves your arm exposed to a "kimura" or "armbar" from the opponent as they can easily isolate and attack it.
    • Allowing the opponent to "stack" you by not creating sufficient distance with your outside leg: This can lead to spinal compression injuries as their weight is directly applied to your torso and head.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo: Practice the leg placement and frame creation with no opponent. Focus on the angle of your inside knee and the extension of your outside leg (50 reps).
    2. Solo: Practice securing the collar grip and maintaining the hip frame without an opponent, ensuring your elbows stay in (50 reps).
    3. Partner Drill (0% resistance): Opponent stands, you are on your back. Practice achieving the Half Guard position and establishing the frame and collar grip. Opponent offers no resistance (10 reps per side).
    4. Partner Drill (25% resistance): Opponent attempts a slow "guard-pass" (e.g., "torreando-pass"). You focus solely on establishing and maintaining the Half Guard position with your frame and grip. Opponent does not actively pass (10 reps per side).
    5. Partner Drill (50% resistance): Opponent attempts to "pass the guard" more actively. You practice transitioning to a basic sweep (e.g., "scissor-sweep") from Half Guard once established. Opponent defends minimally (5 reps per side).
    6. Live Rolling (75% resistance): Integrate Half Guard defensively and offensively during rolling. Focus on keeping the position and attempting simple transitions or sweeps. Opponent actively tries to pass or submit (3-minute rounds).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent has successfully passed one of your legs and is attempting to establish a "guard-pass" like the "torreando-pass" or "knee-slice-pass".
    • When you are on your back and the opponent is postured up, attempting to "smash-pass" your "closed-guard" or "open-guard".
    • When you find yourself in a scramble and the opponent has secured a dominant position, and you need to create a defensive barrier.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Scissor Sweep": Once your Half Guard is established, use your inside leg to hook the opponent's ankle on the same side. Drive your hips up and to the side, pulling their ankle towards you with your inside leg, while your outside leg kicks out to unbalance them.
    • "Hip Bump Sweep": While in Half Guard, secure a "collar grip" and "sleeve grip" on the opponent. Drive your hips to one side, creating space, then quickly bridge your hips up and towards the opponent's chest, using your arms to push them off balance.
    • "Transition to Back Mount": If the opponent overcommits to a "pass", use your inside leg to "underhook" their far leg. Drive your hips into them, creating an angle, and shrimp your body to get your hooks in and take their "back-mount".

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to hold Half Guard?

    Knee pain in Half Guard often stems from trying to "lock" your legs around the opponent's waist without proper hip angling. Your inside knee should be hooked behind their knee, and your hips should be angled slightly away. This creates a wedge, not a vice, protecting your knee joint from excessive valgus stress and hyperextension.

    Q: How do I maintain Half Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on using your frames and grips to create space and prevent them from driving their weight into you. Your outside leg's frame against their hip is crucial. Secure a strong "collar grip" to control their posture. Prioritize maintaining the hip-to-hip distance to avoid being "stacked".

    Q: When should I transition from Half Guard to something else, and not just hold it?

    Half Guard is a transitional position. You should transition when you feel the opponent's weight shift or their posture break, creating an opportunity for a sweep like the "scissor-sweep" or "hip-bump-sweep". If they commit heavily to a pass, look to "underhook" their far leg and "transition to Back Mount". Holding it passively allows them to "pass".
