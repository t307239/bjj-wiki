Front Headlock: A White Belt's Guide to Mastering the Position

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Front Headlock is a dominant control position where you secure your opponent's head and one arm, typically from a top position like Side Control or during a guard pass attempt.

White belts often struggle because they lack the foundational body mechanics and focus on raw strength, leading to ineffective control and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and chest pressure to drive into their head, creating a fulcrum to break their posture and control their movement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a top position (e.g., knee-on-belly or during a failed guard pass), secure a "collar grip" with your right hand, deep inside their collar on your right side.
    2. Simultaneously, secure a "far-side armpit grip" with your left hand on their left armpit, preventing them from establishing a strong base.
    3. Drive your right shoulder into the side of their neck and head, aiming to break their posture forward.
    4. Angle your hips slightly to your right, creating a wedge between their chest and your shoulder.
    5. Transfer your weight forward, pushing your hips towards their head and slightly down.
    6. Keep your left arm tight across their chest, preventing them from turning into you or creating space.
    7. Your left knee should be anchored to the mat, creating a stable base while your right leg is extended for balance and forward pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying pressure by "cranking" the neck: This can lead to cervical spine strain or ligament damage in the neck, especially hyperextension.
    • The correct alternative is to drive your shoulder into the side of their head and neck, using your body weight to break their posture, not your arm strength.
    • Allowing the opponent to turn into you: If you don't maintain tight hip and chest pressure, they can rotate their hips, potentially injuring your shoulder or elbow as it gets trapped.
    • The correct alternative is to keep your hips angled and drive forward, ensuring your shoulder stays glued to their head.
    • Overextending your base while trying to apply pressure: This leaves your lower back vulnerable to injury or allows the opponent to escape by bridging or shrimping.
    • The correct alternative is to maintain a stable base with your left knee on the mat and your hips slightly elevated, driving forward with controlled weight transfer.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice securing the collar grip and far-side armpit grip, then driving your shoulder into an imaginary opponent's head, focusing on the hip angle and weight transfer. (20 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner lies on their side. You practice securing the grips and driving your shoulder into their head, feeling the pressure. Focus on hip angle and weight transfer. (15 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the control. Focus on maintaining the grip and pressure as they try to minimally move. (1 minute rounds, 5 rounds)
    4. Controlled positional sparring: Start in the Front Headlock position. Your partner tries to escape with basic movements, and you focus on maintaining control and breaking posture. (2 minute rounds, 5 rounds, 25% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring with escape focus: Your partner actively attempts common escapes (e.g., shrimping, turning). You focus on reacting to their movements while maintaining control. (2 minute rounds, 5 rounds, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Front Headlock into rolling, focusing on achieving and maintaining the position against a resisting opponent. (5 minute rounds, 3 rounds, 75-90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • During a failed guard pass where the opponent is turtled or their head is low.
    • When the opponent attempts to stand up from your guard and you can secure a headlock grip.
    • From side control when the opponent attempts to bridge or turn, creating an opening for a headlock.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Turtle Escape": The opponent tucks their chin, brings their knees to their chest, and attempts to roll forward or backward. Your goal is to maintain pressure and prevent the roll.
    • The "Hip Escape and Turn": The opponent uses their hips to create space and turns their body away from your pressure, attempting to face you or establish guard.
    • The "Underhook and Base": The opponent secures an underhook on your arm and uses it to create a strong base, allowing them to posture up and potentially escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do a Front Headlock in BJJ?

    Your neck likely hurts because you are using your arm and neck muscles to 'pull' or 'crank' the opponent's head, rather than using your body weight and shoulder pressure. This puts excessive strain on your cervical spine. Instead, drive your shoulder into the side of their head and neck, using your hips and chest to apply forward pressure, effectively breaking their posture without straining your own neck.

    Q: How do I get a Front Headlock on a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on precision and leverage over strength. Secure your collar and armpit grips quickly as they attempt to pass or from a compromised position. Use your hip angle to drive your shoulder into the side of their head, not directly into their face. Your weight transfer should be forward and slightly down, using your body as a wedge, rather than trying to lift or overpower them.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from a failed guard pass to a Front Headlock in BJJ?

    The optimal time is when your opponent's head is low and their posture is broken, often when they are Turtled or trying to force a pass. As you feel them commit to a direction and their head dips, that's your opportunity to secure the collar and armpit grips. Aim to immediately drive your shoulder into the side of their head, preventing them from recovering their posture and establishing control.
