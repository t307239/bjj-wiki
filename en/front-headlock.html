Front Headlock Position in BJJ: A White Belt's Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Front Headlock is a dominant control position, typically achieved from a failed Guard Pass or during scrambles, aiming to restrict the opponent's movement and set up submissions.

White belts often struggle because they lack the precise weight distribution and frame, leading to them being easily swept or losing control.

The key mechanical insight is maintaining a tight, angled connection with your chest to their head, preventing them from creating space or turning into you.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Initiate from a position where your opponent's head is forward, such as a failed guard pass or a snap-down.
    2. Secure a "collar grip" with your right hand, digging deep into the back of their neck, aiming for the trapezius muscle.
    3. Place your left arm across their face, securing a "forearm grip" on the opposite side of their head, just below the ear.
    4. Drive your chest into the side of their head, creating a strong connection and preventing them from lifting their head.
    5. Angle your hips ninety degrees to their body, so your left hip is forward and your right hip is back, creating a "hook" with your right leg.
    6. Distribute your weight forward and slightly to your left, keeping your base narrow but stable, with your left knee slightly bent.
    7. Maintain a "figure-four" grip with your arms, pulling their head down and towards your chest to secure the position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying a grip too high on the neck with the collar grip can pinch the trachea, causing pain and potential airway restriction, while also failing to control their head; maintain grip on the trapezius.
    • Extending your elbow on the forearm grip while the opponent pushes their head back can hyperextend your elbow, risking ligament damage; keep a slight bend in your elbow and use your shoulder for pressure.
    • Allowing your hips to be square with the opponent's body creates a flat base, making you susceptible to sweeps; always angle your hips ninety degrees, driving your left hip forward to create leverage.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice securing the collar and forearm grips on a dummy or mat, focusing on hip angle and chest connection (50 reps).
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): From a static position, one partner allows the other to establish the Front Headlock, focusing on grip and body positioning (10 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Opponent offers slight resistance by trying to create space or turn; focus on maintaining the tight connection (10 reps per side).
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Opponent actively tries to escape by creating space or turning; focus on maintaining control and adjusting hip angle (10 reps per side).
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Opponent attempts basic escapes, and you practice maintaining the Front Headlock and transitioning to a submission setup (10 reps per side).
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Attempt to achieve the Front Headlock during rolling, focusing on positional control and not necessarily submission (5 minutes, focusing on achieving the position).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • During a failed guard pass, when the opponent's head is low and forward.
    • After a snap-down, when the opponent's head is pulled down and they are off-balance.
    • During scrambles, when you find yourself in a position to isolate and control their head.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "The Turtle": The opponent tucks their chin and tucks their knees to their chest, creating a compact defensive shell; you must maintain pressure and look for opportunities to peel legs or secure a choke.
    • "The Turn-In": The opponent attempts to turn their body towards your chest, creating a "U" shape; you must adjust your hip angle and drive forward to prevent them from facing you.
    • "The Space Creation": The opponent tries to create space by pushing their head away or bridging; you must tighten your grips, maintain chest connection, and use your hips to drive them back down.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to hold the Front Headlock?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are using your neck muscles to create the pressure, rather than using your body mechanics. Ensure your collar grip is on their trapezius muscle, not the spine, and drive your chest into the side of their head. Your hips and shoulders should be doing the work of controlling their head, not your cervical spine.

    Q: How do I maintain Front Headlock against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on an extremely tight "collar grip" on their trapezius and use your forearm to control the side of their head, preventing them from posturing up. Angle your hips ninety degrees and drive your chest into the side of their head, using your body weight to maintain the connection rather than brute strength.

    Q: When should I transition from Front Headlock to a submission?

    You should only look to transition to a submission once you have absolute control of the Front Headlock position. This means the opponent cannot create space, turn into you, or easily escape. Common transitions include the Guillotine Choke or the anaconda choke, but only attempt these when the opponent's head is securely trapped and their posture is broken.
