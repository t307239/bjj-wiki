BJJ Back Take: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Back Take is a transition from a control position, most commonly guard or Side Control, to achieving dominant back control. It's a fundamental step towards securing submissions like the rear-naked choke.

White belts often struggle by overthinking or attempting the technique without understanding the critical weight shifts. This leads to losing position or exposing themselves to sweeps and submissions.

The core principle is to "climb" your hooks while maintaining chest-to-back connection, using your hips to create space and leverage. This secures your position before fully committing to the transition.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard: Secure a two-on-one grip on the opponent's right arm (left hand on wrist, right hand on bicep). Simultaneously, secure a collar grip with your left hand on the opponent's right lapel, pulling them forward to break their posture.
    2. Initiate Hip Escape: As the opponent leans forward, perform a hip escape to your right, creating a small pocket of space between your hips and theirs. Your hips should angle slightly towards their left side.
    3. Leg Pummel: Bring your left leg (the one on the same side as the arm you're controlling) up and over their right shoulder, aiming to secure your hook. Your right leg remains inside, ready to follow.
    4. Secure the Hook: Once your left leg is over their shoulder, drive your left foot into the mat or their hip to create a stable base. Your opponent's right arm should now be trapped between your torso and your left thigh.
    5. Climb to the Back: With your left hook secured, bring your right leg up and over their body, aiming to plant your right foot on the mat on their opposite side. This establishes your second hook.
    6. Chest-to-Back Connection: Maintain constant chest-to-back pressure. Your hips should be lower than their hips, and your shoulders should be higher than their shoulders. This creates a "seatbelt" grip.
    7. Seatbelt Grip: Secure a seatbelt grip: your right arm over their left shoulder and your left arm under their right armpit. Your right hand grips your left bicep. Your hips are now squared up to their back.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Neck and Shoulder Strain: Attempting to "pull" the opponent's head down without proper hip engagement can hyperextend the neck. This can damage cervical vertebrae or ligaments. Instead, focus on driving your hips forward to pull their upper body down.
    • Knee Ligament Tears: Lunging forward to secure hooks without maintaining a stable base can cause the knee to buckle inwards. This can tear the ACL or MCL. Keep your base wide and drive your hooks in with control.
    • Lower Back Sprains: Arching your back excessively to try and lift the opponent or create space can lead to lumbar spine strain. Maintain a neutral spine and use your hips and legs for leverage.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Visualization: Mentally rehearse the steps, focusing on grip placement and hip movement. (5 minutes, 0% resistance).
    2. Mirror Drilling (No Partner): Practice the hip escape, leg pummel, and hook placement on an imaginary opponent. Focus on body mechanics. (5 minutes, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner Drilling (No Resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the transition from closed guard to back control. Focus on precise grip changes and hip movement. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. "Flow" Drilling (Light Resistance): Work on the Back Take transition while the partner offers light resistance, allowing you to feel the connection and weight transfer. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled Sparring: Attempt the Back Take in a controlled sparring environment where the partner understands the goal and provides realistic defense. (5 minutes, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling Application: Integrate the Back Take attempt into live rolling, focusing on timing and reaction to the opponent's defense. (5 minutes, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent consistently brings their hips too close to yours in closed guard.
    • Opponent over-extends their posture trying to pass your guard.
    • During transitions from side control where you can secure a back take attempt before they establish their base.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention: If the opponent attempts a leg pummel to escape, maintain strong hip pressure and re-establish your guard by framing with your forearms and shins.
    • Arm Defense: If the opponent secures the seatbelt grip, immediately defend by posting your elbow on the mat and shrimping away to create space, then attempt to turn into them.
    • Preventing Hooks: If the opponent tries to secure their second hook, focus on hip pressure and framing with your arms to prevent their leg from coming over your body.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to Back Take from guard?

    Your back is likely hurting because you are using excessive lumbar extension instead of hip flexion and leg drive. When you try to pull the opponent down, you're arching your spine. Focus on driving your hips forward and using your legs to lift and turn, keeping your spine neutral.

    Q: How do I Back Take against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Focus on breaking their posture with a strong collar and sleeve grip, then using your Hip Escape to create a small, precise pocket of space. Your leg pummel must be quick and decisive to secure the hook before they can use their weight to crush you.

    Q: Should I always try to get the seatbelt grip first when doing a Back Take?

    The seatbelt grip is the ultimate goal for secure back control, but it's not always the first grip you achieve. The priority is securing your hooks. Once your hooks are in and you have chest-to-back connection, then you work to establish the seatbelt grip. Focus on the transition of hooks first.
