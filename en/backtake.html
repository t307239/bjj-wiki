The Essential BJJ Back Take Transition for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Back Take is a transition to gain dominant control from positions like Closed Guard or side control. It aims to establish a superior position for submission or control.

White belts often struggle with poor weight distribution and uncontrolled limb movements, leading to lost positions. They also frequently overcommit to a single grip without establishing secure body control.

The core mechanical principle is creating a superior leverage point by controlling the opponent's hips while simultaneously displacing your own body weight to create an angle for entry.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard: Secure opponent's right sleeve with your left hand using a "palm-to-palm" grip, and your right hand on their left collar with a "lapel-to-lapel" grip. Your hips should be flat on the mat.
    2. Hip Escape: Shift your hips to your left, creating a small gap between your hips and their hips. Simultaneously, post your left foot on the mat for base.
    3. Knee Slice Entry: Drive your right knee *underneath* their right hip, aiming to place it on the mat. Your pelvis should angle towards their right side.
    4. Posture Control: As your knee enters, use your collar grip to pull their head down and your sleeve grip to prevent them from posturing up. Keep your chest tight to their chest.
    5. Hook Insertion: As your knee hits the mat, immediately swing your left leg *around* their right hip to establish your first hook. Your left shin should be parallel to their spine.
    6. Body Triangle Setup: Swing your right leg over their hips, aiming to lock your ankles together for a body triangle. Your pelvis should now be perpendicular to their spine.
    7. Secure Back Mount: Once the body triangle is locked, your chest should be on their upper back. Ensure your hooks are deep and you have control of their posture with your chest pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Lunging forward with your head to secure a collar grip: This can cause your cervical spine to hyperextend, risking a neck injury (whiplash).
    • Correct: Maintain a stable base and use your hips to create the angle for entry, pulling them into your established position.
    • Sweeping your leg across their hips without establishing a hook: This leaves your leg vulnerable to being trapped or allows them to escape easily. It also risks knee ligament strain if you pivot incorrectly.
    • Correct: Ensure your shin is placed firmly against their hip bone and your foot is behind their leg to create a secure hook before swinging your other leg.
    • Trying to lock the body triangle by forcing your foot over their hip: This can lead to hip flexor strain or a groin pull.
    • Correct: Use your free hand to control their hip and gently guide your leg over, ensuring a smooth and controlled lock.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo movement: Practice hip escapes and knee slice entries without a partner. Focus on weight transfer and hip angle. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Execute the back take from closed guard with a compliant partner. Focus on grip placement and hook insertion. (10 sets of 5 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner resists slightly by keeping their base tight. Focus on overcoming mild resistance. (10 sets of 5 reps)
    4. Positional sparring (starting from closed guard): Attempt the back take against a partner defending their guard. Focus on timing and transition. (5 minutes, 50% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring (starting from side control): Practice transitioning to the back when the opponent attempts to escape. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the back take during regular sparring. Focus on applying the learned mechanics against full resistance. (10 minutes, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their hips are close.
    • When the opponent is turtled or giving you their back from another position.
    • When you have established a dominant position like mount and the opponent tries to roll away.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Frame: If they attempt to drive their hips into you during the knee slice, frame with your forearm on their hip and hip escape in the opposite direction to reset.
    • Leg Trap and Posture Control: If they try to pull their leg out after your first hook, trap their leg with your shin and use your upper body to maintain chest pressure and prevent them from turning.
    • Body Triangle Escape: If they attempt to lock a body triangle, immediately focus on breaking the lock by creating space with your hands and hip escaping to the side.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the BJJ Back Take from closed guard?

    Knee pain during the Back Take often stems from improper knee-on-mat pressure. When driving your knee under their hip, if your knee cap directly contacts a hard surface, it can cause bursitis or patellar tendonitis. Ensure you are bringing your shin and foot to the mat to create a broader contact surface, or use a mat with sufficient padding.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Instead of trying to muscle your way in, focus on creating small, precise angles with your hip escapes. Use a strong collar and sleeve grip to break their posture *before* attempting the knee slice entry. Once you create a small opening, commit to the hook insertion quickly.

    Q: The optimal time to attempt a Back Take is when the opponent's hips are closest to you and their posture is compromised, such as when they are attempting a guard pass or turning away. Avoid attempting it when they have excellent posture control or are actively pressuring you with a guard pass, as this offers fewer opportunities for a clean entry.
