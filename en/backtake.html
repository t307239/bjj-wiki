The Back Take is a transition from a dominant position like mount or Side Control to securing the opponent's back, achieving a superior attacking platform. White belts often rush this, neglecting fundamental body positioning and control.

The key is to maintain body alignment and weight distribution, preventing the opponent from turning into you or escaping before you secure the hooks.

Understanding the concept of 'cutting the corner' with your hips, driving your chest into their shoulder blade, is the mechanical core of a successful Back Take.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Mount: Establish control of the opponent's collar with your right hand (cross-collar grip) and their opposite sleeve with your left hand. Your chest should be pressed firmly into their sternum.
    2. Initiate the Turn: Begin rotating your hips ninety degrees to your right, pivoting on your left hip bone. Your weight transfers from being directly over their chest to being on your right hip.
    3. Drive and Slice: As your hips turn, drive your right shoulder into their left shoulder blade. Simultaneously, slice your left knee through the space between their torso and their left arm.
    4. Secure the Hooks: Once your knee is inside, bring your left foot to the mat, creating a "hook" behind their left thigh. Your right foot then follows, hooking behind their right thigh, forming the "body triangle" or "seatbelt" grip.
    5. Maintain Pressure: Keep your hips tight and your chest connected to their upper back. Your arms should remain in a "seatbelt" position: right arm over their shoulder, left arm under their armpit, hands clasped behind their neck.
    6. Adjust Weight: Ensure your weight is distributed forward, driving your hips into their back, not sideways or backward. This prevents them from turning out.
    7. Control the Head and Neck: Use your head to frame against their head, preventing them from turning their chin into your chest.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect hip turn: Rotating hips too late or too far sideways can allow the opponent to bridge or turn into you, leading to a loss of position. The correct movement is a ninety-degree pivot on the hip bone, driving forward.
    • Knee entrapment failure: If the slicing knee fails to penetrate their torso, it can get trapped between their body and arm. This can lead to a knee-on-belly escape for them, or a potential knee injury from twisting awkwardly. Ensure chest-to-shoulder blade connection as you slice.
    • Over-extension of arms: Reaching too far to grab the seatbelt can leave your arms exposed and weak, allowing the opponent to break your grip. Maintain a strong, compact grip with your elbows tucked.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Rotation and Knee Slice: Practice the hip turn and knee slice motion without a partner. Focus on hip angle and forward drive. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    2. Mount to Back Take (No Resistance): Start in mount, have your partner lie flat. Execute the Back Take with full control, focusing on grip and body mechanics. (5 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Mount to Back Take (Light Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance by trying to shrimp or bridge slightly. Focus on maintaining connection and driving through. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Side Control to Back Take (Light Resistance): Start in side control. Partner attempts to turn into you. Execute the transition to Back Mount. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live Rolling (Controlled): In live rolling, actively look for opportunities to transition to the Back Take from mount or side control. Focus on executing the mechanics correctly rather than finishing. (3 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling (Full): Integrate the Back Take into your game during rolling. Focus on securing the position and maintaining control. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When in mount and the opponent is turtled or trying to bridge, creating an opening.
    • When transitioning from side control and the opponent turns into you, offering their back.
    • When the opponent attempts a sweep from guard and exposes their back as they post.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shoulder Post and Hip Escape: If you feel the opponent turning their hips away, immediately post your shoulder on their hip and hip escape to create space and prevent them from securing hooks.
    • Chin Tuck and Frame: If the opponent is driving their chest into your back, tuck your chin to your chest and use your forearms to frame between your chest and their hips, creating space to turn into them.
    • Turtle Up and Grip Fight: If they are close to securing hooks, fight for their grips on your collar and sleeves. Turtle your body to protect your back and create opportunities to turn.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent always escape my Back Take attempt by turning into me?

    This often happens because your weight distribution is too lateral and not forward enough. Instead of driving your hips into their back, you might be sitting directly on their hip. To fix this, focus on driving your chest into their shoulder blade as you turn, and keep your hips tight and forward, almost like you're trying to hug them with your pelvis.

    Q: How can I perform a Back Take against a much larger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body mechanics are crucial. Focus on cutting the corner with your hips very tightly, making it difficult for them to use their mass to turn into you. Secure a strong cross-collar grip to control their head and prevent them from posturing up. Your drive should be through their shoulder blade, not just a general push.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to a Back Take from mount?

    The optimal time to transition to a Back Take from Mount is when the opponent gives you an opening by turning their hips away from you, or when they attempt to bridge to escape. Look for moments where their back is exposed. Avoid trying to force it when they are flat on their back, as this makes it much harder to secure the hooks.
