BJJ Back Take: A Precise Guide for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Back Take is a transition that moves you from a less dominant position to the most dominant one: the back. It allows for high-percentage submissions like the rear-naked choke.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise body control and an over-reliance on strength, leading to stalled attacks and lost positions.

The core insight is to use your hips to create space and leverage, rather than simply trying to grab and pull your opponent's torso.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a collar grip with your right hand on the opponent's left lapel, thumb inside. Your left hand grips the opponent's left sleeve, just above the wrist.
    2. Angle your hips 45 degrees to your left, lifting your left hip off the mat. This creates a space to insert your left knee.
    3. Drive your left knee into the space created between your torso and the opponent's left arm, securing it low on their side.
    4. Simultaneously, release your sleeve grip and secure a cross-collar grip with your left hand on their right lapel, thumb inside. Your right hand maintains the initial collar grip.
    5. "Hitchhike" your right leg up their back, aiming to get your right shin across their chest, between their arms.
    6. "Swivel" your hips to your right, bringing your right leg over their shoulder and securing your right knee on their chest while your left shin stays across their back.
    7. Lock your legs in a "body triangle" by tucking your right foot behind your left knee, or secure "seatbelt control" with your arms wrapped around their torso, right arm over their shoulder, left arm under their chin.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "pulling" opponent's collar without hip elevation: This can strain the cervical spine (neck) if the opponent resists forcefully or falls awkwardly, potentially causing whiplash. Always use your hips to create the angle and space.
    • Attempting to "step over" opponent's head without creating hip space: This can lead to hyperextension of the knee or ankle if the opponent moves, damaging the ACL or ankle ligaments. Ensure your knee is inserted first to control their posture.
    • Gripping too tightly on sleeves without proper body positioning: This can lead to wrist or elbow hyperextension if the opponent breaks the grip suddenly, injuring the wrist joint or ulnar collateral ligament. Focus on controlling their posture with your body first.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip angle and knee insertion movement without a partner. Focus on smooth transitions between grips. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Execute the full sequence from closed guard to back control. Partner remains passive. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the "give" and "take" of the movement. (15 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (defensive posture): Partner attempts to maintain a strong posture, requiring you to work harder for the angle and knee insertion. (10 reps per side)
    5. "Flow roll" with a focus on back takes: Both partners are allowed to move, but the objective is to attempt back takes from specific starting positions. (5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling: Incorporate the Back Take as a primary objective when in closed guard or other suitable positions. (10 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is in your closed guard and attempts to posture up, creating a gap.
    • When opponent successfully passes your guard but you are able to maintain a grip on their collar and sleeve as they move away.
    • During scrambles where you find yourself behind your opponent, even momentarily.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Escape to Turtle": If you feel the Back Take attempt, immediately drop your hips to the mat and drive forward into a "turtle" position, protecting your back by tucking your chin and placing your forearms on the mat.
    • "Bridge and Roll" (from guard): If the opponent has established the initial collar grip and is attempting to hook your leg, bridge your hips explosively towards their trapped leg side and attempt to roll them to your guard.
    • "Post and Stomp": If the opponent has their knee inserted and is trying to "hitchhike" their leg, post your foot on their hip and stomp your leg down forcefully to create space and break their grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to Back Take from closed guard?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are trying to "pull" your opponent's upper body towards you without properly using your hips to create the angle. This puts excessive strain on your shoulder joint and rotator cuff. Focus on elevating your hips and driving your knee into their side to create the space, then "climbing" your leg over their shoulder.

    Q: How can I Back Take against a bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is leverage over brute strength. You must prioritize creating a significant hip angle to break their base. Instead of trying to pull them down, focus on lifting your hips high, inserting your knee deep, and using their forward momentum against them as you transition. Precise grip fighting is also crucial to prevent them from "stacking" you.

The critical element is not letting go of the grips and immediately transitioning your hips to follow their movement, aiming to secure your hooks before they fully establish their pass.
