Mastering the BJJ Back Take: A White Belt Transition Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Back Take is a transition from a dominant position, typically guard or Side Control, to secure control of your opponent's back. It's a fundamental offensive maneuver that leads to high-percentage submissions.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or incorrect body positioning, leading to lost opportunities or injury. Focus on precise mechanics, not just muscling through.

The key insight is using your hips and chest to create a "wall" against your opponent's escape while your limbs secure the "hooks" and control their posture.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard: Secure a high collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside, and your left hand on your opponent's left sleeve. Your hips are on the mat, knees tucked.
    2. Initiate the "Umbrella Sweep" motion: Arch your back, lifting your hips slightly off the mat, and swing your right leg over your opponent's left shoulder, landing your right foot on their hip.
    3. Simultaneously, extend your left leg to create a frame against their right hip, preventing them from posturing up.
    4. As your right leg lands on their hip, transition your grip: release the sleeve grip and secure a gable grip around their neck/upper chest, tucking your chin to your chest.
    5. Drive your chest into their chest, creating a strong "seatbelt" grip with your arms (right arm over their shoulder, left arm under their armpit).
    6. "Walk" your hips to their side, specifically to their right, while keeping your chest glued to theirs. Your right knee should drive forward and slightly across their body.
    7. Swing your left leg over their hips, securing your "hooks" by placing your feet on their inner thighs, heels pointed towards your glutes. Your body should now be perpendicular to theirs, controlling their back.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "kicking" your leg over the opponent's shoulder when initiating the transition: This can hyper-extend the knee or ankle ligaments of your kicking leg if the opponent shifts their weight unexpectedly, leading to ACL or ankle sprains. The correct alternative is a controlled swing, using hip rotation to guide the leg.
    • Over-extending your neck to "choke" the opponent's head: This puts undue stress on the cervical spine and can lead to neck strain or disc injury. The correct alternative is to drive your chest into their chest, using your body weight for control.
    • Allowing your hips to "fall back" instead of forward during the transition: This creates space, allowing the opponent to escape or turn in. It can also strain your lower back if you're not properly braced. The correct alternative is to maintain forward hip pressure and a tight chest-to-chest connection.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip swing and leg over motion from closed guard, focusing on smooth transitions and grip changes. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo drill: Practice securing the seatbelt grip and "walking" your hips to the side, simulating the body turn. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (no resistance): Start in closed guard. Execute the full Back Take transition, focusing on precise body mechanics and grip placement. Partner remains passive. (20 reps per side, 10% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to your hip movement and leg placement. Focus on overcoming minimal pushback. (20 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    5. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the transition by framing and shucking your grips. Focus on maintaining control and finishing the technique. (15 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Back Take transition when opportunities arise, focusing on applying the learned mechanics under dynamic pressure. (5-minute rounds, 75-90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and you have an opportunity to "hook" their leg or body as they move past.
    • When you are in a dominant position like side control and can "climb" your hips to their back.
    • When you successfully execute a sweep from guard and land in a position where their back is exposed.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Shrimping" (Hip Escape): If the attacker attempts to climb your back, immediately "shrimp" your hips away from their controlling leg, creating space to turn your body and face them. Keep your arms tight to your body to block their chest connection.
    • "Frame and Turn In": If the attacker secures the seatbelt grip and tries to walk their hips, use your forearms to create a "frame" between your chest and theirs. Simultaneously, turn your hips into them, attempting to face their chest and break their control.
    • "Leg Pummeling": If the attacker has secured hooks, focus on "pummeling" your legs aggressively to the outside of their hips, attempting to break their hook connection and create space to regain guard or escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to take the back from guard?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are trying to "pull" your opponent's head down with your arms, creating a straining motion. Instead, focus on driving your chest into their chest and tucking your chin to your own chest. This uses your entire body's weight and structure to control their posture, not just your cervical spine.

    Q: How can I take the back against a much bigger opponent when they are in my guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision become even more critical. Instead of relying on strength, focus on creating angles. Use a "hip bump" motion to shift their weight, then "throw" your leg over their shoulder, using their own mass against them. Secure the seatbelt grip and immediately drive your chest into theirs to prevent them from using their size to simply push you off.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a back take from side control?

    The optimal time to transition to a back take from side control is when your opponent is actively trying to "bridge" or "turn in" to escape. As they create that movement, you can "climb" your hips higher up their back, securing the seatbelt grip and using their escape momentum to help you establish hooks. Be ready to adjust your weight distribution as they move.
