Back Take: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Back Take is a transition from a dominant position, typically guard or Side Control, to achieving the back control position. It aims to secure a superior attacking platform for submissions.

White belts often struggle due to imprecise body positioning and a lack of understanding of leverage, leading to wasted energy and missed opportunities.

The key insight is to consistently maintain positional control and create a "hook" with your body to secure the opponent's hips, preventing their escape and facilitating the transition.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from Closed Guard: Secure two collar grips on the opponent's gi, palms facing each other, fingers deep inside the collar. Maintain a tight guard, preventing the opponent from posturing up.
    2. Creating Space: Use your hips to create a small amount of space by pushing them away slightly, while maintaining collar grips.
    3. Initiating the Hip Escape: Execute a sharp hip escape to your right (or left), bringing your right knee *inside* the opponent's hip line. Your left leg remains hooked in their guard.
    4. Securing the "Seatbelt" Grip: As your right hip escapes, immediately transition your right hand to grip the opponent's far shoulder or the back of their neck, creating a "seatbelt" grip. Your left hand maintains its collar grip.
    5. The "Climb" and "Hook": Drive your right knee forward and around the opponent's body, aiming to place it on the mat on their far side. Simultaneously, use your left leg to "hook" behind their closest leg's knee, preventing them from turning in.
    6. Weight Transfer and "S" Mount: Shift your weight forward, driving your hips towards their back. Your right leg now acts as a hook, and your left leg swings over their hips to establish the back mount position (body triangle is an alternative).
    7. Maintaining Control: Continuously maintain pressure with your hips into their back and keep your hooks tight. Your seatbelt grip prevents them from turning their head away.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Hyperextension: Attempting to "kick" the leg over the opponent's hips without proper hip rotation can lead to hyperextension of the knee joint, potentially tearing the ACL or PCL.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Forcing the seatbelt grip by reaching too far across the opponent's back without proper body rotation can cause impingement in the shoulder joint, leading to rotator cuff tears.
    • Lower Back Strain: Exaggerated hip thrusting movements without engaging the core and maintaining a neutral spine can result in lumbar disc strain or facet joint injury.
    • Ankle Sprains: Ineffective "hooking" of the opponent's leg can lead to the foot slipping, causing the ankle to roll outwards or inwards, resulting in a sprain.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Escape and Knee Slice (0%): Practice the hip escape to the side, bringing the knee inside, and then the "climb" motion of the other leg without an opponent. (20 reps per side).
    2. Partner Assisted "Ghost" Back Take (25%): With a compliant partner, practice the hip escape, seatbelt grip, and the "climb" motion. Partner remains static. (10 reps per side).
    3. Controlled Back Take from Guard (50%): From closed guard, initiate the back take. Partner offers light resistance to the hip escape and posture. Focus on securing the seatbelt and hooks. (15 reps per side).
    4. Back Take with Light Resistance (75%): Partner actively tries to resist the hip escape and prevent the seatbelt grip. Focus on maintaining control and completing the transition. (10 reps per side).
    5. Simulated Back Take from Side Control (90%): Start in side control and practice transitioning to the back. Partner offers strong but predictable resistance. (5 reps per side).
    6. Live Rolling - Back Take Focus (100%): During live rolling, specifically look for opportunities to initiate the back take from guard or other positions. Focus on successful transitions, not submission. (5 minute rounds).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is posturing up in your closed guard, creating an opening.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their weight is distributed incorrectly.
    • When you have established a strong grip on their collar and can control their head.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to take the back from guard?

    Your back likely hurts due to an improper Hip Escape and weight transfer. Instead of a sharp, controlled hip escape, you might be twisting your torso excessively without engaging your core. Ensure your hips drive laterally, creating space for your knee to come inside, and then transfer weight forward over their back, not by arching your lower back.

    Q: How do I take the back against a bigger opponent who is very heavy in my guard?

    Against a heavier opponent, leverage becomes critical. Focus on using your legs to create the initial space by "posting" on their hips with your feet. Then, execute a powerful hip escape, driving your knee *deep* inside their hip line. The key is to get your hips lower than theirs and use your body weight to "climb" their back, rather than trying to "pull" them over.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a back take from closed guard?

    The prime opportunity arises when the opponent attempts to posture up and break your guard. As they extend their torso, it creates a momentary imbalance. You can exploit this by attacking their posture with your collar grips, creating a small aMount of space, and then executing your hip escape to initiate the back take before they can re-establish their base.
