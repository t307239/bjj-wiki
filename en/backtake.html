The Back Take is a transition from a dominant position, typically guard or Side Control, to secure control of your opponent's back. It offers a superior attacking platform for submissions like the rear-naked choke.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or poor body positioning, leading to wasted energy and vulnerability. They may also fail to establish secure grips early.

The core mechanical principle is using your hips and base to create leverage and angle, allowing you to 'climb' your hooks and establish a Body Triangle or seatbelt grip. This creates a stable, dominant platform.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard: Secure an overhook with your right arm on the opponent's left shoulder, and an underhook with your left arm on their right arm. Your left hand grips their right collar.
    2. Hip Angle: Simultaneously, drive your hips to your left, turning your torso 45 degrees to the left. Your right knee will slide towards their left armpit.
    3. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your left hip and leg, creating a base. Your right leg swings over their back to establish the first hook.
    4. Grip Transition: Release the collar grip, and secure your opponent's right arm with your left hand. Your right arm maintains the overhook on their shoulder.
    5. Second Hook: Drive your right knee into their hip and use your left foot to hook their left leg (inside their thigh). Your hips remain angled towards your left.
    6. Seatbelt Grip: Climb your right arm over their head, securing a seatbelt grip (right hand on left shoulder, left hand on right hip/waistband).
    7. Final Adjustment: Tighten your hooks by pulling their hips towards your chest with your legs, and pull their shoulders towards you with your arms. Ensure your chest is in contact with their back.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Lunging with your head forward while attempting to hook the leg: This can cause hyperextension of the cervical spine, leading to neck injuries like whiplash.
    • Releasing grips prematurely to 'grab air': This loss of control allows the opponent to escape and can lead to shoulder dislocation if your arm gets caught.
    • Kneeing yourself in the face when turning: Improper hip rotation and head positioning can result in facial impact and potential facial fractures.
    • Overextending your elbow when establishing the seatbelt grip: This can lead to a straight armbar defense from the opponent, potentially causing elbow ligament tears.
    • Trying to 'throw' the opponent over your shoulder during the transition: This uncontrolled movement can result in your own knee or ankle being twisted and damaged.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation (0%): Practice the hip-swiveling and leg-over-the-back motion without a partner, focusing on hip angle and leg placement. 50 reps.
    2. Partnered Static Drill (25%): With a compliant partner lying on their stomach, practice establishing grips and transitioning to the back-mount position. Focus on correct body mechanics. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partnered Semi-Resistant Drill (50%): Partner resists slightly by not fully extending. You practice the transition, focusing on maintaining control and achieving the back mount. 10 reps per side.
    4. Guard Pass to Back Take Drill (75%): Start from a neutral guard position. Attempt a specific guard pass (e.g., knee slice). If the opponent defends by turning away, transition to the back take. 10 reps.
    5. Live Rolling - Specificity (90%): Focus exclusively on back takes from any position for 2 minutes. Your partner knows you are attempting back takes. 3 rounds.
    6. Live Rolling - Full Application (100%): Attempt back takes organically within the flow of sparring. Focus on correct setup and execution. 5 minutes, 5 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is on their hands and knees (turtle position), offering a clear back to attack.
    • During a failed guard pass attempt where the opponent turns away, exposing their back.
    • From a dominant position like side control, if the opponent bridges or turns in a way that exposes their back.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "The Wall": If the attacker attempts to climb hooks, the defender extends their leg and creates a "wall" with their shin against the attacker's hip, preventing the hook from entering. The defender then drives forward to regain base.
    • "The Spin-Out": As the attacker attempts to secure the second hook, the defender violently spins their hips away from the attacker's direction of control, creating space to escape and re-establish guard or stand up.
    • "The Hip Escape and Turn": The defender actively hip escapes away from the attacker's pulling force and simultaneously turns their chest towards the attacker, breaking the seatbelt grip and creating a scramble to regain guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to take the back from closed guard?

    This often happens when you release your grips prematurely or try to force the transition without proper hip angle. If you let go of your collar grip to reach for the back without your hips being angled, your arm can become isolated and vulnerable to a shoulder lock or dislocation if the opponent counters by pulling it. Always maintain at least one secure grip and ensure your hips are turned before committing to the full transition.

    Q: How can I take the back against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paraMount. Do not try to match their strength. Focus on using their weight against them. When transitioning, keep your hips tight and close. Use your legs to "climb" their back, not just your arms to pull. A strong seatbelt grip, combined with tight hooks, will allow you to control their larger frame. Prioritize securing the hooks before attempting to finalize the position.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a back take from open guard?

    The best time to attempt a back take from open guard is when the opponent overcommits to a pass and their posture is broken, or when they are specifically trying to "stack" you. If they are driving forward with their weight low, this is an opportunity to use your legs to off-balance them, enter your hooks, and transition to their back before they can recover their base. Look for moments of imbalance and commitment from your opponent.
