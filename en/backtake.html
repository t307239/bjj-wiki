The Back Take is a transition from a dominant ground position to securing the opponent's back, offering superior control and submission opportunities.

White belts often struggle by rushing, losing posture, or failing to secure grips, leading to escapes and positional loss.

The key is a controlled hip movement and precise grip exchange, allowing you to "climb" your hooks and control the opponent's torso.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting Position: Closed Guard. Opponent is postured up, arms inside your legs.
    2. Grip: Secure a collar grip with your right hand, pulling opponent's head towards your chest. Simultaneously, grip their left sleeve with your left hand, pulling their arm across their body.
    3. Hip Movement: Shrimp your hips to the left, creating space. Your right hip should move towards their left hip.
    4. Leg Swing: Swing your right leg over their left shoulder. Your right knee should drive towards their chest.
    5. Grip Exchange: Release the collar grip. Secure your right arm under their left armpit, hand reaching for their far shoulder blade (seatbelt grip).
    6. Weight Transfer: Drive your weight forward, leaning into them. Your left leg hooks their left thigh from the outside.
    7. Hook Placement: Bring your left leg inside their right thigh, completing the "hooks" (body triangle formation).
    8. Final Control: Secure the seatbelt grip firmly with your right hand on their left shoulder blade. Your left hand can grip your own right bicep or their right collar for stability.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Hip Rotation: When shrimping, rotating hips outwards instead of towards the opponent's side. This strains the lumbar spine and can cause facet joint impingement.
    • Correct: Drive hips laterally towards your opponent's hip line.
    • Jerking Grips: Pulling aggressively on the collar or sleeve without proper body alignment. This can lead to wrist sprains or elbow hyperextension for both parties.
    • Correct: Use your body to establish control, not just arm strength.
    • Stiff-Legged "Climb": Trying to place hooks by straightening legs and pushing. This strains the knees and can tear the ACL if the opponent moves suddenly.
    • Correct: Keep knees bent and use hip flexion/extension to "climb".

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Shrimping: Practice shrimping motion without a partner. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Grip Exchange: Practice grip transitions from closed guard to seatbelt grip. (50 reps)
    3. Partner Assisted Transition (0% resistance): Partner remains passive, you execute the back take. Focus on grip and hip movement. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partner Assisted Transition (25% resistance): Partner offers light resistance to your movement. (5 reps per side)
    5. Controlled Rolling (50% resistance): Attempt the back take during live rolling, but stop if resistance is too high. Focus on maintaining position. (5 minute rounds)
    6. Live Rolling (75-100% resistance): Apply the back take in full sparring, focusing on timing and adaptation. (10 minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent postures up in your closed guard, creating space.
    • When opponent attempts to pass your guard and their weight shifts forward.
    • After successfully sweeping and transitioning to a dominant position where their back is exposed.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Stack" Defense: Opponent drives weight forward and down, "stacking" you. Maintain a tight frame with your arms and hips to prevent them from collapsing you, and look to transition to a sweep or escape.
    • The "Hip Escape" Defense: Opponent tries to escape by shrimping away. If you lose the grip exchange, immediately re-establish guard or transition to a different attack like a sweep.
    • The "Arm Pull" Defense: Opponent tries to peel your collar grip by pulling your arm. Use your body to anchor the grip and maintain control, or transition to a sleeve grip to prevent the escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do the BJJ Back Take from closed guard?

    Neck pain often occurs if you're pulling the opponent's head too hard with your arms alone, creating excessive neck strain. Instead, use your collar grip to guide their head down towards your chest, allowing your torso and hips to drive the movement, not just your neck muscles.

    Q: How can I Back Take against a much bigger opponent when they are defending well in my closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a larger angle with your Hip Escape to generate more space. Instead of a direct collar grip, try a "two-on-one" grip on their sleeve and wrist to break their posture down before initiating the leg swing and hip drive.

    Q: I keep losing the Back Take because my opponent turns into me. How do I prevent this?

    Losing the back due to an opponent turning in often means your hooks are not secure or your weight transfer is insufficient. Ensure your seatbelt grip is tight and your hips are driving forward to "climb" your hooks higher up their back, making it harder for them to turn into you.
