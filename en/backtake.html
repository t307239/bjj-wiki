Back Take: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Back Take is a transition from a non-dominant position to achieving control of your opponent's back. It offers superior leverage for submissions.

White belts often struggle by relying on raw strength or poor body positioning, leading to stalled attempts and increased injury risk.

The key insight is controlling the opponent's hips and creating a "hook" with your leg to prevent them from turning in, allowing you to secure your seatbelt grip.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from Open Guard (e.g., Spider Guard): Establish a collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left arm) with your left hand. Your left leg is hooked behind their right knee.
    2. Initiate Hip Turn: Use your right leg to push off the mat, simultaneously pulling your opponent's collar towards your chest and their sleeve across their body, initiating a hip turn to your right.
    3. Leg Switch and Hip Angle: As their hip turns, swing your right leg over their torso, aiming to place your right knee on their hip bone. Your hips should now be angled at approximately 45 degrees to their body.
    4. Secure the Seatbelt Grip: As your right knee contacts their hip, immediately transition your right hand from the collar to their far shoulder/back. Your left hand maintains the sleeve grip.
    5. Hooking the Far Leg: Drive your left foot (which was initially behind their knee) to the mat, and use your left leg to hook your opponent's far (left) ankle or calf. This is your "back hook."
    6. Securing the Second Hook: With your right knee on their hip and your left leg hooking their far leg, drive your hips forward and slightly up. Swing your left leg over their torso to establish your second "back hook" behind their other leg.
    7. Closing the Distance and Seatbelt: Pull your opponent's shoulders towards your chest, ensuring your knees are tight to their hips. Switch your right hand from their shoulder to their far collar, creating a secure "seatbelt grip" (right hand on their left collar, left hand on their right collar).
    8. Maintaining Control: Keep your chest tight to their back, drive your hips into them, and maintain both back hooks. Your opponent's hips should be unable to turn towards you.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Hip Extension: Attempting to force the transition by extending your hips backward without proper leg control. This can lead to a **torn ACL** in your knee as your knee buckles laterally under load.
    • Jerking the Arm: Yanking the opponent's arm forcefully to create space, rather than using coordinated hip and body movement. This can cause a **rotator cuff tear** in your shoulder or a **biceps tendon rupture**.
    • Over-Rotating the Torso: Twisting your torso excessively to reach for a grip without controlling the opponent's hips. This puts immense strain on your **lumbar spine**, potentially leading to disc herniation or muscle strains.
    • Premature Leg Extension: Extending your legs too early to hook the opponent's back before securing their hip. This leaves your base unstable and can result in a **knee hyperextension injury** if they sprawl or resist.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Turn and Leg Swing (0% Resistance): From a seated position, practice the hip turn and the motion of swinging your leg over the imaginary opponent's hip. Repeat 20 times per side.
    2. Solo Hook Placement (0% Resistance): Practice securing the first back hook (hooking the far ankle/calf) and then transitioning to the second hook. Repeat 20 times.
    3. Partnered Mirroring (25% Resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice the initial collar and sleeve grips, the hip turn, and the leg over the hip motion. Your partner offers minimal resistance, just allowing the movement. Repeat 10 times per side.
    4. Partnered Hook Securing (50% Resistance): Practice securing the first and second back hooks with your partner passively resisting by slightly shifting their weight. Focus on establishing solid hooks. Repeat 10 times per side.
    5. Partnered Seatbelt Grip and Control (75% Resistance): Execute the full back take transition, focusing on securing the seatbelt grip and maintaining chest-to-back pressure. Your partner attempts to defend by turning or creating space. Repeat 5 times per side.
    6. Live Rolling with Focus (90% Resistance): During rolling, actively look for opportunities to initiate the back take from your guard. Focus on correct mechanics, not necessarily completion. Aim for 3 attempts per round.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up in your closed guard, creating an opportunity to attack the neck or arms and transition to the back.
    • When you have established a strong spider guard or de la riva guard and can isolate an arm or leg to initiate a sweep that leads to the back.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and you can counter with a body lock or transition to their back as they move past your legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Turn-In" Defense: As you initiate the hip turn, your opponent immediately turns their hips towards you. You counter by adjusting your leg position to maintain control of their hips, potentially transitioning to a sweep or a submission like a triangle choke.
    • The "Sprawl" Defense: Your opponent sprawls their legs back to prevent you from getting your hooks in. You counter by driving your chest into their back and using your arms to pull their hips back towards you, or by transitioning to a different submission.
    • The "Elbow Escape" Defense: While you are securing the seatbelt, your opponent tries to bring their elbow inside your knee to create space. You counter by driving your knee harder into their hip and maintaining tight hip control, preventing them from creating space.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to Back Take from guard?

    This often happens when you rely on pulling the opponent's arm too forcefully to create space for your transition. Instead of yanking, focus on using your hips to turn their body and your legs to establish hooks. Ensure your seatbelt grip is secure on their far collar and shoulder, not just pulling their sleeve.

    Q: How do I Back Take against a much bigger opponent from guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body mechanics are crucial. Focus on controlling their hips with your legs (e.g., Spider Guard hooks) to initiate the turn. Once you start the transition, drive your chest into their back to negate their size advantage and use your hooks to maintain position. Don't try to muscle them; use their weight against them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Back Take, and how do I avoid giving up my own back?

    The best times are when an opponent is posturing up, attempting a pass, or when you've successfully swept them but can't achieve a dominant top position. To avoid giving up your own back, always maintain awareness of your opponent's hips and posture. If you initiate a transition and they turn into you, don't force it; react by adjusting your position or transitioning to a different submission or guard.
