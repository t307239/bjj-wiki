BJJ Back Take: Your First Steps to Dominance

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Back Take is a transition from a position where your opponent is facing away from you, aiming to secure the dominant back-Mount position. This is crucial for controlling your opponent and setting up submissions.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength or incomplete movements, leading to loss of control or injury. They may not understand the importance of creating angles and leveraging the opponent's structure.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to bridge and turn your opponent, creating a pocket for your legs to enter and secure the back.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From your opponent's closed guard, establish a high collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on their left arm with your left hand.
    2. Post your left foot on their hip, creating a frame, and angle your hips 45 degrees to your right.
    3. Drive your right shoulder into their chest, forcing their weight forward and breaking their posture.
    4. As their weight shifts, step your right foot over their left hip, bringing your right knee to the mat.
    5. Immediately swing your left leg over their hips, creating a body triangle, with your left shin across their stomach and your right foot tucked behind their right knee.
    6. Secure a seatbelt grip: right hand over their left shoulder, left hand under their right armpit, interlocking your fingers behind their neck.
    7. Drive your hips down and slightly forward, compressing their torso, while keeping your chest tight to their back.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Injury: Attempting to 'hook' the opponent's leg with your foot instead of securing a body triangle. This can lead to the opponent's leg hyperextending your knee or ankle, causing ligament tears (ACL, MCL) or sprains.
    • Lower Back Strain: Over-extending your lower back when trying to 'pull' the opponent over your shoulder, rather than using your hips to bridge and turn. This strains the lumbar spine, potentially causing disc compression or muscle pulls.
    • Neck Strain: Grabbing the back of the opponent's head with your hand when securing the seatbelt grip, instead of interlocking fingers behind their neck. This puts direct pressure on your own cervical spine if the opponent resists, risking muscle spasms or nerve impingement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Bridge and Leg Swing: Practice bridging your hips and swinging your legs into the body triangle position without a partner. (50 reps)
    2. Partnered Grip and Frame: With a compliant partner, practice establishing grips and posting your foot on their hip, focusing on hip angle. (25 reps)
    3. Controlled Transition Drill: Partner lies on their stomach. You practice the steps to get to the back-mount position, focusing on hip movement and leg placement. (50 reps)
    4. Semi-Resisted Back Take: Partner attempts to defend by keeping their elbows tucked. You execute the back take, focusing on creating space and securing the body triangle. (25 reps per side)
    5. Light Resistance Back Take: Partner offers light resistance to your movement, simulating a beginner defending. Focus on maintaining control and finishing the back take. (10 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling - Back Take Focus: During rolling, actively look for opportunities to initiate the back take from your guard. (5 minutes, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is in your closed guard and you have successfully broken their posture, creating a gap.
    • When the opponent attempts a guard pass and you can isolate an arm and a leg to initiate the transition.
    • When you have successfully swept the opponent and are transitioning to mount, but they turn away, presenting their back.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Defensive Hip Escape: If the attacker attempts to secure the body triangle, the defender can immediately hip escape to the side, creating space to prevent the triangle from locking.
    • Elbow Post and Frame: If the attacker's shoulder is driving into your chest, you can use your elbows to create a frame and push away, maintaining distance and preventing the posture break.
    • Turn Towards Attacker: As the attacker attempts to swing their leg over, the defender can immediately turn their hips and chest towards the attacker, making it difficult to secure the back and potentially allowing for a guard recovery.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to take the back from guard?

    This often happens when you're trying to 'pull' the opponent over your shoulder with your upper body and arms, rather than using your hips to bridge and turn them. Instead, focus on posting your foot on their hip, driving your shoulder into their chest to break their posture, and then using your hips to pivot and create the space for your legs to enter.

    Q: How do I take the back against a bigger opponent when I'm a white belt?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and angles are even more critical. Focus on breaking their posture first by driving your shoulder into their chest and using your legs to control their hips. Don't try to lift or pull them; instead, use your hip bridge to create a small window to swing your leg over their hips and secure the Body Triangle before they can recover their base.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a back take from closed guard?

    The prime opportunity arises when you've successfully broken the opponent's posture and their weight is forward, making them unstable. This is often after a failed Guard Pass attempt by them or when you've used a sweep attempt to create a brief moment of imbalance. Look for the moment their hips are off the mat or their base is compromised.
