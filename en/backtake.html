The Back Take transitions you to a dominant position controlling your opponent's back. It's a crucial transition from various guard scenarios.

White belts often rush, sacrificing control for position, leading to escapes or counter-attacks.

The key is establishing a secure Body Triangle or hooks before committing to the full transition.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard, secure opponent's collar with your right hand (cross-collar grip) and their left sleeve with your left hand.
    2. Hip angle: Shimmy your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle to their body.
    3. Weight transfer: Drive your right hip towards their left hip, forcing them to post their left arm.
    4. Grip adjustment: As they post, switch your left hand to grip their right arm's elbow, creating a "seatbelt" grip with your right arm across their back.
    5. Hip elevation: Drive your hips up, lifting their weight slightly off the mat.
    6. Leg transition: Bring your right leg over their head, aiming to secure a body triangle with your left leg underneath.
    7. Frame: Maintain your seatbelt grip and ensure your hips are tight to their back to prevent them from turning in.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect hip movement: Driving hips straight forward instead of at an angle can cause your opponent to simply shrimp away, leaving you exposed.
    • Neck strain: Tensing your neck while trying to secure the seatbelt grip can lead to cervical strain or whiplash if the opponent pulls away suddenly.
    • Knee hyperextension: Forcing the body triangle too aggressively without proper hip positioning can overextend your knee joint, risking ligament damage.
    • Armbar vulnerability: Leaving your arm extended too far during the seatbelt grip transition creates an opening for a counter armbar.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip shimmy and seatbelt grip setup without a partner (50 reps).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Partner lies on their back, you practice the full sequence from closed guard (20 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to the hip movement and grip establishment (30 reps per side).
    4. Partner drill (controlled transition): Partner attempts to defend by shrimping or turning, you focus on maintaining control and completing the back take (50% resistance, 10 reps).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the back take from closed guard in a rolling situation, focusing on correct mechanics, not just completion (75% resistance, 5 mins).
    6. Live rolling (full): Integrate the back take attempt naturally into rolling scenarios (100% resistance, 10 mins).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is stuck in your closed guard and attempts to stand or create space.
    • When opponent tries a guard pass and exposes their back momentarily.
    • When you successfully sweep and land in a position where their back is accessible.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent turns into you: Immediately drive your hips forward and attempt to secure a body triangle or transition to mount.
    • Opponent posts their arm to prevent hip angle: Use that posted arm to establish your seatbelt grip and drive your hips up to break their base.
    • Opponent escapes before you secure hooks: Do not chase; reset to guard or establish a different control position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent always escape when I try to take their back from closed guard?

    Your opponent likely escapes because you are not creating a sufficient biomechanical leverage. Ensure your hip angle is at least 45 degrees to their body and you are driving your hip into theirs, rather than just pulling them towards you. This off-balancing action is crucial for their base to break.

    Q: How can I take the back of a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on precise weight distribution and creating angles rather than brute strength. Use your legs to break their posture and create the 45-degree hip angle. Your seatbelt grip should be tight across their back, and your hips should drive upwards, lifting their weight to facilitate the transition.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a back take from closed guard?

    The optimal time is when your opponent attempts to pass your guard or create space to stand up. These actions often lead to them posting an arm or turning their body, which creates the necessary opening. Anticipate these movements and initiate your hip angle and grip transition immediately.
