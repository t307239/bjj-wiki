BJJ Mount Control — Detailed Mechanics

🥋 Blue ★★★☆☆ Intermediate

Mount is one of the highest scoring positions in BJJ, but many practitioners struggle to maintain it effectively. True mount mastery goes beyond just sitting on your opponent — it requires precise weight distribution, proactive hip control, and the ability to switch between high and low mount to maximize pressure while setting up submissions.

High vs. Low Mount

Low mount (hips near opponent's hips) provides stability and makes escape attempts difficult. High mount (hips near the chest/armpits) opens submission opportunities but is harder to maintain. Elite grapplers switch between the two fluidly.

Weight Distribution in Mount

Never post your weight on your hands — keep it all on your hips. Hands should float or be actively attacking. Hip-to-hip contact creates the most stable, heaviest mount. Sink your weight into the mat through the opponent.

The Grapevine vs. Foot Positions

Grapevine (feet hooked inside opponent's legs) immobilizes the bottom player completely but limits your mobility. Feet-flat position (feet outside the hips) allows better movement for submission entries. Choose based on opponent's escape attempts.

Arm Control Hierarchy

From mount, arm control determines your submission options. Underhook = Ezekiel choke or gift wrap. Overhook = armbar or triangle setup. Both arms free = crossface and collar choke options.

Preventing the Upa and Elbow Escape

The two main mount escapes are upa (bridge and roll) and elbow escape. For upa: widen your base and post a hand when you feel the bridge. For elbow escape: keep knees heavy on the ground, float your hips to counter the hip escape.

Step 1: Establish Base and Weight

When transitioning to mount, immediately widen your knees and drop your hips. Crossface the head with one arm to control posture and prevent the upa before it starts.

Step 2: Choose Your Height

Assess the situation: opponent is bridging → drop to low mount and widen knees. Opponent is flat → advance to high mount and start working submission grips.

Step 3: Control the Arms

Reach down and collect an underhook, or use a palm-down post on the shoulder to begin working the gift wrap. Arm control is the gateway to every mount submission.

Step 4: Attack with Submission Chains

In high mount, attack the Ezekiel choke first. If defended, work to S-mount for the armbar. If they straighten the arm, transition to the triangle. The chain prevents any single defensive answer.

Step 5: Maintain Through Transitions

When the opponent successfully starts an elbow escape, float your hips and slide your knee back in under their thigh. Constantly readjust your position to stay ahead of the escape attempt.

Common Mistakes in Mount Control Details

Sitting Too High

Mounting high on the chest gives your partner room to bridge and roll. Sit low — hips near the belt line — and sprawl your weight through your knees.

Reaching Forward Too Early

Leaning forward to grab the collar before establishing hooks invites the upa escape. Secure weight distribution before attacking.

Neglecting Hip Control

Without controlling the hips through knee pressure and foot hooks, escapes become trivially easy. Drive knees inward and maintain active pressure.

Abandoning Base

Losing base while attacking submissions allows reversals. Keep your base wide, weight centered, and never over-commit to a single attack.

Training Tips for Mount Control Details

Shadow Drill at Full Speed

Perform the technique slowly, then progressively increase to competition speed while maintaining crisp mechanics. Video yourself to catch form breakdowns.

Use a Skilled Partner

Training with a partner who can give realistic resistance and honest feedback accelerates technical development more than repetitions with a passive uke.

Isolate Weak Phases

Break the technique into phases and identify which phase breaks down under pressure. Spend disproportionate drilling time on that specific phase.

Compete in Tournaments

Competition reveals real weaknesses that controlled training obscures. Even white belts benefit from early competitive experience.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I prevent my opponent from bucking me off from mount?

To prevent a buck, keep your weight low and forward, driving your hips into their chest. Maintain tight grips, like a gable grip around their neck or controlling their arms, to limit their movement and leverage.

What are the most common mistakes people make when holding mount?

A common mistake is getting too high on the opponent's chest, which gives them space to bridge or shrimp. Another is not actively controlling their hips and arms, allowing them to create frames and escape.

How can I transition from mount to a submission effectively?

Once you have solid mount control, look for submission opportunities like the armbar or americana. Maintain your base and pressure while setting up the submission, ensuring you don't lose your dominant position.

Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

Q: Why do I feel like I'm losing my balance and falling off my opponent when I'm trying to maintain BJJ Mount Control — Detailed Mechanics?

You're likely not anchoring your hips low enough and keeping your base too narrow, causing your center of gravity to be directly over your opponent's. To fix this, focus on driving your hips down towards their sternum, creating a wide base by spreading your knees and shins across their chest and shoulders, and slightly leaning your weight forward to keep your center of gravity over their body.

Q: How can I prevent my opponent from bridging effectively to escape my BJJ Mount Control — Detailed Mechanics, especially when they are much larger than me?

When your opponent attempts a bridge, immediately tuck your chin to your chest and drive your hips down and forward, almost as if you're trying to sit on their face. Simultaneously, spread your knees wider and dig your shins into their hips and ribs to create friction and prevent them from generating upward momentum.

Q: My arms get tired very quickly when I try to keep my opponent pinned in BJJ Mount Control — Detailed Mechanics, what am I doing wrong biomechanically?

You're probably relying too much on your arm strength to squeeze and hold your opponent down, which is inefficient. Instead, focus on using your body weight and leverage by keeping your hips heavy and driving them into their chest, and use your arms primarily for framing and controlling their posture by gripping their shoulders or biceps, not for a full body squeeze.
