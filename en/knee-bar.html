The Knee Bar is a leg lock that targets the knee joint, typically applied from grappling positions like Closed Guard or half guard, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's knee. It's a powerful submission when executed correctly, but also carries significant risk for beginners.

White belts often struggle with the Knee Bar due to a lack of body awareness and fear, leading to uncontrolled movements and potential injury to themselves or their partner. They may rush the setup or fail to secure the necessary control, compromising the submission.

The core mechanical principle of the Knee Bar is creating a fulcrum with your hips and using your body weight to apply a lever arm against the opponent's tibia, isolating and hyperextending the knee joint.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish control by securing both of your opponent's ankles with your feet crossed behind their hips. Ensure your knees are tight against their sides.
    2. Untangle one foot and swing it over your opponent's hip on the side you intend to attack, placing it on the mat for base. This shifts your body angle.
    3. Simultaneously, bring your other leg up and over your opponent's thigh, securing your ankle with your opposite hand (e.g., right hand grips left ankle). This is the 'figure-four' grip.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly to the side of the leg you are attacking, creating a strong base and aligning your body to apply pressure.
    5. With your free hand, reach across your opponent's body and grip their opposite ankle securely. This prevents them from turning out.
    6. Arch your back and lift your hips towards your opponent's knee, applying pressure. Your chest should be in contact with their hamstring area.
    7. Extend your hips forward and upward, simultaneously pulling your opponent's secured ankle towards your chest. This isolates the knee joint and creates hyperextension.
    8. Maintain a tight grip on both ankles throughout the movement to prevent escape and ensure maximum leverage.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the Knee Bar without proper hip alignment: This can lead to the opponent's knee buckling incorrectly, potentially tearing the ACL or MCL.
    • Gripping too loosely on the attacking leg: If your figure-four grip slips, your opponent can often escape by turning, leaving you in a compromised position and risking a knee injury from the failed attempt.
    • Forcing the submission by 'pulling hard' with your arms: This ineffective method strains your shoulders and back and fails to create the necessary leverage on the knee. Instead, drive your hips forward and arch your back to generate power.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and figure-four grip on your own leg, focusing on smooth transitions and correct body positioning. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a willing partner lying on their back, practice the entire sequence from closed guard, focusing on precise body mechanics and grips. (20 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice securing the grips and hip movement. They gently resist leg movement. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drilling (controlled resistance): Partner begins to actively defend but allows you to complete the movement and secure the submission. Focus on maintaining control. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Knee Bar from closed guard during live rolling, but tap immediately if your partner is actively defending and you cannot secure the position. Focus on timing and setup. (5 attempts per round, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (submission focus): Attempt the Knee Bar in live rolling with the intention of finishing, but be mindful of your partner's safety and tap if they are in danger. (10 attempts per round, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up from your closed guard, creating space for you to swing your leg over.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you from closed guard, pushing their hips away from you.
    • When your opponent is trying to pass your guard and their legs are extended, offering an accessible target.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • The 'stack and roll' defense: As the attacker swings their leg over, the defender stacks their weight forward and attempts to roll their body in the direction of the attacking leg, relieving pressure.
    • The 'escape by turning': The defender forcefully turns their body away from the attacking leg, breaking the figure-four grip and potentially escaping the entanglement.
    • The 'grip break and posture up': The defender uses their free hand to break the attacker's figure-four grip and immediately drives their hips back to posture up, creating space and relieving pressure.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar from guard?

    Your knee might hurt due to improper technique, specifically a lack of hip alignment and over-reliance on arm strength. When you don't drive your hips forward and arch your back correctly, you create a shearing force on your own knee instead of isolating the opponent's knee joint. Ensure your hips are driving forward and your chest is pressing into the opponent's hamstring for correct leverage.

    Q: How can I apply a Knee Bar against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on establishing a tight closed guard initially to control their posture. When you swing your leg over, ensure your hips are angled sharply to the side of the leg you're attacking. Drive your hips forward forcefully and keep your chest glued to their hamstring; this creates a strong fulcrum. Grip their ankle securely to prevent them from turning out.

    Q: When is the best time to attack the Knee Bar from guard, and how do I avoid giving up a bad position?

    The ideal time is when your opponent is posturing up or attempting to stack you, as these movements create the necessary space and angle. To avoid giving up a bad position, always maintain a strong base with your hips and keep your free hand actively controlling their opposite ankle or hip. If the setup feels compromised or you cannot secure the grips, disengage and return to a solid guard position rather than forcing a weak attempt.
