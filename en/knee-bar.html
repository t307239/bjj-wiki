The Knee Bar: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Knee Bar is a powerful leg lock that attacks the knee joint, typically initiated from a dominant position like side-control or Mount.

White belts often struggle with this technique due to a lack of understanding of joint mechanics and proper body positioning, leading to missed opportunities or injury.

The key insight is to isolate the opponent's leg and create a fulcrum with your hips, extending their knee against its natural range of motion.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, establish a cross-face grip on the opponent's head with your left arm and control their far arm with your right arm (e.g., elbow grip).
    2. Shift your hips to the opponent's left, creating space to bring your right leg over their hip.
    3. Secure your opponent's right leg by hooking your right arm behind their thigh and gripping your own left bicep.
    4. Walk your hips back, pulling their leg towards your chest, ensuring their knee is bent.
    5. Bring your left leg up and over their hip, creating a tight "figure-four" lock around their thigh and knee.
    6. Extend your hips forward and slightly upward while simultaneously pulling their heel towards your glutes.
    7. Maintain a strong grip on your own bicep with your right hand, keeping your elbows tucked.
    8. Ensure your chest is pressed against their hip and your hips are angled to create torque on the knee joint.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "pulling" their leg without isolating the knee: This can lead to the opponent escaping or you injuring your own shoulder or back from overextension.
    • Instead, focus on trapping the leg and creating a stable base with your hips before applying pressure.
    • Squeezing your legs too tightly around the opponent's thigh before the hip extension: This can cause you to lose control of the leg and allow them to escape.
    • Instead, prioritize securing the grip on the thigh and then using your hips to drive the extension.
    • Attempting to finish the Knee Bar by lifting with your arms: This puts excessive strain on your own body and is biomechanically inefficient for attacking the knee.
    • Instead, drive your hips forward and upward, using your core and legs to create the extension.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip movement and leg over progression without a partner (10 reps each side).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice isolating the leg and setting up the figure-four grip (10 reps each side).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Practice the hip extension and pulling motion against minimal resistance (20 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (controlled resistance): Focus on the finish, with the partner tapping early to a controlled lock (20 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Partner drill (increasing resistance): Work on the transition from side-control to the Knee Bar setup with increasing pressure (15 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Knee Bar from side-control or mount in sparring, prioritizing correct mechanics over submission (5 minutes rolling, 90% intensity).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established side-control and the opponent is trying to shrimp away, creating a space to attack their leg.
    • When you transition to mount and the opponent is defending by turning into you, exposing their leg.
    • When you are in your closed guard and the opponent is posturing up, allowing you to isolate a leg.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Escape by Turning In": The opponent turns their hips towards the attacking leg, relieving pressure on the knee and allowing them to stand or roll out.
    • The "Straightening Defense": The opponent attempts to straighten their leg, making it difficult to get the proper "figure-four" lock and reducing the torque.
    • The "Roll Through Counter": The opponent, feeling the pressure, rolls in the direction of the Knee Bar, attempting to escape by reversing the position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are not properly isolating the opponent's leg and are applying pressure unevenly. Ensure you have a secure "figure-four" lock around their thigh and that their knee is bent. The pain in your knee might stem from hyperextending it yourself while trying to force the submission. Focus on driving your hips forward to extend their knee, not bending your own.

    Q: How can I successfully apply a Knee Bar against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paramount. Focus on precise hip angles and isolating their leg completely. Do not try to muscle them. Use your body weight to trap their leg and create a tight "figure-four" lock. The key is to create a strong fulcrum with your hips, so when you extend, their knee is forced to bend against its natural capacity. Grip strength and body control are more important than brute force.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from side-control to a Knee Bar?

    The optimal time to transition from side-control to a Knee Bar is when the opponent attempts to shrimp away, creating a gap between their hips and yours. This movement often exposes their leg and makes it easier to secure the "figure-four" lock. Also, when they are trying to bridge or push off your hips, their leg might become more accessible for you to trap and attack.
