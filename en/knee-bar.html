The Complete Knee Bar Technique Guide for BJJ White Belts

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Knee Bar is a powerful leg lock targeting the opponent's knee joint, typically applied from guard or Side Control. It aims to hyperextend the knee, forcing a submission.

White belts often struggle because they lack the precise body mechanics and fear of hurting their partner. This leads to sloppy application and potential injury for both.

The core principle is isolating the opponent's leg and using your body to create a fulcrum, levering their lower leg away from their upper leg while controlling their hips.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's left leg by trapping it between your thighs. Your right leg hooks over their left hip, preventing them from posturing up.
    2. Control their left ankle with your right hand, palm facing you, fingers gripping the heel cup. Your left hand grips their left shin, just below the knee, fingers pointing upwards.
    3. Untangle your left leg and swing it over their hips, positioning it perpendicular to their body. Ensure your left shin is across their lower back, creating a tight seatbelt.
    4. Shift your hips to your right by lifting your left hip off the mat. This angles your body, creating a fulcrum point for the lock.
    5. Bring your right leg (the one trapping their ankle) up and over their thigh, placing your right ankle bone just above their knee joint. This is your primary lever.
    6. Squeeze your knees together, bringing your right heel towards your left hip. This action compresses their knee joint.
    7. Extend your hips upwards and slightly backward, pushing your pelvis away from their knee. This creates the hyperextension that forces the submission.
    8. Maintain head control by keeping your head pressed against their chest or shoulder. This prevents them from stacking you.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive, uncontrolled pressure with your hips before establishing a proper frame: This can cause a hyperextension injury to the ACL or MCL in the opponent's knee.
    • Gripping too low on the shin or ankle, allowing the opponent's leg to slip: This can lead to a calf slicer effect or uncontrolled twisting of the ankle if the limb is not properly isolated.
    • Not isolating the leg correctly, allowing the opponent to turn into you: This can result in a twisting injury to the knee or ankle as the limb is not held securely.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drill: Practice the hip angle shift and leg swing without a partner. Focus on hip elevation and shin placement across the back. (50 reps)
    2. Partner Drill (Passive): With a compliant partner in closed guard, practice securing the ankle and shin grips, then executing the hip shift and leg swing. Partner offers no resistance. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner Drill (Controlled Movement): Practice the full Knee Bar sequence from closed guard. Partner gently resists by trying to pull their leg out, but does not actively defend. (15 reps per side)
    4. Partner Drill (Light Resistance - 25%): Apply the Knee Bar. Partner attempts a mild escape, like trying to straighten their leg or shrimp away. You focus on maintaining control and hip angle.
    5. Partner Drill (Moderate Resistance - 50%): Apply the Knee Bar. Partner actively tries to escape by turning, posturing, or defending the grips. You must adjust your body mechanics to maintain the lock.
    6. Live Rolling (Controlled - 75%): During rolling, you can explicitly go for the Knee Bar. Your partner is aware and will defend, but not try to intentionally injure you. Focus on smooth transitions and proper finishing mechanics.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard and you can isolate a leg.
    • When transitioning from side control to an attacking position and you can secure a leg.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and you can catch their leg mid-motion.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Stack" Defense: The opponent drives their weight forward, attempting to stack you and relieve pressure on the knee. To counter, maintain your hip angle and keep your legs tight, preventing them from driving in.
    • The "Turn-In" Defense: The opponent attempts to turn their body into the direction of the Knee Bar, relieving pressure. To counter, maintain control of the ankle and shin, and adjust your hip angle to follow their rotation, keeping the lever arm intact.
    • The "Leg Pull" Defense: The opponent attempts to pull their leg out of the lock. To counter, secure a strong grip on the ankle and shin, and use your hips to drive into their thigh, preventing the escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee bar feel weak and my opponent can easily escape?

    The most common reason for a weak knee bar is insufficient hip isolation and an incorrect body angle. You must shift your hips significantly to your side and swing your finishing leg over their hips, creating a perpendicular fulcrum. Without this angle, you're not effectively levering their knee joint.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, precise body mechanics are paramount. Focus on isolating their leg early and using your hips to create space and leverage. Ensure your finishing leg's ankle bone is positioned high on their thigh, just above the knee. Driving your hips forward and slightly backward, while keeping your core tight, will maximize your finishing power.

    Q: No, you should never 'pull hard' on the ankle with brute force. The Knee Bar is a lever, not a tug-of-war. The submission comes from the extension of your hips and the pressure created by your legs squeezing together, not from yanking their ankle. Yanking can lead to uncontrolled joint stress and potential injury.
