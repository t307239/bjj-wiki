The Knee Bar is a leg lock targeting the opponent's knee joint, applied from various positions like Closed Guard or side control. It achieves hyperextension or twisting of the knee.

White belts often fail due to imprecise body positioning and uncontrolled pressure, leading to ineffective application or injury to themselves or their partner.

The key mechanical insight is isolating the opponent's leg and using your hips to create a fulcrum, driving extension with precise hip pressure, not just squeezing.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure opponent's leg: From closed guard, secure the opponent's right leg by trapping it between your thighs and controlling their ankle with your left hand (grip: opponent's ankle, close-grip).
    2. Hip angle: Turn your hips 90 degrees to the left, creating a perpendicular line to your opponent's body.
    3. Seatbelt grip: With your right arm, reach across your opponent's body and secure a seatbelt grip on their left hip/waist, pulling them towards you.
    4. Leg isolation: Use your right leg to hook behind the opponent's right hip, preventing them from turning out.
    5. Hip drive: Lift your hips off the mat, bringing your left knee towards your chest while simultaneously driving your hips forward and upward.
    6. Knee pressure: Use your chest and the crook of your left elbow to apply downward pressure on the opponent's knee joint.
    7. Extension: Maintain a tight grip on the ankle and drive your hips forward, creating hyperextension of the opponent's knee.
    8. Control the finish: Once the opponent taps, immediately release all pressure and return to a neutral position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect hip rotation: Turning hips away from the opponent's leg instead of perpendicular to it. This leads to insufficient leverage and can strain your own hip flexors and lower back.
    • Gripping the shin instead of the ankle: This provides less control over the foot and can allow the opponent to escape the grip. It also puts your own wrist in a vulnerable position for a counter-sweep or escape.
    • Excessive downward pressure with arms: Squeezing the opponent's leg with your arms and chest without proper hip drive. This can damage your own shoulder joints and elbow ligaments from the strain, and is less effective than hip extension.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0%): Practice the hip rotation and leg isolation movement without a partner, focusing on the range of motion. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill - Static position (25%): With a compliant partner, assume the starting position and practice the grip and hip movement without resistance. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill - Controlled movement (50%): Partner offers minimal resistance as you execute the hip drive and knee pressure. Focus on maintaining control. (15 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill - Basic escape awareness (75%): Partner attempts a basic escape (e.g., turning into you). You adjust your body to maintain the lock or transition. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill - Dynamic movement (90%): Partner actively tries to defend and escape. Focus on maintaining the lock through their movements. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Attempt the Knee Bar during rolling, prioritizing control and safety over submission. Tap early if you feel pressure. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • From closed guard when the opponent postures up significantly, creating space to isolate a leg.
    • When the opponent attempts a guard pass and their weight is shifted, leaving a leg exposed.
    • From half guard when you can secure the opponent's leg and transition to a more dominant position or sweep.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Straightening the leg: The opponent extends their leg fully, making it difficult to apply torque or hyperextension. This is often combined with a hip escape.
    • Turning into the attacker: The opponent turns their body towards the attacker, releasing pressure on the knee joint and often creating an opportunity to escape or transition.
    • Bridging and hip escape: The opponent bridges their hips away and simultaneously hip escapes to create space and relieve pressure, often leading to a scramble or reversal.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to apply the Knee Bar?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you're not isolating the opponent's leg properly and are instead applying pressure to your own knee. Ensure you are perpendicular to your opponent's body and driving your hips forward. Avoid leaning your weight directly onto your own knee; the pressure should come from hip extension and controlled leverage on their leg.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, focus on precise body positioning and leverage rather than brute strength. Ensure you completely isolate their leg and maintain a tight grip on the ankle. Your hip drive is crucial; use your body weight effectively by getting perpendicular and driving your hips up and forward. Focus on controlling their hip to prevent them from turning out.

    Q: Grabbing the shin is a less effective grip for a Knee Bar and significantly increases your risk of injury. It provides less control over the foot, making escapes easier, and can put your own wrist in a vulnerable position. Prioritize reaching the ankle; if you can't, it's better to reset or attempt a different submission than to compromise your grip and safety.
