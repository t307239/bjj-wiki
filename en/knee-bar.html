Knee Bar Fundamentals for BJJ White Belts

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Knee Bar is a leg lock designed to hyperextend the opponent's knee joint. It is typically initiated from guard or when an opponent is postured up. White belts often struggle due to a lack of control and understanding of joint mechanics.

The key to a successful Knee Bar is isolating the opponent's leg and creating a fulcrum with your hips, while simultaneously preventing them from escaping.

This technique relies on generating leverage through hip extension and controlled pressure, not brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure opponent's leg: From closed guard, use your left arm to grip the opponent's right ankle, thumb-down, with your palm facing their shin. Your right arm will secure the thigh.
    2. Hip positioning: Shift your hips to your left by arching your back slightly, creating space to bring the opponent's leg across your body.
    3. Bring leg to chest: Pull the opponent's ankle towards your chest while simultaneously driving your right knee towards their hip.
    4. Secure the thigh: Use your right arm to grip the opponent's right thigh, just above the knee, with your palm facing the thigh.
    5. Hip bridge and extension: Lie back, lifting your hips off the mat. Extend your hips upwards towards the ceiling.
    6. Control the shin: With your left hand gripping the ankle, ensure your forearm is tight against the opponent's shin, preventing them from bending their knee.
    7. Apply pressure: Continue to extend your hips and push your chest towards the opponent's heel. Your right knee acts as a stabilizing point against their hip.
    8. Isolate the knee: Ensure your hips are positioned to create a fulcrum at the opponent's knee joint, perpendicular to their tibia.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly clamping the leg: A white belt might squeeze the opponent's leg with their knees too tightly, causing the opponent's own tibia to press into their knee joint, risking ligament damage (ACL/MCL).
    • Forcing hip extension too early: Attempting to bridge with hips fully extended before the leg is properly isolated can lead to the opponent's leg slipping, causing your own knee to be twisted or hyperextended.
    • Insufficient hip isolation: Not creating a strong fulcrum with your hips allows the opponent to straighten their leg, potentially hyperextending your own knee if you are not careful in your positioning.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the hip movement and leg isolation from closed guard, focusing on the sequence of grips and body positioning. 50 reps.
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from closed guard, focusing on smooth transitions and correct body mechanics. 25 reps per side.
    3. Partner drilling with light resistance (25%): Partner offers minimal resistance to leg isolation and hip movement. Focus on maintaining control. 20 reps per side.
    4. Partner drilling with moderate resistance (50%): Partner actively tries to defend the leg isolation and hip movement. Focus on maintaining grips and body position. 15 reps per side.
    5. Live rolling scenario (75%): Attempt the Knee Bar from closed guard during rolling. Focus on execution and recognizing opportunities, not finishing. 5 attempts.
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Execute the Knee Bar with full intent during rolling, prioritizing safety for both participants. Focus on proper tap-out signals. 15 minutes of rolling.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Opponent is postured up in your closed guard, leaving their leg accessible.
    • Opponent attempts a guard pass, exposing their leg to a straight ankle lock or transitioning into a Knee Bar.
    • During scrambles where you manage to isolate an opponent's leg.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Escape by walking your hips away: If the Knee Bar is being applied, the defender can try to walk their hips away from the attacker's hips, reducing the leverage.
    • Straighten the leg forcefully: A strong, immediate straightening of the leg can sometimes break the attacker's grip and escape the hold.
    • Tuck your heel and invert: Tucking your heel towards your glutes and inverting your body can create space and allow you to pull your leg out.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent's leg feel slippery and hard to grip for the Knee Bar?

    This often happens when your grips are not tight enough or your body positioning is off. Ensure your grip on the ankle is a firm, palm-to-shin grip with thumbs down. Also, make sure your hips are driving towards the opponent's hip as you pull their leg across, creating a fulcrum that prevents them from easily straightening their leg and slipping away.

    Q: How can I effectively apply a Knee Bar against a much larger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, brute strength is your enemy. Focus intensely on biomechanical leverage. Ensure you isolate their leg completely before attempting to bridge. Your hips must act as a precise fulcrum at their knee joint. Keep your core tight and use your entire body to extend, rather than just pushing with your legs.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to a Knee Bar from closed guard?

    The ideal time is when the opponent is postured up, creating space between your guards, and their leg is extended in a way that allows you to isolate it. Look for moments when they are reaching over your hips or trying to create distance, as this often presents an opportunity to secure their ankle and begin the leg isolation process.
