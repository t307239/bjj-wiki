The Knee Bar: A Precise BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Knee Bar is a powerful leg lock that targets the opponent's knee joint, typically initiated from guard or dominant positions. It achieves a submission by hyperextending the knee.

Many white belts fail by relying on brute force or poor body positioning, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Understanding the mechanics is crucial.

The key mechanical insight is creating a fulcrum with your hips and controlling the opponent's leg at the thigh and ankle, isolating the knee for leverage.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure an 'S' grip on the opponent's shin, with your thumbs pointing upwards and fingers interlaced. Your forearm should press against the opponent's hamstring.
    2. Position your hips perpendicular to the opponent's body, creating a right angle. Your hips should be close to their hip line.
    3. Bring your other leg over the opponent's thigh, hooking your heel just above their knee crease. This leg acts as a control point.
    4. Place your free hand on the opponent's ankle, securing a firm grip with your palm facing upwards and fingers pointing towards their toes.
    5. Drive your hips forward, pushing your pelvis into the opponent's thigh. This creates the initial pressure and leverage.
    6. Simultaneously, pull your opponent's ankle towards your chest with your free hand. This motion isolates the knee joint.
    7. Straighten your torso and lift your chest away from the mat, while maintaining hip drive. This increases the hyperextension force on the knee.
    8. Apply pressure by continuing to drive your hips forward and pulling the ankle. The opponent will tap when the knee is under stress.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly attempting to 'push' the opponent's leg with your feet: This can lead to knee valgus stress on your own knee, potentially tearing your ACL or MCL.
    • Grabbing the opponent's foot with both hands and pulling upwards: This shifts the pressure away from the knee joint, making the lock ineffective and risking shoulder or elbow injury from the awkward pull.
    • Not establishing a strong hip fulcrum: This allows the opponent to escape or relieve pressure, and can result in you falling onto your own knee, risking a bone bruise or meniscus tear.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and grip placement without a partner. Focus on hip angle and leg positioning. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Partner lies on their back, you practice the full sequence from a controlled position like closed guard. Focus on smooth transitions and grip accuracy. (20 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to your hip drive and ankle pull. Focus on feeling the correct leverage. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Controlled progression: Partner actively tries to defend but allows you to complete the submission. Focus on maintaining control and adjusting your body. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Submission chaining: Practice the Knee Bar from a position where it's naturally available (e.g., from a broken guard). Partner offers moderate defense. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Knee Bar against a resisting opponent, but with a clear understanding that it's a learning opportunity. Tap early if you feel pressure. (5 attempts per round, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • From closed guard when the opponent stacks you heavily, creating an opportunity to isolate a leg.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their weight distribution is compromised, leaving a leg vulnerable.
    • During scrambles or transitions where you secure a dominant leg entanglement position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Straightening the leg and bridging: The opponent can try to straighten their leg by pushing away with their hips and bridging their hips towards your head, relieving pressure.
    • 'Spiking' the leg: The opponent can try to drive their heel towards the mat, effectively pulling their knee out of extension.
    • Tucking the knee: The opponent can attempt to pull their heel towards their glutes, bending the knee and reducing the hyperextension.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar?

    Your knee might hurt because you are not isolating the joint correctly. If you are pulling with your arms or not creating a strong hip fulcrum, you are likely putting pressure on your own knee or not applying the correct hyperextension to your opponent's knee. Ensure your hips are driving forward and you are pulling the ankle towards your chest, with your body perpendicular to their leg.

    Q: How can I Knee Bar a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on securing a very tight 'S' grip on their shin and ensuring your hooking leg is placed precisely above their knee crease. Your hip drive needs to be explosive, and you must maintain a strong perpendicular body angle. Do not rely on strength; use your body weight and the mechanics of the joint to your advantage.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a Knee Bar from closed guard?

    The optimal time from Closed Guard is often when the opponent is posturing up and leaning forward, or when they attempt to 'stack' you. This posture naturally creates a separation between their hips and legs, making it easier to isolate a limb. Look for moments where their weight is forward and their knees are bent, creating the angle for a strong attack.
