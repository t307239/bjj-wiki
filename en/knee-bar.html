The Knee Bar is a submission targeting the knee joint, typically applied from positions like Closed Guard or side control. It aims to hyperextend the knee, forcing a tap.

White belts often struggle due to uncontrolled hip movement and fear of injuring themselves or their partner. They rush the setup, compromising the critical angles.

The key mechanical insight is creating a fulcrum with your hips and isolating the opponent's leg to apply isolated pressure directly to the knee joint, not the shin or ankle.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's ankle with a "grapevine" grip: both hands cupping the heel and Achilles tendon, fingers pointing towards the toes.
    2. Simultaneously, establish a "figure-four" grip around the opponent's thigh with your arms, crossing your arms behind their knee and gripping your own bicep or wrist.
    3. Drive your hips upwards and slightly to the side of the opponent's trapped leg, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and their leg.
    4. Extend your legs straight and forcefully, keeping your knees slightly bent to avoid hyperextension of your own joints.
    5. Arch your lower back and push your hips forward, away from your opponent's trapped leg.
    6. Ensure your chest is pressed against the opponent's thigh, preventing them from "stacking" you.
    7. Apply continuous, controlled pressure by extending your hips forward and upward, isolating the knee joint's hyperextension.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly gripping the opponent's foot: Gripping the shin or instep instead of the heel and Achilles tendon. This can lead to the opponent easily "kicking out" their leg and also risks hyperextending your own ankle if you try to compensate.
    • Bending your knees too much during the hip extension: This creates a "slack" in the submission, reducing its effectiveness and potentially allowing the opponent to escape. It also places undue stress on your own knee ligaments.
    • Allowing the opponent to "stack" you by not driving your hips forward: This negates the fulcrum effect and can lead to the opponent landing on top of you with full weight, potentially injuring your back or neck and making the submission impossible to finish.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and figure-four grip formation with no partner. 20 repetitions.
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): Partner lies on their back, you practice the setup and hip extension without pressure. 10 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner lightly resists the initial leg grab and hip positioning. Focus on maintaining grips and angles. 10 repetitions per side.
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner attempts to defend by pulling their leg out or posturing up. You focus on maintaining the figure-four and hip angle. 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Partner drilling (75% resistance): Partner actively defends with common escapes (e.g., bridging, pulling leg). You focus on finishing the mechanics. 3 repetitions per side.
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the Knee Bar from the specified position during rolling, with the partner defending realistically but not actively trying to injure. Tap immediately if caught. 5 attempts.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is in your closed guard and you have isolated one of their legs.
    • When you have achieved a strong X-guard or Single Leg X-guard position and can transition to the Knee Bar.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and you can secure their leg during the transition.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Bridging and rotating hips: If your opponent attempts the Knee Bar, immediately bridge your hips up and away from the trapped leg, rotating your body to face the opponent's head. This relieves pressure and allows you to stand or escape.
    • Pulling the leg out and "kicking": If the figure-four grip is not secure, the opponent can often pull their leg out by straightening it and driving their heel towards your hips. This requires a very tight initial grip.
    • "Stacking" the opponent: If the opponent fails to create a proper fulcrum with their hips, you can drive your weight forward and down, stacking them on top of you. This collapses their structure and makes the Knee Bar ineffective.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar from closed guard?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not isolating the pressure correctly. Instead of driving your hips forward to hyperextend the opponent's knee, you might be trying to pull their leg too hard or bending your own knees excessively. This causes your own knee joint to absorb the stress. Focus on creating a strong fulcrum with your hips and maintaining a straight line from your hips to the opponent's heel, applying pressure only to their knee.

    Q: How do I apply a Knee Bar against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and angles are paraMount. Focus on isolating their leg early, preventing them from using their weight advantage to "crush" you. Ensure your figure-four grip is extremely tight and you drive your hips forward with maximum force to create the hyperextension. Do not try to match their strength; instead, use your body's mechanics to create a powerful lever against their knee.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a Knee Bar from closed guard?

    The optimal time to attempt a Knee Bar from closed guard is when your opponent is postured up, with their weight distribution allowing you to isolate one of their legs. If they are too low and flat, it's harder to secure the leg and create the necessary hip angle. Look for opportunities when they are trying to "break" your guard or when they overcommit to a specific Guard Pass, leaving a limb exposed.
