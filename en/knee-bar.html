The Knee Bar: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Knee Bar is a leg lock targeting the opponent's knee joint, applied typically from positions like Closed Guard or side control.

White belts often fail by rushing the setup, neglecting hip control, or applying pressure incorrectly, leading to ineffective attempts or injury.

The key is isolating the leg and creating a strong fulcrum with your hips to hyperextend the knee joint biomechanically.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an opponent's leg by placing your feet on their hips and pulling them into your guard.
    2. Control the opponent's ankle with your near-side arm (e.g., right arm controls right ankle).
    3. Trap the opponent's thigh with your other leg (e.g., left leg over the opponent's right thigh).
    4. Swing your hips to the side, perpendicular to the opponent's body, creating a 90-degree angle.
    5. Place your free arm (e.g., left arm) under the opponent's knee, cupping the back of their thigh.
    6. Drive your hips forward and upward, creating a strong fulcrum point at the opponent's knee.
    7. Squeeze your knees together and pull your hips back to hyperextend the knee joint.
    8. Ensure your own knees are tucked in to protect them from the opponent's counter-pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying pressure by 'grinding' your hips: This can strain your own hip flexors and is biomechanically inefficient for hyperextension.
    • Failing to control the opponent's hips, allowing them to stack: This puts immense pressure on your spine and can lead to a fractured vertebra if they stack with full weight.
    • Grabbing the opponent's foot instead of the ankle and shin: This creates a weak grip and allows the opponent to easily escape, potentially twisting your own ankle if you don't maintain proper alignment.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip swing and leg trap motion without a partner, focusing on hip angle and leg placement (10 reps).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the full sequence from closed guard, focusing on grip and hip movement (5 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to leg isolation and hip control (10 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Partner actively tries to defend the isolation and hip control (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Knee Bar from closed guard, allowing partner to defend and escape, focusing on timing and setup (3 attempts per roll).
    6. Live rolling (full resistance): Integrate the Knee Bar as a natural submission opportunity within a roll, respecting tap signals (full resistance, 100%).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent tries to stack you from closed guard, presenting an isolated leg.
    • Opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg becomes available for isolation.
    • From side control, when you can isolate and control an opponent's leg.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape by turning your hips away from the pressure and bridging to relieve tension on the knee.
    • If the opponent's grip is loose, attempt to pull your leg out by creating space with your free leg.
    • If the opponent is not controlling your hips, attempt to stack them by driving your weight forward and down onto their upper body.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not isolating the opponent's leg correctly or are not driving your hips forward with the proper perpendicular angle. This causes you to apply pressure unevenly across your own knee joint or the opponent's knee, leading to strain rather than hyperextension. Ensure your hips are perpendicular to their body and you are driving forward to create a clean fulcrum.

    Q: How do I Knee Bar a bigger opponent when they are defending well?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on meticulous setup and leverage. Do not attempt to force the isolation; instead, use hip movement and precise grip changes to create openings. Once you have the leg trapped, swing your hips aggressively to the side to create the necessary angle, and then drive your hips forward to generate the hyperextension force. Prioritize their hip position over brute strength.

    Q: When should I transition from trying to sweep to attempting a Knee Bar from closed guard?

    You should consider transitioning to a Knee Bar when your opponent attempts to stack you or defend your sweep by posting their weight on their hands and knees. In these moments, their leg is often extended and vulnerable. Instead of continuing the sweep, use your feet on their hips to pull their leg closer, secure the ankle and thigh trap, and initiate the Knee Bar sequence before they can recover their base.
