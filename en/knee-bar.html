Mastering the Knee Bar in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Knee Bar is a powerful leg lock applied from various dominant positions, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's knee joint. It's a direct attack on a primary joint, requiring precision over brute force.

White belts often fail by rushing the setup or applying incorrect pressure, leading to defensive opportunities or injury. Understanding the sequence and body mechanics is paraMount for success and safety.

The core principle is to isolate the opponent's leg and use your body's leverage to create a fulcrum at their knee, forcing extension through controlled hip pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, break opponent's posture by gripping their collar with your left hand and the sleeve of the same arm with your right hand. Pull them forward and to your left.
    2. As they defend, swing your right leg over their head and secure your right shin across their chest, maintaining the collar and sleeve grips.
    3. Release the sleeve grip and place your right hand on the back of their head, while your left hand grips their right hip.
    4. Swing your left leg over their body, aiming to place it behind their right hip bone, ensuring your left shin is perpendicular to their thigh.
    5. Shift your hips to the right, away from their body, creating space. Your left knee should point towards their feet.
    6. Bring your right leg down, trapping their right thigh between your right hamstring and your right calf. Your right foot should be pressed against their hip.
    7. Squeeze your knees together and push your hips forward and slightly up, driving your weight into their knee joint to create hyperextension.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly trapping the leg: If your right leg is not securely locked around their thigh, their knee can rotate or bend in unintended ways, risking ACL or meniscus tears.
    • Pushing hips forward too early or with incorrect angle: This can cause the opponent's knee to buckle laterally, leading to ligament damage (MCL or LCL tears).
    • Releasing grips prematurely: Without maintaining control of their upper body and hips, the opponent can often escape the position or counter effectively, leaving your own limbs vulnerable.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of the leg entanglement and hip movement without a partner (10 reps each side).
    2. Partner drilling: Isolate the leg trap and hip movement with a compliant partner, focusing on the sequence (10 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    3. Adding upper body control: Practice breaking posture and establishing grips, then transitioning to the leg entanglement (10 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    4. Full technique execution from a static position: Start from the closed guard position, execute the entire Knee Bar setup and finish with a compliant partner (15 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    5. Controlled live drilling: Attempt the Knee Bar from closed guard against a resisting but not actively defending partner (5 attempts each side, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Knee Bar into sparring, attempting it when the opportunity arises naturally (ongoing, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent is postured up in your closed guard, offering a clear opportunity to break their posture and attack.
    • When you successfully sweep or transition to a dominant position where their leg is extended and accessible.
    • As a counter to guard passes where their leg is left isolated and vulnerable for a brief moment.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg straight/escape: If the opponent attempts to straighten their leg, immediately release pressure and transition to a different submission or sweep, avoiding hyperextension.
    • Tucking the knee: If the opponent can tuck their knee towards their own body, disrupting the lock, use this moment to switch to a sweep or another attack.
    • Rolling out/bridging: If the opponent attempts to roll or bridge to relieve pressure, be prepared to adjust your hips and maintain the lock, or disengage and reset.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to apply the Knee Bar in BJJ?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not creating the correct biomechanical fulcrum. Ensure your right leg's hamstring is locked around their thigh and your right foot is pressing into their hip. The pressure should be applied by driving your hips forward, not by twisting or pulling with your hands. This isolates the knee joint for extension.

    Q: How can I effectively apply a Knee Bar against a significantly bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is your primary weapon. Focus on breaking their posture to create angles. Once in the leg entanglement, ensure your shin is firmly across their thigh and your hips are positioned correctly. Drive your hips forward and slightly up, using your core strength to generate the extension, rather than relying on raw strength.

    Q: I keep getting my Knee Bar attempt countered. When is the best time to go for it?

    The most opportune moment to attempt a Knee Bar is when the opponent's knee is extended and relatively immobile, often when they are postured up in your guard or attempting to pass. Avoid going for it when their knee is bent or they have strong control of your hips. A clean break of posture and a solid leg trap are crucial for success.
