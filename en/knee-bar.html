Knee Bar Technique for BJJ White Belts

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Knee Bar is a powerful leg lock that targets the opponent's knee joint, typically applied from positions like Closed Guard or side control. It aims to hyperextend the knee, forcing a submission.

White belts often rush the setup or fail to control the opponent's hips and posture, leading to escapes or missed opportunities. Proper body mechanics are crucial for success and safety.

The key mechanical insight is isolating the opponent's leg and creating a fulcrum with your hips, using your body weight to drive the hyperextension.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an underhook on the opponent's right arm with your left arm, and establish a gable grip (both hands interlocked) around their left thigh, near the knee.
    2. Hip out to your left, bringing your right leg over the opponent's left hip, creating a 90-degree angle between your torso and their leg.
    3. Release the underhook and secure a grip on your own right shin with your right hand. Simultaneously, bring your left leg across the opponent's torso, pinning their hips.
    4. Drive your hips forward and upward, creating extension through the opponent's knee joint.
    5. Maintain a strong grip on your shin with your right hand, pulling your heel towards your glutes to increase pressure.
    6. Keep your chest pressed against the opponent's hip to prevent them from posturing up and relieving pressure.
    7. Apply pressure by extending your hips, not by pulling with your arms. The force should come from your core and hip drive.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure by pulling your opponent's heel towards their own hips: This places rotational stress on the knee, risking ACL or meniscus tears. The correct movement is to drive your hips forward and extend your own leg.
    • Not isolating the leg properly, allowing the opponent to turn their body: This can lead to the knee bending in an uncontrolled manner, causing ligament damage. Ensure your legs are securely wrapped around their hips and torso.
    • Gripping too high on the thigh, near the hip joint: This reduces leverage on the knee and makes it easier for the opponent to escape. Grip must be on the thigh, close to the knee joint itself.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip escape and leg wrap motion without a partner. Focus on the 90-degree angle and hip positioning. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from closed guard, focusing on grip acquisition and hip movement. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to work through the steps and feel the pressure. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend slightly, forcing you to adjust your body mechanics and maintain control. (5 reps per side)
    5. Live rolling (75% resistance): Attempt the Knee Bar in sparring, but with the understanding that the partner will defend vigorously but not with full intensity. (3 attempts per round)
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Execute the Knee Bar in full sparring, focusing on timing, setup, and correct biomechanics under pressure. (Focus on attempts within the round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating space to attack the leg.
    • When opponent attempts to pass your guard and you have control of one of their legs.
    • When you achieve a dominant position like side control and can isolate a leg.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Turn Away: If the Knee Bar is being applied, the opponent can hip escape away from the pressure and turn their body towards the attacking leg, relieving the extension.
    • Straighten the Leg and Bridge: The opponent can attempt to straighten their leg and bridge their hips upwards, creating space and reducing the angle of attack.
    • Guard Retention and Frame Creation: The best counter is proactive prevention. Maintaining strong frames and staying connected to the opponent's hips prevents the isolation required for the Knee Bar.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar, even if my opponent taps?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not isolating the opponent's leg correctly or you are using excessive force with your arms instead of driving with your hips. This can cause hyperextension or twisting of your own knee. Ensure you have a strong grip on your shin, drive your hips forward, and maintain a 90-degree angle between your torso and the opponent's attacked leg, creating a fulcrum around their knee.

    Q: How can I effectively Knee Bar a much larger and stronger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, your hip escape and leg isolation are paraMount. Focus on creating a significant 90-degree angle with your hips when you hip out. Ensure your legs are tightly wrapped around their hips and torso to prevent them from using their strength to post up or turn. Drive your hips forward with maximum extension, using your body weight to create the leverage, rather than trying to overpower them with your arms.

    Q: When is the right time to go for a Knee Bar from closed guard, and what are the common mistakes in timing?

    The ideal time to go for a Knee Bar from closed guard is when the opponent postures up, creating space between their hips and yours, or when they attempt to pass your guard and you can isolate one of their legs. Common timing mistakes include attempting it when they are too close and stacked, making isolation difficult, or when they have strong posture control, allowing them to easily defend or escape.
