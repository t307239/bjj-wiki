The Knee Bar is a leg lock that targets the opponent's knee joint, typically initiated from dominant positions like Side Control or the guard.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute force rather than precise leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The core concept is hyperextending the knee by isolating the shin and creating a fulcrum with your hips, making the opponent's joint the weakest point.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, achieve a strong cross-face grip with your right arm across the opponent's neck, and your left arm securing their left arm at the bicep.
    2. Shift your hips to the opponent's left, initiating a break of their posture by pulling their head and arm towards your chest.
    3. Untangle your legs, bringing your right leg over the opponent's head and your left leg to the outside of their left hip.
    4. Secure your left foot to your right shin, creating a 'figure four' grip around the opponent's left leg.
    5. Drive your hips forward and slightly upwards, ensuring your chest is firmly against the opponent's upper thigh.
    6. Contract your abdominal muscles and push your hips forward while simultaneously pulling your heels towards your glutes.
    7. Apply pressure by extending your hips and lifting your torso, creating hyperextension at the knee joint.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Leg Placement: A white belt might place their leg directly across the opponent's knee instead of isolating the shin. This leads to potential tearing of the collateral ligaments (MCL/LCL) as the knee is forced laterally.
    • Premature Hip Extension: Applying hip extension before securing the leg properly. This can cause the opponent to twist out, but if they don't, it can lead to ACL rupture by excessive rotational force on the knee.
    • Slipping Grip: A loose 'figure four' grip where the foot is not securely locked to the shin. This allows the opponent to escape, but if the pressure is applied prematurely, it can result in a twisting injury to the tibia and fibula.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Movement: Practice hip isolation and forward/backward movement without a partner, focusing on the hip driving action (10 reps).
    2. Figure Four Grip Practice: Practice securing the 'figure four' grip on a willing partner's leg from the guard, focusing on grip tightness (20 reps per leg).
    3. Controlled Hip Drive: Partner lies on their back, you secure the figure four and hip drive without locking the legs, focusing on hip extension and chest pressure (10 reps per side).
    4. Assisted Knee Bar: Partner cooperates by not resisting the leg lock mechanics, allowing you to practice the full sequence with light pressure (5 reps per side).
    5. Light Resistance Drill: Partner offers light resistance to the initial setup and escape attempts, focusing on maintaining control and completing the lock (5 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling (Controlled): Attempt the Knee Bar in live rolling scenarios, prioritizing control and safety, tapping early if the submission is threatened or uncomfortable (3 rounds, 75% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg is extended within your reach.
    • When you have established a strong closed guard and can isolate one of their legs.
    • During transitions where the opponent exposes their leg while attempting to posture up or escape.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Escape: The opponent immediately straightens their leg and drives their hips backward, creating space and breaking the lock.
    • Hip Rotation: The opponent rotates their hips away from the direction of pressure while simultaneously pulling their knee towards their chest, relieving tension.
    • Stretching Out: The opponent utilizes their free leg to push off your hips or chest, creating distance and breaking your base.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar from closed guard?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not isolating the opponent's shin correctly before applying pressure. Ensure your 'figure four' grip is tight around their calf and ankle, and that your hips are driving forward to create the fulcrum. If you're applying pressure before the leg is properly isolated, you risk hyperextending your own knee or causing a twisting injury.

    Q: How do I Knee Bar against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on breaking their posture and isolating their leg with absolute precision. Use your hips to create a strong base and drive forward, not just upward. Ensure your 'figure four' grip is extremely tight, securing their ankle and calf firmly. Don't rely on strength; rely on perfect biomechanical angles.

    Q: When should I give up on a Knee Bar attempt and switch to another technique?

    You should disengage from a Knee Bar attempt if the opponent successfully defends by creating significant space with their hips or by straightening their leg and driving away. If you are spending too long trying to force the submission and losing your dominant position, it's more effective to reset or transition to a sweep or another submission. Look for the opponent's reaction; if they are actively escaping and you can't maintain control, it's time to switch.
