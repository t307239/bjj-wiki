BJJ Knee Bar: A Precise Guide for White Belts

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Knee Bar is a leg lock targeting the opponent's knee joint. It's typically applied from dominant positions like Side Control or mount, or from guard when the opponent is postured up.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of joint mechanics and a tendency to over-rely on strength rather than leverage, leading to ineffective application and injury risk.

The key mechanical insight is isolating the femur and tibia, then creating a fulcrum with your hips while extending your own body to hyperextend the opponent's knee.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a grip: With your opponent on their back, place your arm around their ankle, securing a gable grip (interlocking fingers) around the shin, just above the ankle bone.
    2. Trap the leg: Your other leg will wrap around the opponent's thigh, high up, trapping their upper leg against your torso.
    3. Hip angle: Sit up and swing your hips out to your left (if attacking the right leg), creating a 90-degree angle between your torso and the opponent's leg.
    4. Control the knee: Ensure your arm is firmly controlling the opponent's shin, preventing them from rotating it away.
    5. Weight distribution: Lie back, posting your free foot on the opponent's hip for base, and lean your upper body away from the opponent's knee.
    6. Initiate extension: Squeeze your knees together tightly and lift your hips upwards towards the ceiling, extending your body.
    7. Apply pressure: As you extend, focus on pushing your hips forward and upward, creating a shearing force on the knee joint.
    8. Finish: Continue hip extension until the opponent taps.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure before the leg is properly isolated: This can lead to the opponent's knee buckling inwards or outwards, causing ligament tears (ACL/MCL) due to uncontrolled valgus or varus stress.
    • Gripping too low on the shin or not trapping the thigh: This allows the opponent to rotate their knee or escape the lock, risking a knee-on-stomach hyper-extension or a popped knee if they escape suddenly.
    • Using excessive upper body strength to 'pull' the leg: This ineffective method strains your own back and shoulders and fails to isolate the knee, potentially leading to the opponent escaping or you losing position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill (no resistance): Practice the hip movement and body positioning with a training partner lying still. Focus on the hip angle and leg trap. (50 reps each side)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Execute the entire sequence from the initial grip to hip extension, with your partner offering no resistance. Focus on precise body mechanics. (30 reps each side)
    3. Gentle resistance drill (25%): Partner offers slight resistance to hip movement and leg trapping. Focus on maintaining control and completing the hip extension. (20 reps each side)
    4. Controlled resistance drill (50%): Partner actively tries to defend by posturing up or pulling their leg, but without aggressive defense. Focus on maintaining the lock and completing the submission. (15 reps each side)
    5. Higher resistance drill (75%): Partner defends with more intention, attempting to escape or posture. Focus on maintaining control and finishing the submission while being aware of their movements. (10 reps each side)
    6. Live rolling (90%): Apply the Knee Bar in rolling situations against resisting opponents. Focus on recognizing opportunities and executing the technique with controlled commitment. (5-minute rounds, focus on the technique)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • From Closed Guard: When the opponent postures up and extends their legs, creating an opening to attack one leg.
    • From Side Control: When the opponent attempts to shrimp away or bridge, exposing their leg to a leg entanglement.
    • From Turtle Position: When the opponent tries to escape by turning away, allowing you to secure a leg.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Escape (Hitchhiker): If the opponent is slow to extend, drive your hips forward and use your free leg to swing your hips towards your opponent's head, then use your legs to push their hips away and escape.
    • Re-guarding: If the Knee Bar is not fully locked, use your free foot to push off their hip and quickly bring your legs back to guard, creating space.
    • Tapping: The most effective counter is to tap early and avoid injury.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Knee Bar?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are not properly isolating the opponent's knee joint. Ensure your arm is firmly controlling the shin and your legs are trapping the thigh high up. The pain you feel is likely your own knee compensating for a lack of leverage, or you are applying pressure incorrectly, stressing your own ligaments instead of the opponent's.

    Q: How can I Knee Bar a bigger opponent when they are much stronger than me?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on precise hip angle and trapping the leg high on the thigh to prevent them from using their size to simply stand up or pull out. Once the leg is trapped, use your body weight and hip extension, not brute strength, to create the hyperextension. Squeeze your knees together to maintain control.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a Knee Bar in BJJ?

    The best time to go for a Knee Bar is when the opponent's legs are extended and relatively close to your body, and you have established control over one of their legs. This often occurs when they posture up from your guard, attempt to pass your guard, or are in a vulnerable position like Turtle. Avoid attempting when their legs are far away or they have a strong base.
