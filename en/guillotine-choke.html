Guillotine Choke: The Essential BJJ Technique for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Guillotine Choke is a powerful submission that can be initiated from various positions, most commonly when your opponent is posturing up or attempting to pass your guard. It achieves a choke by compressing the carotid arteries.

Many white belts struggle because they rely on brute strength rather than proper leverage and body mechanics, often leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is to use your entire body, especially your hips and core, to create a constricting force, not just your arms.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, when opponent postures up with head within reach, secure a "high grip" by placing your thumb-side of your right forearm across the front of their neck, just above the Adam's apple.
    2. Simultaneously, reach your left hand over their right shoulder and grab your own right bicep. This is the "arm-in" variation.
    3. Arch your lower back and bring your hips forward, creating a tight connection.
    4. Drive your chest into their chest, preventing them from posturing further.
    5. Rotate your hips slightly to the left, turning your body into the choke.
    6. Engage your core and squeeze your biceps, drawing your elbows in and down.
    7. Maintain head control by keeping your chin tucked and your body tight against theirs.
    8. If they attempt to stack you, continue to drive your hips forward and slightly upwards to maintain leverage.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect grip placement too low on the neck can compress the trachea (windpipe) causing coughing and discomfort, rather than a choke. The correct placement is high on the neck, on the carotid arteries. Always aim for the side of the neck, not the front of the windpipe.
    • Over-extending your neck backwards when your opponent stacks you can strain your cervical spine. Instead, tuck your chin and drive your hips forward to absorb the pressure. Maintain a neutral spine.
    • Squeezing with arms only without hip drive results in a weak choke and can lead to elbow or wrist hyperextension. Use your hips and core to generate the choking force, keeping your arms in a strong, stable position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice securing the high grip and bicep grip on a dummy or pillow, focusing on the hip drive and chest connection. (20 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the grip sequence and hip movement from closed guard. Focus on body positioning. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner attempts to posture up slightly. You practice securing the choke and applying initial pressure. Partner taps early. (25% resistance, 10 reps per side)
    4. Controlled execution: Partner allows you to establish the choke from closed guard. Focus on the complete sequence, including hip drive and squeeze. Partner taps when choke is secure. (50% resistance, 10 reps per side)
    5. Simulated submission: Partner defends passively. You practice the mechanics of finishing the choke, emphasizing the squeeze and body alignment. Partner taps early. (75% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (guarded): During rolling, if the opportunity arises, attempt the guillotine from closed guard. If it's not there, transition smoothly. Focus on applying the mechanics learned. (90% resistance, 2 attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent postures up from your closed guard, exposing their neck.
    • When opponent attempts a guard pass and their head comes within range.
    • When opponent tries to stand up from your guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stack Pass": Opponent drives their weight forward, attempting to stack you. Counter by tucking your chin, driving your hips forward to maintain leverage, and potentially transitioning to a sweep or escape.
    • "Head Inside": Opponent manages to get their head to the inside of your choking arm. Counter by releasing your grip and switching to a different submission or guard.
    • "Defensive Posture": Opponent pulls their head back and creates space. Counter by maintaining tight control and trying to re-establish the choke or transition to another attack.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt after trying a Guillotine Choke?

    Neck pain often occurs if you're using your arms and neck muscles to squeeze excessively, or if your grip is too low on the neck, compressing the trachea. Ensure your grip is high on the sides of the neck, on the carotid arteries, and that you're using your hips and core to generate the choking force. Maintain a neutral neck alignment and tuck your chin to protect your cervical spine.

    Q: How can I Guillotine Choke a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your leverage is paraMount. Focus on a tight "arm-in" grip, ensuring your forearm is across their carotid artery and your bicep is secured by your other hand. Drive your hips forward aggressively to create a strong base and prevent them from posturing. Use your body weight and core to generate the choking pressure, rather than relying solely on arm strength. Keep your body glued to theirs.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a Guillotine Choke from guard?

    The most opportune moment to initiate a Guillotine Choke from guard is when your opponent is actively trying to pass your guard and their head is within your striking distance, or when they posture up to create space. Look for moments of imbalance or when they commit to a forward-moving pass. Avoid attempting it when they have strong control of your hips or are already in a dominant passing position.
