The Guillotine Choke: A White Belt's First Submission

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Guillotine Choke is typically initiated from a standing position or when an opponent attempts a takedown, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of the pressure points and relying on brute strength rather than proper leverage and body mechanics.

The key mechanical insight is creating a strong "frame" with your arms and using your hips to drive forward and compress the neck, not just squeezing with your arms.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent's head is trapped under your armpit, with their chin slightly elevated.
    2. Secure a "front headlock" grip: Your non-choking arm wraps around the back of their neck, palm facing your own face.
    3. Your choking arm slides under their chin, with your forearm pressing against their anterior neck.
    4. Clasp your hands together: Either a "palm-to-palm" grip or " Gable grip" (fingers interlocked) on the wrist of your choking arm.
    5. "Stack" your body: Drive your hips forward and slightly down, creating a posterior pelvic tilt.
    6. "Seatbelt" their head: Use your bicep and forearm of your choking arm to compress their carotid artery.
    7. "Hug" their head: Use your non-choking arm to pull their head into your chest, further compressing the neck.
    8. Maintain a narrow base and keep your weight centered over your hips, driving into them.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grabbing the back of the head with the choking arm: This position allows the opponent to "post" their head and resist effectively, risking strain in your shoulder and elbow joints as you pull against a rigid structure. The correct movement is to slide your forearm *under* the chin.
    • Squeezing with arms only without hip drive: This leads to inefficient pressure and potential bicep tears or shoulder impingement as you try to generate force solely with your upper body against a stronger opponent. The correct movement involves driving your hips forward and "stacking" your body weight.
    • Allowing the opponent to "post" their shoulder against your neck: This creates a "frame" for them, negating choke pressure and potentially causing neck strain or hyperextension if you pull too hard. The correct movement is to keep your choking arm tight and drive your hips to create the compression.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip and body positioning with no resistance. Focus on the hand placement and hip drive. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice securing the front headlock and applying the choke mechanics. Focus on the "stacking" and "hugging" motions. (25 reps, 10% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): The partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the choke pressure and refine your hip drive. (20 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): The partner attempts to defend lightly, forcing you to adjust your grips and weight distribution. (15 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Controlled rolling (guillotine focus): Announce you are attempting the guillotine. Your partner may resist but not actively try to escape. Focus on transitioning into the choke from common positions. (10 reps, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (opportunistic): Look for opportunities to apply the guillotine when the situation naturally arises. Your partner will defend fully. (Focus on application, not completion. 5 attempts, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When an opponent shoots for a double-leg takedown and their head is low.
    • When an opponent attempts to "pass" your guard by "stacking" you.
    • When you secure a "front headlock" position after a failed sweep or during scrambles.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "The Escape Hip": The opponent drives their hips *away* from your choking arm, creating space to remove their head. This involves a strong "base" and pushing their hips back with your legs.
    • "The Chin Tuck": The opponent tucks their chin tightly to their chest, making it difficult for your forearm to reach the carotid artery. This requires them to maintain a strong neck posture.
    • "The Step Around": The opponent steps their outside leg (relative to your choking arm) behind them and "steps around" your body, forcing you to release the grip or face a sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do a Guillotine Choke?

    Your neck might be hurting because you are pushing your head *into* your opponent's head or neck, creating a "crushing" effect rather than a "choking" effect. This can also happen if you are not properly tucking your chin and are instead exposing your own neck. Ensure your forearm is on their carotid artery and your head is driving *forward*, not directly into their skull, while maintaining your own chin tucked.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, brute strength is your enemy; leverage is your friend. Focus on getting your choking arm *under* their chin and establishing a tight grip. Drive your hips forward and "stack" your body weight onto them, forcing their head down. Don't try to pull them up; instead, use your body to compress their neck against their own shoulder and chest, creating the choke pressure with your frame and hip drive.

    Q: The Guillotine Choke is most effective when the opponent's head is trapped and their body is somewhat "squared up" or leaning into you, limiting their ability to "step around" or create space. This often occurs during takedown attempts, when an opponent is trying to "stack" your guard, or in scrambles where control of the head is momentarily established. Avoid attempting it when the opponent has significant "off-balancing" leverage or is already moving away from you.
