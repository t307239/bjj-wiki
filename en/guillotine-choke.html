The Guillotine Choke is primarily applied from the Closed Guard or when an opponent attempts to pass your guard. It aims to restrict blood flow to the brain by compressing the carotid arteries.

Many white belts struggle because they rely on brute strength rather than precise body mechanics, leading to ineffective chokes and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to create a fulcrum and your arms to drive into the neck, rather than just squeezing with your biceps.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a closed guard position with your legs wrapped around the opponent's waist, feet locked on the outside of their hips.
    2. As the opponent postures up or attempts to pass, reach your right arm across their neck, securing your bicep to the side of their neck, just below the jawline.
    3. Simultaneously, bring your left hand to grip your right wrist, forming a "figure-four" grip with your right arm inside.
    4. Tuck your chin to your chest, preventing them from posturing up further and protecting your own neck.
    5. Drop your hips slightly, creating a small gap between your hips and their hips, angling your torso slightly to your right.
    6. Drive your hips forward and up, using your legs to maintain the closed guard and prevent them from escaping.
    7. Squeeze your grip by pulling your right wrist towards your left shoulder, while simultaneously driving your chest into their throat. This creates a powerful compression on the carotid arteries.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to crank the neck by rotating your head excessively can damage the cervical spine ligaments and intervertebral discs. The correct movement is to drive your hips and pull your grip, not twist the head.
    • Overextending your elbows while trying to "pull harder" can cause ulnar nerve damage or medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow). Instead, focus on driving your hips and squeezing with your forearms.
    • Failing to maintain a tight closed guard allows the opponent to posture up, negating the choke and potentially injuring your own neck by being pulled into an awkward position. Keep your knees tight and your feet locked.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip setup and hip drive motion without a partner. Focus on the feeling of creating the fulcrum with your hips. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the full sequence of establishing the guard, securing the grip, and driving the hips. Focus on the exact body mechanics. (20 reps per side)
    3. Light resistance drilling: Your partner offers minimal resistance to your guard pass attempts. Focus on maintaining the guard and executing the choke mechanics smoothly. (15 reps per side)
    4. Controlled resistance drilling: Your partner actively tries to defend but allows you to finish the choke if applied correctly. Focus on recognizing opportunities and finishing the submission. (10 reps per side)
    5. Semi-live rolling: Roll with a partner where the focus is on practicing specific techniques. You can attempt the guillotine, and your partner can defend or escape. (5 minutes, 50% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Apply the guillotine choke during regular sparring. Focus on timing and execution under pressure. (10 minutes, 75-90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard from the closed guard and gets their head too low.
    • When the opponent is posturing up forcefully from your closed guard, exposing their neck.
    • When an opponent attempts a takedown and you can secure a front headlock position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape the head: If the opponent's head is not fully trapped, they can often simply turn their head away from the choke and escape. The correct defense is to ensure your arm is deep and your grip is secure.
    • Posture up and break the grip: A skilled opponent will try to posture up and break your grip by widening the angle between your arms or by creating space with their shoulders. You counter by driving your hips forward and maintaining a tight frame.
    • "Stack" pass: If the opponent is larger and can get their weight over you, they can attempt to "stack" you, which can relieve pressure. The counter is to maintain your hip angle and drive your hips into them, preventing the stack.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Guillotine Choke in BJJ?

    Wrist pain during a Guillotine Choke often occurs when you are over-gripping with your forearm or trying to "muscle" the choke instead of using your body. Ensure your grip is on your own wrist, not just your opponent's neck, and focus on driving your hips forward. This biomechanical action generates the power, reducing strain on your wrist.

    Q: How can I Guillotine Choke a bigger opponent effectively in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and body mechanics are crucial for the Guillotine Choke. Do not try to match their strength. Instead, focus on getting your hips underneath their center of gravity by dropping them slightly. Drive your hips forward and up, using your legs to maintain control, and ensure your grip is tight on your own wrist to maximize the choking pressure.

    Q: When is the best time to set up a Guillotine Choke from closed guard for a white belt?

    The optimal time for a white belt to attempt a Guillotine Choke from closed guard is when the opponent is actively trying to posture up or pass your guard. As they extend their neck forward to create space or drive through, this is your window to secure the grip and drive your hips. Avoid attempting it when they are already low and have their head tucked.
