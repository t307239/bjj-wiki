Mastering the Guillotine Choke in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Guillotine Choke is a powerful submission that can be initiated from various positions, most commonly when the opponent's head is trapped. It aims to constrict blood flow to the brain.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or incorrect body positioning, leading to ineffective chokes and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is creating a fulcrum with your forearm against the opponent's carotid artery while using your hips and core to generate leverage and pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a standing position or during a guard pass attempt, secure a grip around the opponent's neck with your dominant arm. Your forearm should press into the side of their neck, aiming for the carotid artery.
    2. Your other hand grips your own bicep of the choking arm, creating a "figure-four" or "S" grip. This locks the choke mechanism.
    3. Bring your hips forward, driving them into the opponent's torso. Your pelvis should be angled slightly towards the side of the choking arm.
    4. Lean your upper body back, creating tension in the choke. Keep your back straight and engage your core muscles.
    5. If in the opponent's guard, ensure your hips are heavy and your weight is distributed to prevent them from creating space.
    6. Use your legs to either secure a guard (closed or open) or maintain a strong base if standing.
    7. To finish, subtly lift your hips and arch your back, increasing the pressure on the carotid artery. Avoid excessive head cranking.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping the opponent's windpipe (trachea) instead of the carotid artery: This causes pain and coughing, not a submission, and can damage the trachea. The correct action is to ensure your forearm is positioned on the side of the neck, compressing the blood vessels.
    • Tucking your chin tightly and pulling upwards excessively: This can lead to neck strain or hyperextension of the cervical spine. Instead, maintain a neutral head position and use hip drive and core engagement to finish the choke.
    • Allowing the opponent to stack your weight by leaning too far forward without hip control: This can result in shoulder or back injuries. Maintain a strong base with your hips underneath you and your weight distributed downwards.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip setup and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the hip drive and shoulder engagement. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice securing the choke and driving your hips. Focus on feeling the pressure points. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Introduce slight pushing from the partner. Focus on maintaining the choke and hip position. (25% resistance, 15 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): The partner attempts to defend. Focus on holding the choke and adjusting your body to counter their movements. (50% resistance, 10 reps per side)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Guillotine Choke during rolling, focusing on correct setup and timing. Tap early if caught. (75% resistance, 5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (full intensity): Execute the Guillotine Choke as a primary submission option, applying full technique and awareness. (100% resistance, 10 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent shoots for a double-leg takedown and you can catch their head.
    • During a guard pass, when the opponent turtles or attempts to scramble up.
    • When you have established a strong closed guard and the opponent tries to posture up.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Stomp": If the opponent has a guillotine, step your foot on the same side as their choking arm flat on the mat. This disrupts their hip drive and creates space to escape. Immediately transition to a guard pass or a sweep.
    • The "Roll": If caught in a standing guillotine, the defender can initiate a forward roll to relieve pressure. This requires precise timing and body awareness to avoid injury. Upon completion, secure a dominant position.
    • The "Escape to Mount/Side Control": From the opponent's guard, if they have a guillotine, focus on breaking their grip by posting your elbow on their hip and creating space. Then, drive your hips down and secure a pass to mount or side control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do a Guillotine Choke in BJJ, even though I'm not getting a tap?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are applying pressure incorrectly. Instead of focusing on the carotid arteries on the sides of the neck, you might be compressing the windpipe (trachea) or hyperextending your own neck. Ensure your forearm is angled to cut off blood flow, not air, and use your hips to drive forward, not just your arms to pull.

    Q: How can a white belt effectively use the Guillotine Choke against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Instead of trying to match their strength, focus on the biomechanical principles. Secure a tight figure-four grip and drive your hips forward aggressively. Your goal is to use your body weight and the opponent's momentum against them, creating a tight choke without needing immense strength.

    Q: Is it better to pull up or push forward when finishing a Guillotine Choke in BJJ?

    The most effective finish for a Guillotine Choke involves a combination of hip drive and a slight backward lean. Pushing your hips forward into the opponent's torso creates the primary pressure, while leaning back locks in the choke. Pulling straight up often leads to a less effective choke and can put undue stress on your own neck and shoulders.
