The Smash Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling guard pass scenario, aiming to flatten an opponent to bypass their legs and secure Side Control.

White belts often struggle by focusing on brute strength and neglecting the fundamental weight distribution required for control.

The core principle is to use your shoulder and hips to drive down on one of their legs, breaking their base and creating an angle to pass.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on their left arm with your left hand (assuming you are passing from their left side).
    2. Step your left foot forward, placing it slightly outside their left hip, creating a 45-degree angle with their body.
    3. Lower your base by bending your knees, ensuring your hips are lower than their hips.
    4. Drive your right shoulder into their left hip, applying downward pressure to start collapsing their base.
    5. Simultaneously, use your left hand to pull their left sleeve towards your right hip, creating tension and controlling their arm.
    6. Shift your weight forward, driving your chest into their torso, while maintaining downward pressure on their hip with your shoulder.
    7. As their left leg collapses, step your right foot around their hips, securing a strong side control position with your hips low and chest tight.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Hyperextension: Attempting to "push off" with your back leg while your lead knee is collapsing can cause your lead knee to buckle outwards, leading to MCL or ACL tears.
    • Corrective Action: Keep both knees bent and under control, driving forward with your hips and chest, not pushing with your trailing leg.
    • Lumbar Spine Flexion: Arching your lower back excessively to "reach" for a grip or drive your shoulder can lead to disc compression and facet joint strain.
    • Corrective Action: Maintain a neutral spine, engaging your core and driving with your hips and leg drive, not your back.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Driving your shoulder directly into an opponent's hip with a straight arm and no rotational force can lead to rotator cuff impingement or labral tears.
    • Corrective Action: Rotate your torso slightly as you drive your shoulder into their hip, creating a "smashing" action rather than a direct impact.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling: Practice the footwork and hip movement without resistance. Focus on the 45-degree angle and hip lowering. (20 reps each side).
    2. Static Partner Drill (0% Resistance): Your partner lies on their back. Practice the grips and shoulder drive, focusing on sinking your hips and getting the 45-degree angle. (10 reps each side).
    3. Light Resistance Drill (25% Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure of collapsing their leg. Focus on the shoulder-to-hip connection. (15 reps each side).
    4. Controlled Pass Drill (50% Resistance): Partner actively tries to maintain their guard but does not actively counter. Focus on the full sequence of collapsing the leg and stepping around. (20 reps each side).
    5. Live Passing (75% Resistance): Partner attempts to defend and reset their guard. Focus on maintaining pressure and adapting your angles as they resist. (3-minute rounds, 3 rounds).
    6. Live Rolling (90-100% Resistance): Execute the Smash Pass in a sparring situation. Focus on applying the principles learned and adapting to their reactions. (5-minute rounds, 5 rounds).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is framing with their hands on your hips from closed guard and you can break one grip.
    • When your opponent attempts to establish a spider guard or lasso guard and you can isolate one of their arms.
    • When transitioning from a failed knee-slice pass attempt and their legs are still somewhat extended.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention/Reset: If the passer's shoulder pressure is too high, quickly bring your knees together and frame with your forearms to create space and re-establish guard.
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): As the passer drives their shoulder into your hip, actively shrimp your hips away to create an angle and prevent them from flattening you.
    • Leg Pummeling: If the passer attempts to smash one leg, actively pummel your free leg in between their hips and torso to create space and potentially re-guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my hip hurt when I try to Smash Pass against a closed guard?

    Your hip pain is likely due to your pelvis being too high and your torso too upright when you drive your shoulder. This creates a direct, shearing force on your hip joint. To fix this, ensure your hips are significantly lower than your opponent's before initiating the pass, and drive your shoulder *into* their hip while rotating your torso, not just pushing straight.

    Q: How can I Smash Pass a much bigger opponent who has strong grips?

    Against a larger opponent, brute force is your enemy. Focus on biomechanical leverage. Secure a strong cross-collar grip and use your far-side hand to control their opposite sleeve, pulling it across your body to break their posture. Then, sink your hips much lower than theirs and drive your shoulder into their hip with a rotational motion, aiming to off-balance them rather than push them over.

    Q: The Smash Pass is an excellent transition when your initial guard pass attempt stalls. For instance, if your knee-slice pass gets stuck because your opponent is framing well, you can use that same knee pressure to drive into their hip and transition to the Smash Pass. Look for opportunities where their legs are somewhat extended and you can isolate one side of their base.
