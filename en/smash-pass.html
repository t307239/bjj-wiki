The Smash Pass is a guard passing technique initiated from a standing or kneeling position against an opponent's Closed Guard. Its goal is to break the opponent's guard and establish side control or mount.

White belts often struggle because they lack precise control of their frames and weight, leading to ineffective pressure and easy guard retention.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and arm to drive their knee down and across your body, creating a superior hip angle to pass.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip opponent's collar with your right hand (if passing on their left side) and their left pant leg (shin area) with your left hand. Maintain a strong, deep grip on the collar, not just the lapel.
    2. Position your right knee directly outside their left hip, creating a strong base. Your left foot should be slightly behind your right knee for stability.
    3. Drive your right shoulder into their left hip, creating a wedge. Your head should be up and driving forward, not down.
    4. Begin to drive your right knee forward and slightly across their body, applying downward pressure on their left hip with your shoulder.
    5. Simultaneously, pull their left leg across your body with your left hand, preventing them from framing or bringing their knee back.
    6. Shift your weight forward, transferring your body weight over their hips and down towards the mat. Your hips should be lower than their hips.
    7. As their guard breaks, continue to drive your shoulder into their hip and extend your base by stepping your left foot forward, securing side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly driving your head down into the opponent's hip can cause neck strain or hyperextension. The head should remain up, driving forward like a wedge.
    • Grabbing the opponent's ankle instead of the shin/pant leg allows them to easily pull their leg free and retain guard. Always grip the pant leg or shin for control.
    • Failing to establish a strong base with your right knee outside their hip leaves you vulnerable to sweeps. Keep your knee firmly planted and driving forward.
    • Overextending your arm to grip the collar can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strain. Maintain a strong, controlled grip with your elbow tucked.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip transitions and hip drive motion without a partner. Focus on the feel of weight transfer. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): With a willing partner, practice the initial grips and the shoulder drive to break their guard. Partner offers no resistance. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner gently resists the initial guard break. Focus on maintaining your base and drive. (10 reps per side)
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to retain guard and frame. Focus on maintaining pressure and hip angle. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partner drilling (75% resistance): Partner actively defends and attempts to recover guard. Focus on securing the pass once the guard is broken. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Apply the Smash Pass in a controlled sparring situation. Focus on execution and timing. (5 attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Against a closed guard that is not fully locked or is starting to open.
    • When the opponent's hips are slightly angled away from you, creating a space to attack their leg.
    • As a response to the opponent trying to shrimp or create space to open their guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention (Frame and Re-guard): The opponent can stiff-arm your shoulder and hip with their forearm and elbow, creating a frame to prevent forward pressure, then bring their knee back across to re-establish guard.
    • Hip Escape and Sweep: If the passer commits too heavily and their base is compromised, the opponent can hip escape to create an angle and attempt a sweep, often by bringing their top leg over the passer's head.
    • Leg Pummeling and Counter-Pass: The opponent can attempt to pummel their trapped leg back through, or use the momentum of the smash to transition into their own guard pass or submission attempt.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to Smash Pass?

    Your shoulder pain likely stems from overextending your arm to grip the collar or from driving your shoulder into the opponent's hip without proper support. Ensure your elbow stays relatively tucked and use your body weight, not just arm strength, to drive. Maintain an upright posture and drive your head forward, not down, to protect your neck and shoulder.

    Q: How can I effectively Smash Pass against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on precision and leverage over brute strength. Use their size against them by driving your shoulder into their hip to disrupt their base and create an angle. Secure a deep collar grip to control their posture, preventing them from using their size to stack you. Prioritize breaking their legs apart with your knee and arm to negate their strength advantage.

    Q: When is the best time to initiate a Smash Pass from a standing position?

    From a standing position, the ideal time to initiate a Smash Pass is when you have established a strong base and control of at least one of their legs. Look for moments when they are leaning forward or when their guard is slightly open. Avoid initiating when they have a very tight, locked closed guard, as this will be too difficult to break effectively without proper positioning first.
