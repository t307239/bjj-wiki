The Smash Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanically Accurate Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Smash Pass typically starts from a Closed Guard or half guard, aiming to flatten the opponent and establish side control. It's a powerful pass that crushes their base and mobility.

White belts often struggle by using brute strength rather than leverage, leading to stalled passes and potential injury. They might also lack hip pressure and proper shoulder placement.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder to drive into their hip, creating an angle and breaking their structure, while your opposite arm controls their leg to prevent recovery.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish cross-collar grip with your right hand and cross-sleeve grip with your left hand.
    2. Keep your hips low and drive your left shoulder into the opponent's right hip, angling your body 45 degrees to their left.
    3. Simultaneously, apply downward pressure with your cross-collar grip, pulling their head towards your chest.
    4. Use your left hand to grip their right pant leg at the ankle, preventing them from posting it.
    5. Drive your left shoulder forward, creating a wedge between their hips and chest, flattening them onto their back.
    6. Shift your weight from your hips onto your chest and shoulders, maintaining downward pressure.
    7. Step your right leg back and to the side, creating a stable base, and begin to slide your left leg through their legs.
    8. As you slide your left leg through, continue driving your right shoulder into their hip and use your left hand to push their ankle away, creating space to pass.
    9. Secure a strong side control position by controlling their far arm and hip, ensuring your chest is heavy on them.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly driving your head into the opponent's hip can strain your neck and cervical spine. The force should be distributed through your shoulder and upper back.
    • Overextending your arm to grip the pant leg can lead to elbow hyperextension or a shoulder dislocation if the opponent pulls strongly.
    • Attempting to muscle through without proper hip angling and weight transfer can result in knee hyperextension or ankle sprains as you lose balance.
    • Not maintaining a strong cross-collar grip can allow the opponent to create space and regain guard, leaving you vulnerable.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip angle, shoulder drive, and leg pummeling motion without a partner. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner lies flat. Practice the grip setup, shoulder drive, and leg control to achieve side control. Focus on smooth transitions. (5 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and movement. Focus on driving through their hips. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled passing: Partner actively tries to prevent the pass but doesn't actively counter. Focus on maintaining pressure and correct mechanics. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Semi-live drilling: Partner attempts to recover guard or create space. You focus on maintaining the pass and establishing side control. (3 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Smash Pass into live sparring. Focus on applying the technique when the opportunity arises. (5 minutes, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent has a tight closed guard and is actively trying to keep you close.
    • When the opponent is attempting to open their guard or transition to a half guard.
    • When you have established a strong grip on their collar and sleeve, giving you control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard recovery: The opponent can attempt to bring their free leg back over your hips to re-establish closed guard. To counter, maintain shoulder pressure and immediately pummel your legs to create space.
    • Hip escape and shrimp: If the opponent flattens you, they can try to shrimp their hips away and bring their knees to their chest. To counter, keep your weight distributed forward and use your arms to control their hips.
    • Knee shield/Half guard entry: The opponent might use their leg to create a knee shield or transition to half guard. To counter, maintain strong chest-to-chest pressure and adjust your shoulder drive to their hip to prevent this transition.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try the Smash Pass?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are using it as a primary force generator instead of a fulcrum for leverage. When you drive your shoulder into their hip, ensure your spine is aligned and your weight is transferring through your hips and legs, not just your shoulder joint. Maintain a strong base and drive with your entire body.

    Q: How do I Smash Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage and precise angles are even more critical. Focus on getting your shoulder deep into their hip crease, almost like you're trying to hug their hip. Use their size against them by driving them to the side, making them unstable. Control their legs tightly to prevent them from using their mass to re-establish guard.

    Q: When is the best time to initiate the Smash Pass from closed guard?

    The ideal time to initiate the Smash Pass from closed guard is when the opponent's hips are slightly away from yours, creating a small gap. This usually happens when they are attempting to open their guard or transition. Look for moments of slight instability or when they are reaching for grips, as this is when their structure is most vulnerable to being collapsed.
