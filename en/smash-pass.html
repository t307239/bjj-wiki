The Smash Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position, aiming to break the opponent's Closed Guard and establish side control. It focuses on collapsing the guard structures, not necessarily on speed or brute strength.

Many white belts struggle by trying to force their way through, leading to stalled passes and potential injury. They often neglect fundamental weight distribution and frame creation.

The key insight is to use your body weight and limb positioning to create asymmetrical pressure, forcing the opponent to react and opening a path for your hips to advance.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grips: Secure a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left sleeve) with your left hand. Your opponent is in closed guard.
    2. Weight Transfer: Drive your left knee (same side as sleeve grip) forward, placing it slightly outside your opponent's hip line. Your right leg remains in a stable base.
    3. Hip Angle: Angle your hips to your left, creating a diagonal line from your right foot to your left knee. This shifts your center of gravity forward.
    4. Smash Action: With your collar grip, pull your opponent's head and upper body towards your left side. Simultaneously, with your sleeve grip, pull their arm forward and slightly across their body.
    5. Head Position: Drive your head down onto their chest or shoulder, creating a strong connection and preventing them from posturing up.
    6. Leg Drive: Extend your right leg backward, pushing off the mat to drive your hips forward and down.
    7. Guard Break: As their guard opens from the pressure and asymmetrical pull, drive your left knee further forward, aiming to get your shin across their hip.
    8. Transition: Immediately drive your hips down and forward, securing side control by placing your chest on their chest and your left knee on the mat alongside their hip.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Injury: Attempting to drive your knee directly into the opponent's hip socket without proper hip angling can cause a hyperextension or twisting injury to the knee ligament. The correct movement involves angling your hips first and driving the knee forward as a consequence, not the primary action.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Grabbing the collar and pulling with excessive shoulder rotation without body weight transfer can lead to rotator cuff strain or impingement. Focus on using your lats and core to pull, while simultaneously driving your body forward.
    • Wrist Sprain: Gripping the sleeve too tightly with an outstretched arm while the opponent resists can lead to a wrist sprain from the opposing force. Maintain a slightly bent elbow and use your entire arm and body to create the pulling pressure, not just finger strength.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip & Posture Drill: Practice securing collar and sleeve grips, angling hips, and driving head down without resistance. (5 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partnered Guard Collapse Drill: Partner maintains a loose closed guard. You practice the initial grip, hip angle, and asymmetrical pull to collapse their guard. Partner offers minimal resistance. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    3. Knee Drive and Hip Push Drill: From a collapsed guard, practice driving your knee across and pushing your hips forward. Partner remains passive. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Side Control Transition Drill: Execute the full Smash Pass sequence, focusing on breaking the guard and transitioning to side control. Partner offers light resistance to the pass. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled Pass Drill: Execute the Smash Pass against a resisting opponent who is actively trying to recompose guard. Focus on maintaining control and pressure. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling Simulation: Attempt the Smash Pass during positional sparring rounds, focusing on correct application and reacting to opponent's defenses. (2-minute rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent has a tight closed guard and is trying to create frames with their arms.
    • When the opponent's hips are slightly elevated, making it difficult to get under their legs for other passes.
    • When you have established good upper body control and can prevent them from posturing up.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Re-guarding: If the passer's hips fail to advance, the guarded player can attempt to bring their feet back to their hips to re-establish guard.
    • Shrimping and Re-guarding: If the passer commits too heavily and leaves space, the guarded player can shrimp their hips away and attempt to put their feet back on the hips.
    • Re-guarding with Leg Extension: If the passer drives their knee too far across, the guarded player can try to extend their leg to create space and re-guard, potentially transitioning to a half-guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I do the Smash Pass?

    Your wrist might hurt because you're relying on finger strength to grip the sleeve and pulling with a hyperextended or unstable wrist. To fix this, use your entire forearm and shoulder to generate pulling power, keeping your wrist in a neutral, stable position. Focus on a strong elbow bend and driving your body weight through the grip, not just pulling with your fingers.

    Q: How can I Smash Pass against a much bigger opponent who is using their weight effectively?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage and precision become paraMount. Instead of trying to muscle through, focus on creating a very sharp hip angle and driving your head into their chest to disrupt their base. Use your cross-collar grip to break their posture and pull their upper body offline, making their weight less of a factor. Aim to get your hips lower than theirs to create a pressure advantage.

    Q: When should I stop trying the Smash Pass and switch to another guard pass?

    You should switch if you've made multiple attempts and the opponent consistently defends by keeping their guard tight and preventing your hips from advancing. If they are successfully using their frames to keep you at bay or are transitioning to a stronger defensive position like Half Guard, it's time to disengage. Continue to make attempts, but be ready to transition to a knee-slice or leg-drag if the Smash Pass opportunity closes.
