The Hip Bump Sweep is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to displace your opponent's weight and create a sweep to your advantage.

White belts often struggle by trying to muscle the sweep, neglecting the crucial hip movement and weight transfer.

The core principle is using your hip's leverage to unbalance your opponent, not brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish two grips: left sleeve grip with your right hand, and left collar grip with your left hand. Maintain a tight closed guard posture.
    2. Shift your hips to your right, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and your opponent's torso.
    3. Place your left foot flat on the mat on your opponent's right hip, creating a base.
    4. Lift your right leg, placing your right foot on your opponent's left hip, keeping your knee bent.
    5. Simultaneously, drive your right hip upwards and towards your opponent's left side.
    6. As your right hip drives up, use your collar and sleeve grips to pull your opponent's upper body towards your right.
    7. Your left foot on their hip acts as a pivot point, while your upward hip drive creates the unbalancing force, leading to the sweep.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to lift your opponent with your arms alone: This strains the shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) and can lead to rotator cuff tears or dislocations by overextending the joint capsule.
    • Driving your hips straight up without angling: This places excessive shear force on the lumbar spine, potentially causing disc compression or muscle strains.
    • Letting your opponent stack you without creating space: This can result in knee hyperextension or ligament tears (ACL/MCL) if their weight forces your knee beyond its natural range of motion.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Bump Movement: Practice the hip elevation and angling motion without a partner, focusing on hip articulation. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partnered Gripping and Hip Angle: With a compliant partner, practice establishing grips and angling your hips. (20 reps)
    3. Partnered Hip Lift with Light Resistance: Execute the hip bump while the partner offers minimal, passive resistance. (30 reps)
    4. Partnered Hip Bump Sweep with 25% Resistance: Partner actively tries to resist but allows the sweep to occur. (40 reps)
    5. Partnered Hip Bump Sweep with 50% Resistance: Partner actively resists the sweep attempt, requiring more precise timing and hip drive. (50 reps)
    6. Live Rolling Integration (90% Resistance): Attempt the Hip Bump Sweep during sparring, focusing on correct mechanics over success. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard, leaning forward.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard by stacking you.
    • When your opponent is off-balance and leaning heavily to one side.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Pass Defense: If your opponent tries to stack, immediately widen your base and drive your knees towards their armpits to create space and prevent being compressed.
    • Hip Escape to Guard Recovery: If the sweep is failing, perform a hip escape (shrimp) to regain distance and re-establish your guard, avoiding a compromised position.
    • Toreando Pass Setup: If the opponent bases out well, use the momentum of their base to transition to a toreando guard pass by releasing the collar grip and attacking their leg.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try the Hip Bump Sweep?

    Your back is likely hurting because you are trying to lift your opponent with your upper body or by arching your back excessively. The Hip Bump Sweep is powered by the glutes and hip flexors. Ensure you are angling your hips to the side first, then driving your hip *upwards* and *towards* your opponent's side, not just straight up.

    Q: How can I Hip Bump Sweep a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is even more critical. Focus on creating a significant hip angle (at least 45 degrees) and ensuring your supporting foot is firmly planted on their hip to generate rotational force. You must also time your hip drive precisely as they lean forward, using their own weight against them.

    Q: When should I stop trying the Hip Bump Sweep and switch to another technique?

    You should consider switching if your opponent is consistently defending by maintaining a strong, upright base, or if they are successfully stacking you. If you feel your hips are stuck and you cannot generate the necessary angle or upward drive, it's better to disengage, shrimp out, and reset your guard rather than forcing a compromised sweep.
