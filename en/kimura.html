The Kimura: A Biomechanically Precise BJJ Technique Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock typically initiated from side control or Closed Guard, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's arm. White belts often struggle by using brute force instead of leverage. The key insight is to control the opponent's posture and create a strong mechanical advantage by isolating their arm and rotating it against its natural range of motion.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's right arm with your left arm wrapping their wrist. Your left forearm should be across their bicep, creating a "figure-four" grip with your right arm, which grips your own left wrist.
    2. Drive your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and your opponent's body. This breaks their posture and aligns their arm for the lock.
    3. Use your left leg to hook behind their right knee, preventing them from posturing up or turning into you.
    4. With your right hand gripping your left wrist, pull your opponent's arm towards your chest, keeping their elbow bent at approximately 90 degrees.
    5. Rotate your torso to your left, simultaneously lifting their elbow slightly and turning your hips. This motion creates a rotational force on the shoulder joint.
    6. Continue the rotation, aiming to bring their elbow higher than their shoulder and their hand towards their head. This isolates the shoulder joint.
    7. Apply upward pressure with your left forearm across their bicep while simultaneously driving your hips forward and up, forcing their shoulder into hyperextension. The pressure is applied to the humerus, rotating it externally and forcing abduction and external rotation at the glenohumeral joint.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the Kimura by "pulling hard" on the opponent's arm without establishing hip angle and posture control can lead to tearing the opponent's rotator cuff or biceps tendon, as it forces uncontrolled external rotation.
    • Attempting to "crank" the submission by jerking the opponent's arm upwards without proper isolation can cause a shoulder dislocation by forcing the humerus out of the glenoid cavity.
    • Over-extending your own elbow while attempting the grip can strain your elbow ligaments, especially if the opponent resists by pulling their arm away forcefully. Maintain a stable, controlled grip and focus on generating power from your hips and core.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the figure-four grip and hip movement without a partner. Focus on the hip angle and body positioning. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling with no resistance: Partner stands or sits passively. Execute the Kimura grip and hip movement, focusing on isolating the arm and creating the correct angle. (25 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling with light resistance: Partner offers slight resistance to posture and arm isolation. You practice overcoming this with hip pressure and controlled movements. (20 reps per side)
    4. Controlled drilling with positional control: Start from side control or closed guard. Partner allows you to establish the Kimura position and applies minimal defense. Focus on the submission mechanics. (15 reps per side)
    5. Semi-live drilling: Partner actively defends but allows you to work through the mechanics. If they defend effectively, reset. (10 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Kimura when the opportunity arises. Focus on recognizing the setup and applying the mechanics learned, not on forcing the submission. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When in closed guard and the opponent postures up, creating space to establish the figure-four grip.
    • When in side control and the opponent is trying to bridge or shrimp, allowing you to secure their arm.
    • When transitioning between positions and you catch an opponent's arm out of position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Tuck and Roll" Defense: If the opponent attempts the Kimura from closed guard, you can "tuck" your elbow to your ribs and "roll" your body forward, creating a stack and relieving pressure on your shoulder.
    • "Stair-Step" Escape: From side control, if the Kimura is being applied, step your leg over their head and create space by "stair-stepping" your hips away, breaking the angle.
    • "Arm Pull" Defense: If the opponent has a weak grip, you can attempt to "pull" your arm out by straightening it and driving your elbow towards the mat, creating a direct line of force to escape the figure-four grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Kimura?

    Your wrist might hurt because you are relying on wrist strength to finish the submission. The Kimura is a shoulder lock, not a Wrist Lock. Ensure your grip is a strong "figure-four" where your right hand grips your left wrist firmly. The power comes from driving your hips and rotating your torso, not from twisting your opponent's wrist.

    Q: How do I Kimura against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, biomechanical advantage is crucial. Focus on establishing a tight figure-four grip and driving your hips into their side to create a sharp angle. This breaks their posture and makes their larger frame less effective. Use your legs to control their hips and prevent them from posturing up or turning into you, isolating their arm effectively.

    Q: When is the best time to try a Kimura in BJJ?

    The Kimura is best attempted when you have established positional control, such as Side Control or closed guard, and your opponent is either posturing up or attempting to escape. Look for opportunities where their arm is extended or vulnerable, allowing you to secure the figure-four grip and begin the biomechanical sequence of the submission without giving them too much opportunity to defend.
