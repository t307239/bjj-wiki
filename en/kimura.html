The Kimura: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock typically initiated from guard or Side Control, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's glenohumeral joint.

White belts often struggle by muscling the submission or losing control of the opponent's posture, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key is isolating the opponent's arm and using your body to create a lever, not just brute strength, to apply pressure.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's right arm with your left arm wrapped around their bicep. Your left hand grips your own left shoulder.
    2. Your right hand reaches across and grips your opponent's right wrist, forming a "figure-four" grip with your left arm.
    3. Drive your hips forward and slightly to your left, creating a base with your left leg extended and your right knee tucked towards your opponent's hip.
    4. Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, bringing your opponent's elbow towards their navel.
    5. Keep your opponent's elbow bent at approximately 90 degrees throughout the movement.
    6. Lean back slightly, using your hips as a fulcrum, while simultaneously pushing their wrist away from their body.
    7. Apply controlled pressure by extending your left arm forward while pulling their wrist towards your left hip, creating internal rotation of their shoulder.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the Kimura by "pulling hard" on the opponent's wrist: This can cause a hyperextension or tear of the rotator cuff ligaments in the opponent's shoulder.
    • The correct action is to control the elbow and use hip movement to create the lever, not just wrist traction.
    • Failing to control the opponent's posture and allowing them to "stack" into you: This can lead to the opponent's weight crushing your chest and abdomen, risking rib fractures or discomfort.
    • Maintain a tight grip and use your legs to control distance, keeping their weight distributed.
    • Releasing the figure-four grip prematurely or too loosely: This allows the opponent to escape their arm, potentially leading to a scramble where your own arm could be vulnerable to a counter-submission like an armbar.
    • Maintain a secure figure-four grip until the submission is complete or you transition.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip setup and hip movement without a partner, focusing on the "figure-four" and hip drive. (20 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a willing partner, practice the full sequence of grips and body mechanics, focusing on smooth transitions. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the setup and pressure application. Focus on maintaining grips. (10 reps)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance - 50%): Partner actively defends slightly, forcing you to adjust your mechanics and maintain control. (10 reps)
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Partner attempts to escape the submission, requiring you to use precise biomechanics to maintain the lock. (10 reps)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Attempt the Kimura from the specified position during rolling, focusing on the setup and application under full resistance. (3 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • From closed guard when the opponent postures up and extends an arm towards you.
    • From side control when the opponent attempts to frame or bridge, exposing an arm.
    • From half guard when the opponent attempts to pass by dropping their weight heavily on your chest.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Elbow escape: If the opponent is applying too much pressure, tuck your elbow tightly to your ribs and drive your hips away to create space.
    • "Shrimping" to create distance: If you feel the submission being locked in, use your hips to push away and create space to disengage or transition.
    • Turning into the submission: If the opponent is attempting to extend your arm, turn your body towards their hips to relieve pressure and potentially escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do a Kimura?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you're using excessive wrist or arm strength to force the lock, rather than using your hips and body to create a proper lever. This puts undue stress on the glenohumeral joint and rotator cuff. Focus on isolating the opponent's elbow and rotating your torso while driving your hips forward to create the biomechanical advantage.

    Q: How do I finish a Kimura against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on controlling their elbow and preventing them from posturing up. Use your legs to create a strong base and keep their weight from crushing you. Your hip drive and torso rotation become even more critical to generate the necessary leverage. Think of your body as a winch, using your entire frame to apply pressure.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Kimura from closed guard?

    The optimal time to attempt a Kimura from Closed Guard is when your opponent postures up and extends one of their arms towards you, either to try and break your guard or to create space. This posture gives you the opportunity to isolate their arm and control their elbow before they can fully retract it or defend effectively. A common mistake is trying it when they are already low and compact.
