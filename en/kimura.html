The Kimura: A White Belt's Biomechanically Sound Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock typically initiated from guard or Side Control, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's shoulder joint.

White belts often rush, neglecting the subtle control needed, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key is creating a strong frame with your legs and controlling the opponent's posture to isolate their arm effectively.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a figure-four grip on the opponent's arm: your right hand grips your left wrist, and your left hand grips your right forearm.
    2. Secure the opponent's wrist with your left hand, palm facing you, thumb pointing upwards.
    3. Drive your hips forward and to your left, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and the opponent's torso.
    4. Use your legs to maintain closed guard, preventing the opponent from posturing up or creating space.
    5. With your right arm, reach *under* the opponent's armpit and secure your left wrist, forming the figure-four grip.
    6. Begin to lift the opponent's elbow upwards while simultaneously pulling their wrist towards their own sternum.
    7. Rotate your hips to your right, simultaneously extending your legs and pulling the opponent's arm across their body, applying pressure to the shoulder joint.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'pull hard' with your arms alone: This can lead to biceps tears or shoulder impingement by hyperextending your own or the opponent's shoulder in an uncontrolled manner.
    • Allowing the opponent to 'bridge' into you without controlling their posture: This can cause a hyperextension injury to your own shoulder or a spinal injury if their weight is not managed.
    • Gripping your own forearm instead of your wrist: This reduces leverage and can lead to wrist or elbow hyperextension injuries due to poor biomechanical alignment.
    • Not rotating your hips sufficiently: This results in a weak application of the lock, often leading to shoulder strain or the opponent escaping due to insufficient rotational force.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the figure-four grip and arm isolation motion without a partner (5 reps each side).
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice establishing the grip and moving your hips to the 45-degree angle (10 reps each side).
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner resists slightly by trying to pull their arm out, focus on maintaining the grip and hip angle (10 reps each side).
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend, focus on maintaining control and the correct arm path, but do not apply submission pressure (15 reps each side).
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner defends with moderate resistance, practice finishing the lock slowly, stopping immediately if tap occurs (15 reps each side).
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Apply the Kimura in rolling situations, focusing on timing and correct execution, but prioritize safety and avoid over-applying pressure (5 attempts per roll).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is in your closed guard and you can isolate one of their arms.
    • When you achieve side control and can establish a strong cross-face and grip on their arm.
    • When transitioning from a failed submission attempt and you can secure their arm.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stacking Pass Defense: If the opponent attempts to stack you, immediately drive your hips forward and try to create a frame with your forearm against their chest, preventing them from collapsing onto you.
    • Arm Release: If the opponent is applying pressure, try to rotate your hand *away* from your body, creating space to pull your arm out and re-establish guard.
    • Bridging to Escape: If the opponent has a loose grip, bridge your hips sharply and try to roll them in the direction of their trapped arm to relieve pressure and escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do the Kimura from closed guard?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are not controlling the opponent's posture effectively, allowing them to 'stack' you. This means their weight is collapsing onto your shoulder, not the intended joint lock. To fix this, focus on keeping your hips active and driving them *forward* and to the side, creating a strong base and isolating their arm before applying pressure.

    Q: How do I apply the Kimura against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paraMount. Instead of trying to muscle it, focus on using your legs to control their posture and hips, preventing them from using their weight advantage. Ensure your figure-four grip is tight, and use your hip rotation to create the torque. The goal is to make their arm do the work, not your strength.

    Q: When should I transition to a Kimura instead of trying to sweep from closed guard?

    You should consider transitioning to a Kimura when your opponent is defending your sweeps by posturing up or creating a strong base, leaving one of their arms accessible. If they are too heavy or strong to sweep effectively, isolating an arm for a submission like the Kimura becomes a more efficient way to score points or achieve a finish.
