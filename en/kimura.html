The Kimura: A Biomechanically Accurate BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock typically initiated from a Closed Guard or side control position, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's shoulder joint.

White belts often rush the setup or fail to control the opponent's posture, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key is isolating the arm and creating a strong mechanical advantage by leveraging your hips and body weight, not just brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure the opponent's arm by placing your left shin across their chest and your right shin over their left shoulder, creating a "figure-four" grip around their arm with your own arms.
    2. Your left hand grips your own right wrist, forming the Kimura grip. Ensure your left forearm presses into the opponent's bicep, controlling their upper arm.
    3. Shift your hips to your right, so your body is at approximately a 45-degree angle to the opponent's torso. This creates lateral tension.
    4. Drive your left shoulder into the opponent's left shoulder, preventing them from posturing up or turning into you. Maintain head control by placing your head on their chest or shoulder.
    5. Begin to "walk" your hips away from the opponent's trapped arm. This movement extends the shoulder joint.
    6. Simultaneously, rotate your hips and torso counter-clockwise, pulling the opponent's elbow towards their hips while pushing their wrist towards their head.
    7. As you continue the hip rotation and extension, apply upward pressure with your left wrist and downward pressure with your right hand on their elbow, increasing the torque on the shoulder.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying pressure by "pulling hard" with your arms: This can cause the opponent's rotator cuff to tear as their arm is forced into an unnatural external rotation and extension.
    • Allowing the opponent to turn into you: If you don't maintain shoulder-to-shoulder pressure with your left shoulder, they can relieve the pressure by turning, potentially injuring your own shoulder or elbow if you resist improperly.
    • Rushing the setup and not establishing a strong figure-four grip: This allows the opponent to escape the hold before it's fully secured, potentially leading to them falling onto your arm with significant force, risking elbow hyperextension.
    • Over-extending your own elbow while trying to finish: Aggressively straightening your arms to finish the lock can lead to elbow joint strain or ligament damage in your own arm.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice establishing the figure-four grip and hip movement without a partner. Focus on the body rotation and weight shift. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    2. Partnered drilling (no resistance): Practice the full sequence with a compliant partner, focusing on precise movements and grip establishment. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Light resistance drilling: Partner offers minimal resistance to your setup and execution, allowing you to feel the correct body mechanics. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled resistance drilling: Partner actively tries to defend but does not aggressively resist. Focus on maintaining control and finishing the lock. (20 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Dynamic drilling: Partner begins to actively defend and attempt to escape, but the drill stops if a submission is achieved or the position is lost. (15 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (specific focus): Practice attempting the Kimura from the specified positions during rolling, but only against partners who are comfortable tapping. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From closed guard when the opponent postures up and reaches over your body with one arm.
    • From side control when the opponent attempts to frame or shrimp away, leaving an arm exposed.
    • When the opponent tries to pass your guard and their arm is trapped between your legs.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Escape by turning your elbow inward and "humping" your hips forward towards the opponent's head, relieving pressure.
    • "Stacking" your weight onto the opponent by driving your shoulders and hips into them, collapsing their base and reducing leverage.
    • If the opponent secures the grip, try to "frame" with your free hand between their arms to create space and prevent the figure-four lock from fully forming.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Kimura from closed guard?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are not establishing a proper "figure-four" grip. Instead of gripping your own wrist, you might be gripping your opponent's arm directly or using an insecure grip. Ensure your left hand firmly grips your own right wrist, with your left forearm pressing into the opponent's bicep. This transfers the force to your shoulder and back, not just your wrist.

    Q: How do I apply the Kimura against a much bigger and stronger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body mechanics are crucial. Focus on establishing a tight figure-four grip and then aggressively shifting your hips to a 45-degree angle. Use your body weight by rotating your hips and torso, driving your left shoulder into their shoulder to maintain posture control. The key is to create a mechanical advantage by extending their shoulder, not overpowering them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Kimura from closed guard, and how do I avoid giving up my back?

    The ideal time is when the opponent postures up and reaches to control your head or chest, exposing an arm. To avoid giving up your back, always maintain head control by placing your head on their chest or shoulder. As you initiate the hip shift and rotation for the Kimura, keep your left shoulder firmly pressed into their shoulder, preventing them from turning into you and taking your back.
