Kimura: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock typically initiated from Closed Guard or side control, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's shoulder joint.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of control, relying on brute strength instead of leverage, leading to missed submissions and potential injury.

The key insight is to isolate the opponent's arm and create a figure-four grip to leverage their body against their own shoulder, minimizing your effort.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's right arm across their chest with your left arm, establishing a "figure-four" grip: your left hand grips your left wrist, and your left forearm is behind their upper arm.
    2. Extend your legs, creating space to bring your hips slightly forward and to your right, angling your body approximately 45 degrees to your opponent's left.
    3. Use your right leg to hook around your opponent's left hip, preventing them from posturing up and creating a stable base.
    4. Shift your weight forward, driving your chest towards your opponent's chest, compressing them slightly and creating tension on the trapped arm.
    5. Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, simultaneously pulling your opponent's elbow towards your chest while pushing their wrist away from their body.
    6. Continue this rotation and pulling motion, applying pressure directly against the shoulder joint's anterior capsule.
    7. The submission is achieved when the opponent verbally submits or you feel their shoulder reach its anatomical limit of external rotation and abduction.
    8. Maintain your hip and base connection throughout the entire movement to prevent escapes and maintain control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grabbing the opponent's bicep with your forearm instead of their wrist: This often results in the opponent "stacking" you by driving their weight forward, potentially causing anterior shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strain in your own shoulder if you resist incorrectly.
    • Pulling the arm straight up towards the ceiling: This misapplies the force and can lead to a biceps tear or shoulder dislocation for the opponent, and strains your own back from poor leverage.
    • Not securing the figure-four grip tightly: This allows the opponent to pull their arm out, leading to a loss of position and potentially exposing you to sweeps or other attacks, while you have wasted energy.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice securing the figure-four grip and performing the hip hook motion with no opponent. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Practice the entire sequence from closed guard to submission, focusing on smooth transitions and correct body positioning. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Opponent attempts to posture up or defend minimally. Focus on maintaining hip control and applying the rotation. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Opponent actively tries to escape by posturing, creating frames, or turning. Focus on maintaining tightness and adjusting your angle. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt Kimura from closed guard during rolling, but tap early if the submission is not clean or if you feel a dangerous position. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full): Look for opportunities to apply the Kimura during rolling, focusing on proper execution and safety for both partners. (10 minutes, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have your opponent in closed guard and they are postured up, presenting an arm.
    • When you have secured side control and are able to isolate one of their arms.
    • During transitions, if you catch an arm and can quickly establish the figure-four grip.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Arm Isolation: If the opponent attempts the Kimura, immediately bring your elbow to your ribs and try to pull your arm out, creating space to defend.
    • Stack Defense: If the opponent tries to stack you, drive your hips into them and try to bring your legs back to a closed guard position.
    • Rolling Escape: If the opponent has secured the grip and is applying pressure, attempt to roll towards the side of the trapped arm to relieve pressure and potentially escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Kimura in BJJ?

    Your wrist might be hurting because you are not correctly securing the figure-four grip. Ensure your gripping hand is on your own wrist, not your opponent's wrist directly, and that your forearm is snug behind their upper arm. This alignment distributes pressure to the shoulder joint, not your wrist.

    Q: How can a smaller white belt successfully apply a Kimura against a bigger opponent?

    Leverage is key for smaller practitioners. Focus on using your hips and core to generate rotational power, rather than trying to overpower them. Securing a tight figure-four grip and using your body weight to drive into their chest will amplify your force, allowing you to manipulate their larger frame.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Kimura from closed guard, and what if my opponent just pulls their arm back?

    The optimal time is when your opponent is postured up and their arm is extended. If they pull their arm back, don't chase it with just your arm. Instead, adjust your hips, maintain your leg control, and try to re-establish the arm isolation by moving your body around their arm.
