The Kimura: A Biomechanically Sound Guide for BJJ White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock, typically applied from Closed Guard or side control, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's shoulder joint. White belts often struggle due to a lack of control and an over-reliance on brute strength, leading to ineffective attempts.

The key mechanical insight is creating a stable base and using your body's leverage, not just arm strength, to control the opponent's posture and isolate the arm for the lock.

Mastering the Kimura requires understanding leverage and control, not just a strong grip.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's arm by establishing a "figure-four" grip: your right hand grips your opponent's right wrist (palm facing you), and your left arm wraps around their bicep to grip your own right wrist.
    2. Shift your hips to your right, creating a slight angle between your torso and your opponent's. This off-angles their shoulder.
    3. Maintain pressure with your closed guard to prevent your opponent from posturing up or creating space. Keep your knees tight.
    4. Begin to "walk" your hips away from the trapped arm, while simultaneously pulling the opponent's elbow slightly towards your chest. This stretches their shoulder.
    5. As you continue to walk your hips and apply pressure, begin to elevate your own hips upward. This upward motion creates the rotational force on the shoulder.
    6. Simultaneously, apply downward pressure on the opponent's wrist (held by your right hand) and a slight upward push with your chest against their bicep (held by your left arm).
    7. The final phase involves extending your hips and torso upwards while maintaining the figure-four grip and wrist control, creating external rotation and hyperextension at the glenohumeral joint.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the Kimura by "pulling hard" with only arm strength: This can lead to elbow hyperextension or tearing of the rotator cuff ligaments in your own arm if the opponent resists incorrectly. The correct movement involves using hip elevation and body torque.
    • Not controlling the opponent's posture with the legs: If the opponent postures up, their shoulder is not properly isolated, and attempting to force the Kimura can strain your own shoulder or elbow. Maintain a tight closed guard to control their base.
    • Releasing the figure-four grip too early or loosely: This allows the opponent to escape the lock or counter. Ensure a secure grip throughout the entire movement, from setup to submission.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip Practice: Practice forming the figure-four grip and maintaining it without a partner. Focus on the feel of the grip. (50 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Positional Setup Drill: From closed guard, practice isolating one arm and forming the figure-four grip. Focus on hip angle and maintaining guard. (25 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    3. Controlled Movement Drill: With a partner providing minimal resistance (e.g., just holding their arm), practice the hip walk and body angle shift. Focus on the feeling of stretching the shoulder. (20 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Elevator Drill: Practice the hip elevation and slight upward push while maintaining the grip. Partner maintains a neutral posture. (20 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Submission Execution Drill: Execute the full Kimura motion with a partner who understands to "tap" early if they feel discomfort, allowing you to feel the correct pressure. Focus on the synchronized hip and arm movement. (15 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling Application: Attempt the Kimura from closed guard during light rolling. Focus on proper setup and execution, prioritizing control over speed. (5-minute rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • From closed guard when the opponent tries to posture up or stack you, creating an opportunity to isolate an arm.
    • From side control when you have established good control and can isolate an opponent's arm while preventing them from bridging.
    • When an opponent attempts a guard pass that leaves an arm exposed, and you can quickly secure the figure-four grip.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Escape to Guard" Counter: If you are on the receiving end, immediately try to bring your free arm across your body to defend your face and neck, then use your hips to "bridge" towards the trapped arm, creating space to untangle. This requires a strong, quick hip escape.
    • The "Roll Through" Counter: The opponent attempts to roll you in the direction of the Kimura. Instead of resisting the roll, go with it, using your momentum to roll in the same direction, which can often release the pressure or lead to a scramble.
    • The "Stack and Pass" Counter: If the opponent is not applying sufficient pressure, you can attempt to "stack" them by driving your weight forward onto their shoulders, forcing them to release the grip or creating an opening to pass their guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do a Kimura from closed guard?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are relying too much on raw arm strength and not enough on hip leverage and body mechanics. This can cause your rotator cuff to strain or your glenohumeral joint to be hyperextended. Ensure you are walking your hips away, creating an angle, and elevating your hips to create the rotational force, rather than just pulling.

    Q: How can I do a Kimura against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are even more critical. Focus on preventing them from posturing up by keeping your guard tight. When you secure the figure-four grip, actively walk your hips away to create a significant angle, isolating their shoulder. Then, use your entire body's weight and upward hip drive to create the submission, rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: I can't seem to finish the Kimura; my opponent just defends it. What am I missing biomechanically?

    You are likely missing the crucial hip elevation and torso extension component. The Kimura is not just a Wrist Lock; it's a shoulder lock initiated by rotating the opponent's arm. Ensure that as you walk your hips away, you are simultaneously driving your hips UPWARD and slightly forward, while keeping their wrist controlled. This upward hip movement is what creates the painful external rotation at the shoulder.
