The Kimura is a powerful shoulder lock applied from various positions, most commonly Closed Guard. It aims to hyperextend the opponent's shoulder joint, forcing a tap.

White belts often struggle by relying on raw strength or incorrect leverage. They may try to "muscle" the lock, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key is isolating the opponent's arm and controlling their body's rotation, using your hips and core to generate the torque, not just your arms.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's arm: grip their wrist with your left hand (if attacking their right arm).
    2. Wrap your right arm over their biceps, creating a "figure-four" grip by grabbing your own right wrist with your left hand.
    3. Maintain your closed guard by squeezing your legs, preventing your opponent from posturing up or passing.
    4. "Walk" your hips to your left, creating a slight angle away from your opponent's attacked arm.
    5. Keep your opponent's elbow bent to approximately 90 degrees, with their forearm across their own chest.
    6. Begin to rotate your hips back towards your right, creating a pulling motion with your figure-four grip.
    7. As you rotate, slightly lift your hips off the mat, driving your opponent's elbow upwards and outwards, applying pressure to the shoulder joint.
    8. Maintain constant pressure by keeping your opponent's wrist close to their own head, preventing them from "hiding" their arm.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Arm trapping error: Attempting to pull the opponent's arm across their body without controlling their shoulder rotation. This can lead to the opponent's rotator cuff tearing or a biceps tendon rupture.
    • Hip angle error: Failing to "walk" your hips to the side, leading to a direct "stacking" pressure. This can cause spinal compression or a facet joint injury in the opponent's back.
    • Grip failure: Gripping too loosely on the figure-four or wrist, allowing the opponent to escape the lock before it's applied. This often results in the practitioner's own shoulder being put at risk when the opponent counters.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo grip practice: Rep 100. Practice the figure-four grip and wrist grip with no partner, focusing on the feel and tightness.
    2. Partnered static drill: Rep 50 per side. With a compliant partner in your closed guard, practice establishing the grips and the initial hip walk without applying pressure.
    3. Controlled movement drill: Rep 25 per side. Partner resists slightly as you practice the hip walk and initial rotation, stopping before any pressure is applied. Focus on body positioning.
    4. Partial resistance drill: Rep 20 per side. Partner offers light resistance to the hip walk and rotation. Focus on maintaining control and finding the correct angles.
    5. Controlled submission drill: Rep 15 per side. Apply the Kimura with controlled pressure, stopping immediately upon your partner's tap. Focus on smooth execution and understanding the submission point.
    6. Live rolling (controlled): 3 rounds. Attempt the Kimura from closed guard when opportunities arise, but focus on maintaining good technique and understanding when to disengage if the opportunity isn't perfect. (Resistance: 75%)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard and you can isolate one of their arms.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and you can trap an arm during the transition.
    • From side control, when you can secure an "americana" grip and transition to a Kimura grip.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Arm Escape: If the opponent's wrist is not secured, they can "hide" their arm by bringing their elbow in towards their body, creating space to pull their arm out.
    • Stacking Defense: If the attacker fails to walk their hips, the defender can "stack" their weight onto the attacker, making the submission impossible and potentially creating an escape.
    • Roll Through: If the attacker commits fully to the lock, the defender can sometimes roll through the submission, using the attacker's momentum to escape to a dominant position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Kimura from closed guard?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are gripping your own wrist too tightly or at an awkward angle, causing hyperextension. Instead, focus on a "figure-four" grip where your forearm is cradled by your own bicep, and your grip on your own wrist is relaxed but secure. The pressure should be on the opponent's shoulder, not your own wrist.

    Q: How can I apply the Kimura against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and technique are paraMount. Focus intensely on isolating their arm and maintaining tight control with your figure-four grip. Crucially, 'walk' your hips far to the side and keep your own hips elevated. This creates a better angle for you to apply torque and leverage their smaller frame against their own shoulder joint, rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Kimura and when should I abandon it?

    The best time is when their arm is isolated and their body is somewhat perpendicular to your hips. Abandon it if they posture up strongly, if they pull their arm out by bringing their elbow to their hip, or if they "stack" you effectively. Continuously reassess their posture and limb positioning; if the angle is wrong, it's better to reset your guard or transition to another attack.
