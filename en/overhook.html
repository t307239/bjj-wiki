The Overhook is a dominant position often achieved from Side Control or mount, aiming to restrict your opponent's movement and set up submissions.

White belts often struggle by overextending or neglecting proper weight distribution, leading to loss of position.

The key is to use your shoulder and upper arm to create a powerful, stable lever against your opponent's torso, controlling their hips and upper body.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, establish a strong cross-face grip with your left hand on their right cheek, pulling their head towards your chest.
    2. Your right arm wraps around their torso, hooking your right forearm deeply under their armpit and over their upper back.
    3. Your right hand grips your own left bicep, creating a tight "figure-four" or "seatbelt" grip.
    4. Drive your right shoulder into their upper chest, creating a wedge.
    5. Shift your weight forward, placing your chest directly onto their chest, hips angled slightly away from their hips.
    6. Your left leg extends back for base, while your right knee tucks slightly, keeping your hips low and heavy.
    7. Maintain constant pressure through your right shoulder and chest, preventing them from bridging or turning into you.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too loosely with the overhook arm: This allows the opponent to create space and escape by shrugging their shoulder, potentially leading to a sprained elbow or shoulder if they yank suddenly.
    • Incorrect: Loose grip, allowing opponent to shrug.
    • Correct: Tight figure-four grip on your own bicep, creating a solid lever.
    • Overextending your neck to reach for the cross-face: This puts your cervical spine at risk of hyperextension or whiplash if the opponent suddenly shifts their weight or creates a sudden movement.
    • Incorrect: Arching neck to reach.
    • Correct: Keeping head neutral and using shoulder pressure to control head.
    • Letting your hips rise too high when establishing the overhook: This reduces your base and stability, making you vulnerable to sweeps or reversals, and can strain your lower back if you lose balance.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip and shoulder placement without a partner, focusing on the "figure-four" and shoulder drive. (50 reps each side)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Partner lies passively. Execute the overhook entry from side control, focusing on weight distribution and grip tightness. (20 reps each side)
    3. Partner drilling (light pressure): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure of the overhook. Focus on maintaining the position. (30 reps each side)
    4. Positional sparring (50% resistance): Start from side control with the overhook. Partner attempts to escape, you focus on maintaining and improving. (5 rounds, 3 minutes each)
    5. Positional sparring (75% resistance): Increase partner's resistance. Focus on transitioning and preventing escapes. (5 rounds, 3 minutes each)
    6. Live rolling (100% resistance): Attempt to utilize the overhook in a live roll, focusing on maintaining control and setting up transitions. (10 rounds, 5 minutes each)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When passing to side control and the opponent's arm is inside your armpit, creating an opportunity to secure the overhook.
    • When the opponent attempts to bridge or shrimp away from your side control, and you can intercept their upper body with an overhook.
    • When you have established a strong cross-face and are looking to prevent the opponent from creating frames or returning to guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shoulder shrug and hip escape: The opponent attempts to shrug their shoulder out of the overhook while simultaneously shrimping their hips away, creating space to recover guard.
    • Underhook and elbow escape: The opponent secures an underhook on your far side and drives their elbow into the mat, creating a wedge to shrimp their hips out.
    • Bridge and turn: The opponent bridges hard into you, attempting to create space to roll their hips and turn into you, often looking to recover guard or escape to their knees.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do an Overhook in BJJ?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are incorrectly driving your shoulder directly into the opponent's scapula or upper arm bone, rather than using it to create a lever against their torso. Ensure your overhook arm is deep under their armpit and your grip is secured on your own bicep, allowing your shoulder to create pressure against their ribs and diaphragm. Keep your elbow tucked and avoid forcing your shoulder into a compromised angle.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, focus on leverage and timing rather than brute strength. Use your legs and hips to drive your shoulder into their upper torso, creating a wedge. When they attempt to move, look for opportunities to secure the overhook as they shift their weight. Grip your own bicep tightly to maximize the lever effect, and use your chest pressure to pin their upper body, preventing them from using their size to simply push you off.

    Q: The optimal time to attack is when you feel the opponent's weight and mobility significantly decrease due to your overhook control. This often happens when they are unable to effectively bridge or shrimp. Common submission setups include transitioning to a kimura by bringing their arm across their body, or using the overhook to control their posture for a choke like a cross-collar or arm-triangle. Always maintain pressure with your overhook while setting up your submission.
