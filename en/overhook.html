The Overhook Position in BJJ: A White Belt's Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Overhook is a dominant control position, typically achieved from Closed Guard or half guard, that restricts your opponent's movement. It allows for powerful sweeps and submissions by limiting their base and mobility.

White belts often struggle with the Overhook because they lack the understanding of weight distribution and leverage, leading to a loss of control or injury. They tend to grip too loosely or rely on brute strength.

The key mechanical insight is creating a strong, stable frame with your arm and shoulder, driving your weight forward to break their posture and establish superior control.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a standard collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on their left arm with your left hand.
    2. Shift your hips to your left, creating an angle where your left hip is slightly closer to their left hip.
    3. Drive your left shoulder into their right shoulder, creating a wedge.
    4. Begin to extend your left leg, allowing your opponent to post their right foot on the mat for base.
    5. As they post, retract your left leg and simultaneously drive your left elbow down and back, hooking their right arm from the outside (this is the Overhook).
    6. Simultaneously, deepen your collar grip, pulling their head down and to your left, while maintaining pressure with your left shoulder.
    7. Your right arm remains connected, preventing them from posturing up or creating space, and your hips are angled to maintain this secure frame.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to force an Overhook by pulling their arm across your body with only sleeve grips can strain your shoulder capsule. The correct method involves driving your shoulder into their body first to create the angle before securing the hook.
    • Overextending your elbow when securing the Overhook without proper hip angle can lead to elbow hyperextension injuries. Ensure your hips are angled and you are driving your shoulder into their torso before completing the hook.
    • Grabbing the Overhook too high on their shoulder or neck without establishing a strong frame can allow them to easily shrimp out or post their leg. Focus on hooking their arm at the elbow or upper bicep, with your shoulder actively pressing into their torso.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip shift, shoulder drive, and arm hook motion without a partner. Focus on the feeling of creating a stable frame. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (static): With a compliant partner, practice establishing the Overhook from closed guard. Focus on grip accuracy and hip angle. (25 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance as you secure the Overhook and practice maintaining it. (20 reps per side)
    4. Guard retention drill: You are in closed guard, partner tries to pass. You secure the Overhook to stop the pass. Partner offers 25% resistance to escape. (30 reps)
    5. Sweep attempt drill: From the Overhook position, attempt a sweep (e.g., scissor sweep). Partner offers 50% resistance to defend. (20 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (controlled): Implement the Overhook in rolling, focusing on establishing and maintaining the position, and then transitioning to a sweep or submission. Partner offers 75% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing down within your closed guard, creating an opportunity to isolate an arm.
    • When your opponent tries to stack you from closed guard, allowing you to use their pressure to create the Overhook angle.
    • When you are in half guard and your opponent is posturing up, giving you an opportunity to hook their arm as they try to establish base.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Elbow escape: If they have an Overhook on your right arm, shrimp your hips to your left and bring your right knee towards your right elbow, creating space to bring your hips back to a neutral position.
    • Posture break and grip break: If they have an Overhook and are controlling your posture, focus on breaking their collar grip first by prying their fingers open, then work to get your arm free.
    • Hip rotation and base adjustment: If you are caught with an Overhook, rotate your hips away from the hook and widen your base to make it harder for them to control your posture and drive weight.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an Overhook from closed guard?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are trying to force the Overhook by pulling their arm across your body without establishing the correct biomechanical angle. This puts excessive strain on your rotator cuff. Instead, focus on shifting your hips to the side and driving your shoulder into their torso first, creating a wedge before you hook their arm.

    Q: How can I use the Overhook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, the Overhook is about leverage and control, not strength. Focus on creating a tight frame with your shoulder and arm, driving your weight forward to break their posture. Use your hips to create angles and limit their ability to use their size advantage to stack or crush you. Prioritize grip fighting to prevent them from establishing dominant grips.

    Q: When should I use an Overhook versus just keeping my closed guard?

    You should transition to an Overhook when your opponent's posture is broken or they are posturing up in a way that allows you to isolate an arm and create a strong control point. It's not a default position for closed guard; it's a strategic choice to set up sweeps or submissions when you see an opening to limit their mobility and establish dominance.
