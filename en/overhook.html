BJJ Overhook Position: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Overhook is a dominant control position, most commonly achieved from Side Control or when establishing guard. It allows you to restrict your opponent's movement and set up submissions or transitions.

White belts often struggle with the Overhook because they rely on brute strength and don't understand the leverage points. This leads to ineffective control and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and upper arm as a fulcrum, creating a strong wedge to break your opponent's posture and control their scapula.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, establish a cross-face grip with your non-overhooking arm, pressing your forearm against their jawline, turning their head away.
    2. Simultaneously, drive your shoulder deep under their armpit, aiming to place your upper arm bone directly against their scapula.
    3. Your overhooking arm's forearm should wrap around their back, hooking their far latissimus dorsi muscle, just below the armpit.
    4. Your hips should be angled slightly towards their head, creating a wedge with your shoulder.
    5. Apply downward pressure with your shoulder into their scapula, forcing their arm to stay trapped and their shoulder to rotate internally.
    6. Maintain a tight base with your knees, keeping your weight centered over your hips, not your feet.
    7. Your head should be pressed into their neck or shoulder, preventing them from turning into you.
    8. The goal is to create a frame with your shoulder and arm that prevents their shoulder blade from moving freely.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying an Overhook by "hooking" with your hand and pulling your opponent's arm across your body: This can strain your rotator cuff and bicep by forcing an unnatural angle on your shoulder joint. The correct method involves driving your shoulder and upper arm bone into their scapula.
    • Trying to maintain the Overhook by squeezing your bicep around their arm: This can lead to elbow hyperextension injuries if they bridge or twist forcefully. Instead, use your shoulder and forearm to create a stable wedge against their body.
    • Overhooking too high on the neck or too low on the back: This compromises leverage and can expose your own neck or allow your opponent to escape. Always aim for the scapula and the latissimus dorsi.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the shoulder-to-scapula and arm-around-back mechanics without a partner. Focus on the feeling of creating the wedge. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill - passive: Your partner lies on their side or back. Practice establishing the Overhook position, focusing on grip and body alignment. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill - light pressure: Your partner offers minimal resistance. Practice executing the Overhook from a neutral starting position and holding it. (5 minutes, 25% resistance)
    4. Transition drilling: Start in side control and practice transitioning to the Overhook position, then hold for 10 seconds before returning to side control. (5 minutes, 50% resistance)
    5. Controlled rolling: Roll with a partner who understands the goal. Focus on establishing and maintaining the Overhook for extended periods. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Overhook into your regular sparring. Focus on using it to control your opponent and set up attacks. (5 minutes, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you achieve side control and your opponent is actively framing against your chest.
    • When you are defending guard and manage to secure an overhook on their arm as they attempt to pass.
    • When you are in your opponent's guard and they are trying to create space with their legs, allowing you to establish an overhook.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Elbow-to-knee escape: If your opponent has an Overhook from side control, bring your elbow to your knee on the same side, creating a tight defensive frame and attempting to shrimp away.
    • Arm-out-and-under: If you feel the Overhook being established, immediately try to pull your trapped arm across your body and under your opponent's chin, creating space to potentially transition to your guard.
    • Hip pressure and forward drive: If your opponent is attempting an Overhook from your guard, drive your hips forward and apply downward pressure on their Overhooking arm, trying to break their structure and flatten them out.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do an Overhook in BJJ?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are using your bicep and forearm to squeeze the opponent's arm, rather than using your shoulder and upper arm bone as a fulcrum against their scapula. This forces your shoulder joint into an unnatural and vulnerable position. Focus on driving your shoulder deep under their armpit and pinning their scapula to the mat with downward pressure.

    Q: How can I establish an Overhook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage is key. Instead of trying to muscle the Overhook, focus on off-balancing them first. Use a frame to create a small gap, then quickly drive your shoulder under their armpit. Your hips should be angled to create a wedge, and you'll use your body weight to keep their shoulder trapped, not just your arm strength.

    Q: When should I use an Overhook versus just controlling their collar in BJJ?

    Collar grips are excellent for controlling posture and setting up submissions like chokes. An Overhook, however, is primarily a positional control tool. It excels at restricting an opponent's ability to turn into you or create space by framing with their arm. Use the Overhook when your primary goal is to limit their movement and maintain dominant control, often as a precursor to other attacks.
