The BJJ Overhook: A White Belt's Precise Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The overhook is a dominant position achieved when your arm passes over your opponent's shoulder and secures a grip on their back or shoulder blade. It's primarily used to control an opponent's posture and prevent them from posturing up, setting up sweeps and submissions.

White belts often struggle with the overhook by relying on brute strength or poor posture, leading to them being easily countered or giving up their own position. They forget that the overhook is about leverage and control, not just trapping an arm.

The key mechanical insight is to use your shoulder and scapula to drive into your opponent, creating a strong frame that limits their movement and allows you to dictate the engagement. Think of it as creating a 'wall' with your upper body.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a dominant position like closed guard or side control, establish your body positioning close to your opponent's shoulder.
    2. Wrap your dominant arm around your opponent's neck and shoulder, aiming to get your bicep and forearm on the outside of their neck.
    3. Your hand (your 'hook') should reach across your opponent's upper back, ideally gripping their shoulder blade or the fabric of their gi on their upper back.
    4. Simultaneously, your non-dominant arm should secure a grip on your opponent's opposite arm (e.g., sleeve grip, wrist grip) to prevent them from posturing up or framing.
    5. Drive your shoulder and scapula into your opponent's neck and upper chest, creating a strong frame that collapses their posture.
    6. Adjust your hips to be perpendicular to your opponent's body, increasing the leverage and control.
    7. Maintain a tight seal with your chest to their chest, preventing them from creating space and posturing up.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to force the overhook by pulling with your arm alone: This can lead to elbow hyperextension, damaging the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) of the elbow. Instead, use your shoulder and scapula to drive into your opponent.
    • Allowing your elbow to flare out wide when establishing the overhook: This creates an opening for your opponent to attack your arm with a kimura or americana. Keep your elbow tucked in and connected to your body.
    • Overextending your neck to reach the grip: This puts your cervical spine at risk of strain or injury. Instead, use hip and torso movement to close the distance and secure the grip.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice establishing the overhook grip on a dummy or training partner without resistance. Focus on hip movement and shoulder drive. (50 reps each side)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner allows you to establish the overhook from a specific starting position (e.g., guard pass). Focus on the grip and body positioning. (20 reps each side)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance - 25%): Your partner offers minimal resistance to you establishing the overhook, allowing you to feel the control. (20 reps each side)
    4. Partner drilling (moderate resistance - 50%): Your partner actively tries to prevent you from establishing the overhook, requiring you to use your mechanics. (20 reps each side)
    5. Situational sparring (75%): Start from a position where the overhook is possible and spar with the goal of achieving and maintaining it. (5 rounds of 2 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Integrate the overhook into your live rolling, focusing on its application and transition to other techniques. (5 rounds of 3 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard, and you can snake your arm over their shoulder.
    • When you are in side control and your opponent is trying to bridge or create space, allowing you to secure an overhook on their upper body.
    • When transitioning from a failed sweep or submission attempt, and you can use the overhook to regain control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Elbow Escape (Hip Escape): If your opponent has an overhook in your guard, drive your hips to the side, away from their controlling arm, and shrimp out to create space. This breaks their posture and control.
    • Posture Up and Grip Break: From the bottom, if your opponent has an overhook and is trying to smash you, try to get your hands on their gripping arm's wrist and elbow, then use your legs to create space and break their grip while posturing up.
    • Framing with the opposite arm: If your opponent has an overhook, use your free arm to frame against their head or shoulder, creating space and preventing them from fully collapsing your posture. This can lead to a switch or escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an overhook?

    Your shoulder is likely hurting because you are forcing the overhook with your arm strength rather than using your body's leverage. When you try to reach too far or rotate your shoulder unnaturally, you can strain the rotator cuff. Focus on driving your shoulder and scapula into your opponent's neck and upper chest, using your hips to close the distance. This allows for a more stable and less strenuous grip.

    Q: How can I get an overhook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, the overhook is about efficient leverage, not raw strength. Focus on timing and closing the distance rapidly. Use your hips to get underneath their center of gravity, then drive your shoulder into their neck and upper chest. Aim to secure the grip on their upper back and shoulder blade, using your body weight to pin them rather than trying to lift or pull them. Think of it as pinning them to the mat with your body.

    Q: When is the best time to use an overhook in BJJ?

    The overhook is most effective when your opponent is postured up or trying to create space. In your guard, it's a prime tool to prevent them from posturing up and to set up sweeps like the scissor sweep or hip bump sweep. From top positions like Side Control, it can prevent them from bridging or turning into you, allowing you to maintain control and transition to other attacks. It's a control position that can transition into offense.
