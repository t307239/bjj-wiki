The Overhook: A Foundational BJJ Position for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The overhook is a dominant control position, typically achieved from Closed Guard or half guard, aiming to restrict your opponent's posture and mobility.

White belts often struggle by overextending their arm or lacking structural integrity, leading to loss of position or injury.

The key is to use your arm as a lever, creating a strong frame and driving your opponent's weight onto their own structure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a standard cross-collar grip with your right hand and a cross-sleeve grip with your left hand on your opponent's gi.
    2. Shift your hips slightly to your left, creating a 45-degree angle between your torsos.
    3. Drive your left elbow down towards your opponent's left hip, creating a strong frame.
    4. Simultaneously, reach your right arm *over* your opponent's right shoulder, securing a grip on their gi material on their upper back, just below the shoulder blade.
    5. Contract your right bicep and lats to pull their shoulder down and into your chest, establishing the overhook.
    6. Maintain your left arm's frame against their hip, preventing them from posturing up or bridging effectively.
    7. Keep your knees tight to your chest, maintaining a strong base and preventing them from passing your guard.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Punching your arm straight out and reaching for the overhook: This leaves your shoulder vulnerable to an armbar or kimura, as your humerus is exposed and your scapula is not retracted.
    • Trying to pull the opponent's shoulder down with a loose grip: This fails to create leverage, allowing the opponent to easily escape by shrugging their shoulder or turning into you, potentially straining your elbow or wrist.
    • Not creating a base with your non-overhooking arm: Leaving your left arm extended or limp allows the opponent to easily push it away, break your posture, and pass your guard, while your left shoulder is at risk of hyperextension if they drive into you.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the arm and hip movement of securing the overhook without a partner. Focus on the reach and the pull, 20 reps.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Partner stands passively. Practice securing the overhook from closed guard, focusing on grip and frame. 5 sets of 5 reps each side.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to the overhook. Focus on maintaining the grip and frame against slight pressure. 5 sets of 5 reps each side (25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance): Partner actively tries to create space or posture up. Focus on using the overhook to control their shoulder and hip frame. 5 sets of 5 reps each side (50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt to establish the overhook from guard during light sparring. Focus on positional control, not submission. 3 rounds x 3 minutes (75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (full): Integrate the overhook into your rolling game, looking for opportunities to improve position or attack. 5 rounds x 5 minutes (90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up in your closed guard and you want to break their posture.
    • When you are in half guard and your opponent has established side control but is reaching for your head or arm.
    • When you are attempting a sweep from guard and your opponent is defending by reaching over your body.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Shoulder shrug and elbow drive: The opponent shrugs their overhooked shoulder up and drives their elbow down towards their own hip, creating space and breaking the grip.
    • Underhook escape: The opponent secures their own underhook on your arm and uses it to drive their shoulder back, creating a frame and allowing them to turn into you.
    • Hip escape and posture up: The opponent performs a strong hip escape to create distance, then uses their free arm to push off your shoulder and posture up, breaking your control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get the Overhook in BJJ?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are reaching too far with a straight arm, exposing your glenohumeral joint and rotator cuff to excessive strain. Instead, bend your elbow and use your lats and bicep to pull their shoulder down, keeping your scapula retracted for stability. Ensure your non-overhooking arm maintains a solid frame against their hip.

    Q: How do I Overhook against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage is even more critical. Focus on creating a sharp angle with your hips (45 degrees) to off-balance them. Use your legs to actively pull their hips towards you while simultaneously driving your overhooking arm's elbow down to their hip, not just around their shoulder. A strong collar grip on the other side will help you break their posture.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Overhook to an attack in BJJ?

    The optimal time to transition from an overhook to an attack is when you have successfully broken your opponent's posture and they are bent over, creating an opening. This often occurs when their weight is loaded onto the arm you have overhooked. Look for opportunities to attack with sweeps like the scissor sweep or transitions to submissions like the triangle choke or Armbar as they try to recover their posture.
