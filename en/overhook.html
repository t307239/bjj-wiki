Understanding the Overhook in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

The Overhook is a dominant position where you secure an opponent's arm with your arm going *over* theirs. It's often established from neutral positions or during transitions, aiming to control their upper body and set up sweeps or submissions.

Many white belts struggle with the Overhook by relying on raw strength or poor posture, leading to them being easily swept or losing the position. They often fail to create a strong base and proper leverage.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and elbow to create a strong fulcrum, driving your hips forward to establish superior leverage and control over their posture and base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a neutral standing position or while establishing guard, step your right foot slightly forward and angle your hips to your opponent's left.
    2. Extend your right arm, palm facing up, and reach across your opponent's chest towards their right armpit.
    3. Secure a "collar grip" on the back of their gi collar with your right hand, or "sleeve grip" on their right sleeve near the elbow.
    4. Simultaneously, step your left foot back to widen your base and lower your center of gravity, creating a strong tripod.
    5. Drive your right elbow down and slightly forward, keeping it tucked close to your body. This is your primary control point.
    6. Rotate your hips to the left, pressing your right shoulder into your opponent's right shoulder blade. This creates a "hook" with your arm.
    7. Maintain a strong frame with your left arm, either framing on their hip or gripping their left sleeve, to prevent them from posturing up or turning into you.
    8. Keep your weight distributed evenly over your feet, with a slight forward lean to pressure your opponent.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Error: Extending your elbow fully and trying to "pull" the opponent's arm down with just your bicep. Damage: This can cause elbow hyperextension or ligament tears (ulnar collateral ligament) in your own elbow or their elbow if they resist incorrectly. Correction: Keep your elbow bent at approximately 90 degrees and use your shoulder and torso to create the leverage, driving your elbow forward and down.
    • Error: Standing too upright with a narrow base, attempting to secure the Overhook. Damage: This instability makes you vulnerable to sweeps like the Osoto-Gari or Harai-Goshi, potentially leading to knee or ankle injuries due to awkward falls. Correction: Widen your base by stepping one foot back and lower your hips to create a stable, grounded foundation.
    • Error: Allowing your head to drift too far forward or to the side when establishing the Overhook. Damage: This can lead to neck hyperextension or strain, especially if the opponent drives into you, potentially causing cervical disc issues. Correction: Keep your head up, looking towards your opponent's head, and maintain a strong, aligned posture.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling: Practice the footwork and arm positioning of establishing the Overhook against an imaginary opponent. (5 sets of 10 reps each side).
    2. Partnered Static Gripping: With a partner, practice securing the Overhook grips and maintaining the shoulder-to-shoulder pressure without any movement. (5 sets of 30 seconds each side).
    3. Controlled Transition Drilling (0% Resistance): From a neutral position, one partner offers minimal resistance as the other attempts to establish the Overhook. Focus on correct body mechanics. (5 sets of 5 reps each side).
    4. Positional Control Drilling (25% Resistance): From the established Overhook position, the defender offers slight resistance to prevent the attacker from advancing. The attacker focuses on maintaining the Overhook and controlling posture. (5 sets of 1 minute each side).
    5. Sweep/Submission Setup Drilling (50% Resistance): Once the Overhook is established, the attacker attempts a basic sweep or submission from the position, while the defender actively tries to escape or defend. (5 sets of 1.5 minutes each side).
    6. Live Rolling (75-90% Resistance): Incorporate the Overhook into sparring rounds. Focus on recognizing opportunities to establish it and use it effectively, while the defender works to counter it. (3 rounds of 5 minutes each).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent attempts a takedown and you can secure an Overhook to defend and potentially counter.
    • When transitioning from open guard or half guard and you can secure their arm to prevent them from passing.
    • When an opponent is posturing up in your guard, and you can reach across to secure their arm for control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Whizzer" (Counter-Overhook): The defender secures their own Overhook on the attacker's arm, driving their shoulder into the attacker's chest and using their free arm to grip the attacker's far hip. This neutralizes the attacker's leverage and allows for potential sweeps or escapes.
    • The "Shrimp and Turn" Escape: The defender shrimpos their hips away and turns their body towards the trapped arm, creating space to disengage or re-establish guard. This requires a quick, explosive movement.
    • "Breaking the Grip" and "Creating Space": The defender uses their free hand to pry at the attacker's grip on their collar or sleeve, while simultaneously pushing with their hips and shoulders to create enough space to either stand up or return to a more neutral guard position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do an Overhook in BJJ?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are trying to force the Overhook with raw strength rather than using proper biomechanics. When you extend your arm fully and try to pull down, you put excessive strain on your rotator cuff and shoulder joint. Instead, keep your elbow bent at around 90 degrees, drive your shoulder into their scapula, and use your hips to create leverage, turning it into a shoulder-on-shoulder pressure.

    Q: Against a bigger opponent, the Overhook relies even more on precise technique and leverage. Focus on getting your hips lower than theirs, widening your base, and securing a strong collar grip if possible. Drive your elbow down and forward, using your shoulder as a fulcrum, and pivot your hips to create a strong "hook." Don't try to match their strength; out-leverage them by controlling their posture and base.

    Q: The Overhook is primarily a dominant *position*, not a submission itself, though it sets up many submissions. The best times to go for it are when an opponent is trying to pass your guard, when you're defending a takedown, or when transitioning between guards. Its purpose is to control their upper body, limit their mobility, and create opportunities for sweeps or chokes like a Rear Naked Choke if you can achieve a back-take.
