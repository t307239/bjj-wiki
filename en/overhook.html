BJJ Overhook Position: A White Belt's Precise Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Overhook is a powerful positional control, typically established from within the opponent's guard or during transitions. It aims to restrict opponent's upper body mobility and set up sweeps or submissions.

White belts often struggle with the Overhook by relying on brute strength or poor posture, leading to energy expenditure and loss of control.

The core principle is controlling the opponent's scapula and elbow, preventing them from creating frames or turning into you, thereby controlling their base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Initiate from a position where your opponent's arm is accessible, such as during a guard pass attempt or from side control.
    2. Secure a grip on the opponent's triceps or biceps with your forearm against their upper arm, creating a "hook".
    3. Simultaneously, establish a secondary grip on their far shoulder blade or upper back with your opposite hand.
    4. Position your body perpendicular to the opponent, with your hips driving towards their hip on the side of the overhook.
    5. Create a strong base by placing your knee on the mat on the side of the overhook, and your other foot flat on the mat.
    6. Apply pressure by driving your shoulder into their upper back, compressing their torso and limiting their ability to bridge or shrimp.
    7. Maintain a tight frame with your elbow and forearm, preventing them from posturing up or creating space.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying the overhook by extending your arm fully: This can lead to hyperextension of your elbow joint, potentially damaging the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL).
    • Gripping too loosely on the opponent's shoulder blade: This allows them to create space and escape the control, often leading to a guard pass against you.
    • Trying to "muscle" the overhook without proper body positioning: This strains your shoulder joint and can lead to rotator cuff tears or impingement syndrome.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the arm and body positioning of the overhook against a training dummy or air, focusing on exact angles and grips. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner allows you to establish the overhook from a starting position, holding it passively. Focus on maintaining correct body mechanics. (20 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance as you establish the overhook, allowing you to feel the pressure and adjust. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Static position drill: Start in a position where the overhook is established (e.g., side control) and hold the position for 30 seconds, focusing on maintaining tightness. (10 sets, 50% resistance)
    5. Transition drill: Practice transitioning into the overhook from common guard pass attempts, with your partner resisting slightly. (10 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (controlled): Implement the overhook during sparring, focusing on establishing and maintaining it for short periods, then releasing to continue the roll. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their arm is exposed to your inside.
    • When you are in side control and can isolate one of their arms.
    • When transitioning from mount or knee-on-belly and the opponent tries to shrimp away.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Creating Space: If your opponent has an overhook on you, focus on creating space by shrimping your hips away and driving your shoulder into their chest, breaking their posture.
    • Hand Fighting: Actively fight to break their grip on your shoulder blade or upper back, using your free hand to peel their fingers or wrist away.
    • Turning In: If they have a strong overhook, try to turn your body into their overhooking arm, using your shoulder to drive into their armpit and disrupt their control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an overhook in BJJ?

    Shoulder pain during overhook attempts is often due to poor body positioning and gripping. If you are extending your arm fully or trying to pull their arm across with just your arm strength, you are putting excessive strain on your shoulder joint. Instead, focus on using your hips and core to drive into your opponent, keeping your elbow bent and your forearm tight against their upper arm, while using your other hand to control their shoulder blade.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, brute strength is your enemy. Focus on precision and leverage. Instead of trying to hook their arm directly, use your feet to create angles and off-balance them first. Once they are slightly unstable, use your forearm to slide under their armpit and hook their triceps or biceps. Simultaneously, use your opposite hand to grip their far shoulder blade, driving your hips in to compress their torso and limit their base.

    Q: The optimal time to transition from an Overhook to a submission is when you have successfully restricted your opponent's mobility and broken their posture. This typically occurs when their weight is compromised, they are unable to defend effectively, or they are forced into an awkward position. For instance, a strong overhook can set up a triangle choke if you can isolate their leg, or an armbar if you can control their posture and extend their arm.
