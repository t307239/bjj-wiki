The Clock Choke is a submission typically initiated from Side Control or mount, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain by compressing the carotid arteries.

White belts often struggle with proper weight distribution and grip application, leading to ineffective attempts and potential self-injury.

The key mechanical insight is using your body weight and hip angle to create a fulcrum, leveraging your opponent's own neck structure against them.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, establish a "seatbelt" grip on the opponent's far shoulder with your near-side arm, palm facing your face.
    2. Place your other hand (far side) under the opponent's chin, thumb inside the collar, fingers pointing towards their head.
    3. Rotate your hips towards the opponent's head, creating a "clock" face angle where their head is at 12 o'clock and your hips are at 6 o'clock.
    4. Drive your chest into the opponent's shoulder on the side of your seatbelt grip, flattening them to the mat.
    5. Extend your hips slightly upwards while simultaneously pulling your seatbelt arm's elbow back and down.
    6. Use your chin to press into the opponent's collarbone on the choking side, preventing them from creating space.
    7. Squeeze your elbows together, creating pressure by drawing your hands towards each other, completing the choke.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect grip placement: Placing the choking hand too high on the neck can lead to pressure on the trachea (windpipe) instead of the carotid arteries, causing coughing and discomfort without submission, and potentially damaging the trachea.
    • Excessive neck twisting: Attempting to force the choke by twisting the opponent's head can strain the cervical spine and ligaments, leading to neck pain or injury.
    • Improper hip angle: Failing to rotate hips to the 6 o'clock position allows the opponent to turn into you, relieving pressure and potentially leading to a sweep or escape, while also straining your own lower back.
    • Over-reliance on arm strength: Trying to "pull" the choke with arm strength alone, without proper hip drive and body weight, is inefficient and can lead to bicep or shoulder strain.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip acquisition and hip rotation on a dummy or mat, focusing on the correct angles and weight distribution. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the full sequence of grips, hip movement, and body positioning, focusing on feel. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal defense, allowing you to feel the pressure build as you execute the choke. Focus on maintaining control. (10 reps per side)
    4. Positional sparring (light): Attempt the Clock Choke from side control during light positional sparring, focusing on recognizing the opportunity and executing the mechanics. (5 attempts per round)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): During live rolling, actively look for opportunities to set up the Clock Choke, allowing for natural resistance and reaction from the opponent. (15% of rolling time)
    6. Live rolling (full): Integrate the Clock Choke as a natural submission option during full intensity rolling, focusing on adapting to the opponent's reactions. (25% of rolling time)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong side control position with the opponent flat on their back.
    • When the opponent attempts to bridge or shrimp away, creating an opening for your choking arm.
    • When the opponent tucks their chin defensively, making it difficult for them to escape the collar grip.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape the grip: The opponent can quickly remove the choking hand by shucking the arm over their head or by turning their head away from the pressure.
    • Bridge and turn: By bridging their hips and turning their head towards the choking arm, they can relieve pressure and create space to escape.
    • Posture up and create space: The opponent can attempt to sit up and create distance, breaking the tight connection needed for the choke.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Clock Choke?

    Wrist pain often stems from an incorrect grip on the collar. If your thumb is not deeply embedded, or if your wrist is bent at an extreme angle to accommodate the grip, you're putting undue stress on the joint. Ensure your thumb is inside the collar, and your forearm is as straight as possible, pressing into their neck. This alignment distributes the force better and protects your wrist.

    Q: How can I get the Clock Choke to work against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body positioning are paraMount, not brute strength. Focus on using your hips to create a tight angle, driving your chest into their shoulder to flatten them, and using their own body weight against them. Your goal is to make them feel like they are being 'rolled' by your body weight, not to simply pull their head. Ensure your seatbelt grip is high and tight on the shoulder.

    Q: I'm struggling to finish the Clock Choke, it feels like I'm not applying enough pressure. What am I missing?

    The most common reason for a weak Clock Choke is insufficient hip drive and improper body angle. You need to rotate your hips to create a "clock" shape, with your hips at 6 o'clock and their head at 12 o'clock. Then, actively drive your chest into their shoulder while simultaneously squeezing your elbows and drawing your hands together. Think of it as compressing them between your body and your arms, not just pulling.
