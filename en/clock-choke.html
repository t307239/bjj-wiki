Clock Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Clock Choke is initiated from the Closed Guard, aiming to submit the opponent by compressing their carotid arteries. White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or incorrect body positioning, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. The key to the Clock Choke is isolating one arm and creating a precise angle of pressure using your hips and the opponent's own shoulder.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the opponent's gi lapel with your right hand, gripping the far side of their collar as deeply as possible, fingers pointing towards their sternum.
    2. With your left hand, secure the opponent's right sleeve, gripping the fabric just above the wrist joint.
    3. Drive your hips to your left, creating a 90-degree angle between your torso and the opponent's body, ensuring your right hip is lower than your left.
    4. Swing your right leg over the opponent's head, placing your right foot on the mat outside their left shoulder, maintaining a strong base.
    5. Thread your left arm under the opponent's right armpit, reaching to grip your own right bicep, creating a "figure-four" grip on their neck.
    6. Arch your back and drive your chest into the opponent's neck and shoulder, simultaneously pulling your right elbow down and back.
    7. Maintain hip pressure by pushing your left hip towards the opponent's left hip, creating a fulcrum for the choke.
    8. Keep your head tight to the opponent's head, preventing them from posturing up or turning their head away from the choke.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive upward force with the choking arm instead of a downward pull: This can strain the rotator cuff and shoulder joint due to uncontrolled abduction and external rotation.
    • Gripping the gi too loosely or too high on the collar: This allows the opponent to escape the choke easily and can lead to wrist hyperextension of the gripping hand.
    • Allowing the opponent to turn their head into the choke: This can cause neck strain and compromise the choke's effectiveness, potentially leading to cervical spine issues.
    • Forgetting to drive hips forward and create an angle: This results in a weak choke and can lead to the opponent easily posturing up or escaping the guard.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip changes and body positioning without a partner. Focus on hip movement and leg placement. (50 reps each side)
    2. Partner assisted drilling (no resistance): Your partner remains still while you execute the entire sequence, focusing on smooth transitions and correct body angles. (10 reps each side)
    3. Light resistance drilling: Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure and adjust your grips and body mechanics. (20 reps each side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled resistance drilling: Partner actively tries to defend but allows you to work through the steps, focusing on maintaining control and finishing the choke. (15 reps each side, 50% resistance)
    5. Flow rolling with focus: Practice the Clock Choke as part of a rolling sequence, aiming to set it up from the closed guard. Partner offers moderate defense. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Clock Choke during live rolling, prioritizing correct technique over immediate submission. (2 x 5-minute rounds, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured up slightly, creating space between your guard and their torso.
    • When the opponent attempts to stack you from the closed guard, opening up their neck.
    • When you have successfully isolated one of their arms across your body.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • To defend the initial grip: If your collar grip is weak, immediately break it by prying their fingers open with your thumb and forefinger.
    • To prevent the leg over the head: If you feel the leg coming, keep your head down and try to turn your hips away from the direction of the leg.
    • To escape the choke once applied: Immediately try to turn your head into the choke, reducing the pressure, and bring your free arm to defend your neck or pry the choke open.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to grip the collar for the Clock Choke?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping the gi too tightly with a bent wrist or gripping too high on the collar. Ensure your wrist remains neutral or slightly extended, and aim for the deepest part of the collar possible, near the opponent's sternum. This maximizes leverage and minimizes strain on your wrist.

    Q: How can I use the Clock Choke against a much larger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on hip mobility and creating a sharp angle. Instead of trying to muscle the choke, use your hips to create space and drive your right leg over their head to establish a strong base. Your weight distribution and the angle of your body are more critical than raw strength.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to the Clock Choke from closed guard?

    The optimal time is when the opponent postures up or attempts to stack you. These actions create the necessary space between your bodies. If they are too flat or too tight, attempting the Clock Choke can be difficult and might lead to them passing your guard.
