The Headquarters Pass is a guard passing technique initiated from a standing or kneeling position, aiming to secure Side Control or mount. White belts often struggle due to a lack of fundamental weight distribution and control, leading to predictable movements.

The key mechanical insight is creating a strong, off-balancing base while driving your shoulder into the opponent's hip to break their posture and create an opening.

This pass fundamentally relies on controlling the opponent's hips and posture to prevent them from re-establishing guard or creating frames.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start in a staggered stance, one knee down, the other foot forward, maintaining a base width slightly wider than shoulder-width.
    2. Secure a cross-collar grip with your lead hand (closest to opponent's head) and a sleeve grip on the same side with your trail hand.
    3. Drive your lead shoulder into the opponent's hip pocket, aiming to create a shallow angle between your torso and their hip.
    4. Simultaneously, extend your trail leg backwards, creating a strong base and transferring weight forward onto your lead shoulder.
    5. Use your cross-collar grip to pull their head down and towards your lead shoulder, breaking their posture.
    6. With your sleeve grip, pull their arm across their body, further disrupting their balance and creating a space.
    7. Drive your hips forward and down, maintaining pressure with your lead shoulder, as you step your trail leg up and over their far hip to secure side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to force the pass by pulling with the arms alone: This can lead to elbow hyperextension, damaging the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL), or shoulder impingement. The correct method uses leg drive and body weight, not just arm strength.
    • Lunging forward without a stable base: This can result in knee valgus collapse, stressing the medial collateral ligament (MCL) and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). Maintain a wide, stable base and drive from the hips.
    • Allowing the opponent to grab your head or neck during the pass: This can lead to dangerous chokes or neck cranks, risking cervical spine injury. Keep your head up and maintain control of their posture throughout the movement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of the staggered stance, hip drive, and shoulder pressure in the air (10 reps per side).
    2. Partner drilling with no resistance, focusing on grip placement and weight transfer (20 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drilling with light resistance, focusing on breaking posture and clearing the hip (30 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drilling with increasing resistance, focusing on maintaining pressure and hip control (40 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling, focusing solely on initiating the Headquarters Pass when the opportunity arises (5 rounds, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling, integrating the Headquarters Pass as a primary passing option (5 rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is attempting to close their guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • When the opponent has a loose or disconnected closed guard, allowing you to penetrate their hip.
    • When the opponent attempts a low guard or has their hips too far away from your knees.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Leg Pummeling: If the opponent feels your shoulder pressure, they can pummel their leg (e.g., their far leg) between your hips and torso to create space and prevent penetration. You must maintain tight hip control and drive to avoid this.
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If you fail to secure a strong shoulder drive, the opponent can hip escape and attempt to re-establish their guard (e.g., butterfly guard or open guard). This requires you to maintain constant forward pressure.
    • Frame and Push: The opponent can use their arms to create frames against your shoulders or head, pushing you away. You must use your collar grip to break their posture and your shoulder pressure to overcome these frames.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do the Headquarters Pass?

    Your shoulder might hurt because you're relying too much on arm strength to drive forward, or you're not creating the correct angle. Ensure your lead shoulder is driving into the opponent's hip pocket, not their chest, and that your base is wide and stable to support your weight transfer. Focus on driving your hips forward and down, using your legs for power.

    Q: How do I do the Headquarters Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage and precise angles are even more critical. Focus on establishing a very tight cross-collar grip to pull their head down and break their posture significantly. Use your shoulder pressure to create a 'lever' against their hip, and ensure your base is wide enough to support your weight as you drive forward. Avoid trying to lift or overpower them; instead, focus on off-balancing and controlling their upper body.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Headquarters Pass against someone in closed guard?

    The ideal time to attempt the Headquarters Pass against Closed Guard is when the opponent is slightly off-balance, perhaps leaning too far forward or backward, or when their hips are not tightly locked. Look for any slight opening or disconnection in their guard. If their guard is extremely tight and secure, other passing techniques might be more effective initially.
