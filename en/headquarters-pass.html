Headquarters Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Headquarters Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a Closed Guard, aiming to secure side control.

White belts often struggle due to imprecise hip pressure and incorrect weight distribution, leading to ineffective passes or reversals.

The key insight is maintaining constant downward pressure on the opponent's hips while isolating one leg to create a clear path to Side Control.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent is in closed guard. Assume a kneeling position with your hips close to their hips, knees framing their thighs.
    2. Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (same side) with your left hand.
    3. Drive your chest forward, forcing your opponent's hips to flatten against the mat. Your hip angle should be slightly forward of vertical.
    4. With your left hand, grip the opponent's right pant leg at the knee. Keep your elbow tucked.
    5. Begin to drive your right knee forward, aiming to place it inside the opponent's right thigh.
    6. Simultaneously, drive your hips down and forward, maintaining chest-to-chest connection.
    7. Peel the opponent's right leg off your hip with your left hand, sliding your right knee further inside.
    8. Drive your opponent's right hip to the mat by pushing down with your chest and forward with your hips.
    9. Step your left leg across their body, securing side control with your right knee on the mat and your chest on their chest.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly attempting to 'push' the opponent's legs apart with straight arms: This can hyperextend the elbow joint, leading to ligamentous tears (e.g., UCL sprain) or shoulder impingement. The correct action is to use your body weight and hip drive to create space.
    • Bending at the waist with rounded shoulders when driving forward: This strains the lumbar spine and increases the risk of disc injury. Maintain a neutral spine and drive with your hips and legs.
    • Allowing the opponent to hook your leg as you step out: This can lead to a sweep attempt where your knee or ankle can be twisted unnaturally, risking ligament tears (ACL, MCL) or fractures. Keep your base wide and your knee driving forward to prevent this hook.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg isolation and knee slide motion without a partner for 5 minutes, focusing on hip pressure and knee direction.
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): Partner lies on their back, you practice the entire pass sequence. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the hip pressure and leg peel. 10 reps per side.
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to maintain closed guard, you focus on breaking the guard and securing the pass. 10 reps per side.
    5. Live rolling (75% resistance): Focus on executing the Headquarters Pass against a resisting opponent. Aim for 5 successful passes.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Execute the Headquarters Pass as a primary objective during rolling. Aim for 3 successful passes.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent attempts to close their guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • Opponent has established a strong closed guard, and you have established a stable base.
    • You are in a situation where the opponent is trying to create distance to set up sweeps.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Bump Sweep: As the opponent attempts to drive their knee inside, frame with your forearm on their hip and bridge your hips to the opposite side, creating an angle to sweep.
    • Scissor Sweep: If the opponent's leg is isolated but they haven't fully committed to the pass, use your legs to create a scissor motion, trapping one of their legs and sweeping.
    • Re-guarding: If the pass is slow, the opponent can use their free leg to re-establish guard by hooking your hip or leg and pulling themselves back into guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent keep reversing me when I try the Headquarters Pass?

    This often happens when you lose connection with their hips or your weight distribution is incorrect. If you allow space between your chest and their hips, they can bridge effectively. Ensure your chest stays heavy on their chest and your hips are driving *down* and *forward*, not just forward.

    Q: How can I perform the Headquarters Pass more effectively against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Focus on a strong cross-collar grip to control their posture and prevent them from posturing up. Drive your weight *down* into their hips to flatten them, making their size less of an advantage. Use your knee to aggressively penetrate their thigh space.

    Q: When should I stop trying to Headquarters Pass and switch to another guard pass?

    You should switch if the opponent has a very strong closed guard that you cannot break with consistent hip pressure, or if they are actively framing and creating significant space. If they consistently manage to hook your leg as you attempt to step out, it's a sign you need to abandon the attempt and try something like a knee-slice or leg-drag.
