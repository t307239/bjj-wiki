The Headquarters Pass is a fundamental BJJ guard pass initiated from a standing or kneeling position, aiming to bypass the opponent's legs and achieve Side Control or mount.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise body control, leading to predictable movements and easy counter-attacks from the guard player.

The core mechanic involves creating a strong, stable base while simultaneously attacking the opponent's posture and hip line, forcing them to break their guard structure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grips: Establish a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (same-side or opposite-side) with your left hand. Your thumb should be inside the collar grip.
    2. Base: Kneel on your left knee, with your right knee forward and flat on the mat, creating a stable base. Your hips should be squared with the opponent's hips.
    3. Posture Control: Pull down on the collar grip to break the opponent's posture. Simultaneously, use your left arm to control their right sleeve, preventing them from framing.
    4. Hip Pressure: Drive your right knee forward, applying downward pressure towards the opponent's hip. Your body should be angled slightly towards their left side.
    5. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your right knee and the ball of your left foot. Your torso should lean forward, creating a "smash" effect.
    6. Clearing the Leg: With your right knee driving forward and your hips angled, begin to peel their right leg off your torso. Use your left arm to maintain sleeve control and prevent them from re-establishing guard.
    7. Transition to Side Control: Once their right leg is cleared, step your right leg through, placing your right foot flat on the mat. Keep your hips low and drive forward, securing side control with your chest on their chest.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Knee Placement: Driving the knee directly into the opponent's stomach or solar plexus without proper hip angling can cause rib fractures or internal organ damage. The knee should be directed towards their hip line, not their torso.
    • Over-Extending the Elbow: When breaking posture with the collar grip, if you hyperextend your right elbow without proper support from your core and base, you risk ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) tears. Maintain a slight bend in the elbow and use your entire body to create the pulling force.
    • Jerking the Sleeve: Yanking the sleeve grip without coordinating with hip movement can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears, especially if the opponent resists by pulling back. Instead, use the sleeve grip to control their arm's angle and prevent them from posting.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Knee-on-Mat Drill (0% Resistance): Practice the kneeling base, posture break, and hip drive mechanics without an opponent. Focus on correct body angles and weight distribution. (20 reps)
    2. Partnered Posture Control (25% Resistance): Your partner lies on their back. You practice the collar and sleeve grips, and the posture break. Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the mechanics. (15 reps)
    3. Partnered Leg Peel Drill (50% Resistance): With your partner in closed guard, you practice the kneeling base, posture break, and the initial knee drive. Your partner attempts to keep their guard closed but does not actively resist the pass. (10 reps per side)
    4. Controlled Pass Attempt (75% Resistance): Execute the full Headquarters Pass against a partner who is actively trying to maintain guard but is not defending with advanced techniques. Focus on completing the leg peel and transition. (5 reps per side)
    5. Semi-Live Pass Attempt (90% Resistance): Your partner attempts to defend the pass using basic guard retention. You focus on applying the correct biomechanics to overcome their defenses. (3 rounds, 1 minute each)
    6. Live Rolling (100% Resistance): Attempt the Headquarters Pass during live rolling. Focus on timing, grip fighting, and adapting to your partner's reactions. (5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is in closed guard and you can establish strong collar and sleeve grips.
    • When the opponent's hips are slightly off-center, creating an opening for your knee to enter.
    • When the opponent attempts to shrimp or create space, but their posture is still compromised.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Recovery (Hip Escape): As the attacker drives their knee forward, the defender can shrimp their hips away, creating space to re-establish guard. The attacker must maintain constant pressure and adjust their base.
    • Posture Up and Frame: If the attacker's posture break is weak, the defender can use their arms to frame against the attacker's shoulders or biceps and actively push to regain their upright posture. This negates the attacker's pressure.
    • Spider Guard/Lasso Counter: If the attacker commits too heavily to the pass, the defender can use their sleeve grip to control the attacker's arm and transition to a spider guard or lasso guard, using the control to create sweeps or other passing opportunities.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right knee hurt when I try to do the Headquarters Pass?

    This often happens when you're driving your knee directly into your opponent's hip bone or lower ribs without proper angling. The correct biomechanics involve placing your right knee on the mat slightly behind their hip line, allowing your torso to drive forward and your hips to angle towards their side. This distributes the pressure correctly and avoids direct impact on your knee joint.

    Q: How can I use the Headquarters Pass against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on superior grip fighting and posture control. Instead of relying solely on brute force, use precise collar and sleeve grips to break their posture down significantly. Then, drive your knee towards their hip line, using your body weight and angling to create the space to peel their leg. Prioritize breaking their structure before attempting the pass.

    Q: When should I switch my grips during the Headquarters Pass, or is it always cross-collar and same-side sleeve?

    While cross-collar and same-side sleeve is a common and effective grip combination, you can adapt. If your opponent offers an opposite-side sleeve grip, you can utilize that to control their arm. The key is to always have a grip that allows you to control their posture and prevent them from posting their arm to defend. The objective is control, not a specific grip name.
