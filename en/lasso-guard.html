Lasso Guard: A Precise BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Lasso Guard is an open guard position initiated from bottom, aiming to control one of the opponent's arms with your leg. It creates off-balancing opportunities and sets up various attacks.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From bottom guard, secure a standard closed guard grip (both hands on opponent's collar, or one sleeve and one collar).
    2. As the opponent postures up or attempts to pass, release one collar grip and extend your right leg (if right-handed).
    3. Wrap your right leg over the opponent's left arm, aiming to place the shin across their upper back, just below the shoulder blade.
    4. Simultaneously, grip your own right shin with your left hand to create a strong "lasso" grip, closing the triangle around their arm.
    5. Your left hand should maintain a grip on the opponent's right sleeve or collar, preventing them from posturing fully.
    6. Keep your hips angled towards your left, creating a lever with your lassoed leg.
    7. Drive your hips slightly forward and to your left, pulling their arm across your body and off-balancing them to their right.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect leg placement: Placing the lasso leg too low on the opponent's back or not securing the shin-to-shin connection can lead to the opponent easily removing their arm. This can cause hyperextension of the opponent's shoulder if they forcefully pull away, or strain your own knee ligaments if you maintain a weak grip.
    • Neglecting the secondary grip: Failing to grip your own shin securely with your free hand allows the "lasso" to loosen. This compromises control, and if the opponent pulls their arm out violently, it can lead to wrist sprains or ligament tears in your hand and wrist.
    • Poor hip angle and weight distribution: Keeping your hips square or not creating an angle with your body prevents you from generating leverage. This can lead to your own base collapsing when the opponent applies pressure, risking ankle or knee injuries as you are forced into awkward positions.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the leg wrap and grip acquisition on a dummy or on yourself, focusing on the shin-to-shin connection and securing your own shin grip. 50 reps.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice entering the Lasso Guard from closed guard, focusing on the exact leg and arm movements. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner attempts to posture up. You enter Lasso Guard, focusing on maintaining the lasso grip and hip angle. Partner offers minimal resistance to posture. 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance): Partner attempts to pass. You initiate Lasso Guard and focus on controlling their arm and off-balancing. Partner attempts to create some pressure. 5 reps per side.
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt Lasso Guard entry and control during rolling with a partner who understands you are drilling. Focus on maintaining the position and initiating transitions. 3 rounds, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Integrate Lasso Guard naturally into your rolling. Focus on understanding when the opportunity arises and executing the entry and control. 5 rounds, 90% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Opponent postures up significantly from closed guard, creating space.
    • Opponent attempts a knee-slice or pressure pass, bringing their knee close to your body.
    • Opponent has a deep grip on your collar or shoulders, limiting your mobility.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Opponent stacks: If the opponent attempts to stack you by driving forward, immediately widen your base and drive your hips back. Use your non-lassoed leg to push their hip away, creating space to transition to X-guard or recover guard.
    • Opponent removes arm: If the opponent tries to pull their arm out, maintain a tight grip on your shin and sleeve. Simultaneously, use your hips to shrimp away and bring your other leg up to block their hips, preventing them from creating space.
    • Opponent breaks posture: If the opponent breaks your Lasso grip by driving their elbow down, immediately switch to a spider guard grip or transition to a single leg X-guard by extending your leg under their hip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to grip my own shin in Lasso Guard?

    This often happens because you are gripping your shin with an open palm, leading to wrist extension. Instead, cup your shin with your fingers pointing towards your knee and wrap your thumb around the outside. This creates a more stable grip and reduces strain on the wrist joint, allowing for better leverage.

    Q: How do I maintain Lasso Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a strong frame with your lassoed leg and maintaining a tight grip on your shin. Use your hips to actively push into their arm, preventing them from driving their weight forward. Your secondary grip on their sleeve or collar becomes crucial for controlling their posture and creating angles for sweeps.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Lasso Guard to an attack like a triangle choke?

    The ideal time to transition is when the opponent's arm is trapped tightly, and you have successfully pulled their weight onto that trapped limb. This off-balancing creates an opening. Look for opportunities when they try to relieve pressure by leaning away or attempting to pass, as this is when their posture is most compromised for a submission.
