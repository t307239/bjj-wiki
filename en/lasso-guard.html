Lasso Guard: A Precise BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Lasso Guard originates from open guard, aiming to control one of your opponent's arms and legs simultaneously. It creates a strong entanglement for sweeps and submissions.

White belts often struggle by overextending or creating insufficient frames, leading to loss of control and potential injury. A passive mindset is the enemy here.

The core insight is using your opponent's forward pressure and weight against them, creating leverage through your hip and shoulder connection.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From an open guard position, secure a high collar grip with your right hand on your opponent's gi collar, directly under their chin. Your left hand secures their right sleeve at the wrist.
    2. Swing your right leg up and over your opponent's right arm, placing the inside of your right knee on their right shoulder blade. Your right foot hooks behind their right elbow.
    3. Simultaneously, move your left leg to hook behind your opponent's right knee. Your left foot should be on the outside of their thigh, close to their hip.
    4. Maintain a strong frame with your left arm, pushing against your opponent's right biceps to prevent them from posturing up. Your left forearm should be perpendicular to their arm.
    5. Your right hip should be slightly elevated, creating a wedge between your opponent's shoulder and torso. This off-balances them forward.
    6. With your collar grip, pull your opponent's head down and to your left, creating a tight connection. Your left sleeve grip helps control their arm's angle.
    7. Shift your weight onto your left hip, allowing your right leg to actively pull their arm across your body, further tightening the lasso.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'pull hard' with your collar grip without establishing hip elevation and frame: This can hyperextend your opponent's neck or cause your own shoulder to dislocate if they resist by pushing their head back.
    • Letting your left leg slip from behind your opponent's knee, allowing them to stand up: This exposes your knee to potential hyperextension or ligament damage if they drive forward aggressively.
    • Over-extending your right leg to 'hook' their elbow without proper hip engagement: This can strain your hamstring or cause your knee to buckle if your opponent pushes their arm through.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the leg entanglement and hip elevation without a partner. Focus on the correct placement of your right knee and left foot. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner stands in front of you. Practice establishing the collar and sleeve grips, then entangling your legs. Focus on maintaining frames. (25 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light pressure): Your partner offers minimal forward pressure. Practice the hip elevation and pulling mechanics. (25 reps)
    4. Partner drill (controlled movement): Your partner attempts to posture up or move. Practice maintaining the lasso and creating sweeps. (50 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (strategic attempt): Call 'Lasso' before engaging. Attempt to set up the lasso guard from guard. Focus on transitions and maintaining the entanglement. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full application): Attempt to use Lasso Guard as a primary guard. Focus on sweep execution and submission threats. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up aggressively from your closed guard, creating space to transition to open guard.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard and their arm is extended towards you.
    • When you have established a strong grip on their collar and can control their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stacking Pass Defense: If your opponent attempts to stack you, immediately drive your hips back, creating space. Use your free leg to push their hips away, and consider transitioning to X-guard.
    • Standing Up: If your opponent tries to stand up and break the lasso, maintain your collar grip and use your right leg to control their arm. Drive your hips forward to prevent them from fully straightening their posture.
    • Re-guarding: If the lasso entanglement is broken, immediately bring your knees to your chest and re-establish a strong closed guard or transition to a different open guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do Lasso Guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping the sleeve too tightly with your fingers extended, rather than using a secure fist grip. Ensure your left hand has a firm grasp on their sleeve at the wrist, and use your forearm to create a frame against their biceps. Avoid 'death-gripping' the fabric; focus on control.

    Q: How do I prevent my opponent from stepping over my legs when I attempt Lasso Guard?

    To prevent them from stepping over, ensure your left leg is actively hooked behind their knee, with your foot on the outside of their thigh. Simultaneously, drive your right hip into their shoulder. This creates a strong wedge that makes it biomechanically difficult for them to step over without significant effort.

    Q: Can I use Lasso Guard in No-Gi BJJ?

    Yes, Lasso Guard can be adapted for No-Gi, but it requires different grip strategies. Instead of a collar grip, use a wrist-on-wrist or bicep grip with your right hand on their arm. Your left hand can grip their bicep or triceps. The leg entanglement remains similar, but the lack of fabric makes the grips more slippery and reliant on body positioning.
