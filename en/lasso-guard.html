Lasso Guard: A Biomechanical Breakdown for BJJ White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Lasso Guard originates from open guard, aiming to control one of your opponent's arms and legs simultaneously for sweeps and submissions.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of body awareness and relying on brute force instead of leverage.

The key mechanical insight is using your hip as a fulcrum to create angles and leverage, not just the arm wrap.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or open guard position, establish a closed guard grip on your opponent's right sleeve with your left hand.
    2. With your right arm, reach *under* your opponent's right armpit and grip your own left sleeve (the Lasso grip).
    3. Simultaneously, begin to extend your right leg, placing your right foot on your opponent's hip, angling your hips 45 degrees to your left.
    4. Maintain tension on the sleeve grip with your left hand, pulling their arm across your body.
    5. Use your right leg on their hip to create distance and leverage, preventing them from posturing up.
    6. Your left leg should be positioned to either control their other leg (e.g., by hooking behind their knee) or to initiate a sweep.
    7. Shift your weight slightly onto your left hip, using your right leg as a prop and your Lasso arm as a control point.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping your own sleeve too high and pulling your arm inwards: This strains the shoulder joint's anterior capsule and can lead to rotator cuff tears. The correct action is to maintain a slight bend in your elbow and use your shoulder to create the Lasso, not just your hand.
    • Extending your Lasso leg straight and stiff: This puts immense pressure on your knee joint, risking ligament sprains (ACL/MCL). Keep a slight bend in your knee and use your hip to drive the extension.
    • Attempting to pull your opponent down with only your arms: This overloads your wrists and elbows, risking sprains and tendonitis. Use your hips and legs to generate the primary movement and leverage.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo movement drill (0%): Practice the arm wrap and leg extension motion without a partner, focusing on smooth transitions and hip movement. (5 reps each side)
    2. Partnered setup (25%): Opponent stands passively. You practice establishing the Lasso grip and leg on hip, focusing on the correct angles. (5 reps each side)
    3. Controlled Lasso entry (50%): Opponent offers minimal resistance to your guard entry. You focus on securing the Lasso and controlling their arm. (5 reps each side)
    4. Lasso to Sweep initiation (75%): Opponent attempts to posture or pass lightly. You practice initiating a basic sweep from Lasso (e.g., scissor sweep). (5 reps each side)
    5. Lasso to Submission attempt (90%): Opponent defends passively but allows you to set up a basic submission (e.g., triangle). (3 reps each side)
    6. Live rolling with Lasso focus (100%): Attempt to implement Lasso Guard in rolling, prioritizing control and execution over winning the roll. (5-minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent is postured up and attempting to pass your guard.
    • When opponent is standing and you have established a strong open guard base.
    • When opponent is trying to break your closed guard and you can transition.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posture Up and Grip Break: Opponent steps their hips back, creates a wide base, and uses both hands to break your Lasso grip by "shucking" your arm off their shoulder. They then drive forward.
    • Knee Slice Pass: Opponent drives their knee across your body, aiming to cut through your guard while maintaining pressure. You must actively use your Lasso leg to impede their progress and your free leg to create space.
    • Stacking Pass: Opponent grips your hips and drives their weight forward, attempting to stack you. You must extend your Lasso leg to create space and prevent the stack, potentially transitioning to a different guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to make the Lasso Guard grip?

    Wrist pain in Lasso Guard often stems from gripping your own sleeve too tightly with a pronated (palm down) wrist, especially when pulling. To fix this, aim for a neutral wrist position. When you grip your sleeve, keep your palm facing towards your body and your elbow slightly bent. The leverage should come from your shoulder and the extension of your leg, not a death grip on your own arm.

    Q: How do I maintain Lasso Guard against a much bigger opponent who is heavy?

    Against a heavier opponent, leverage and angles are paraMount. Do not try to 'pull' them down. Instead, use your Lasso leg on their hip to create a strong frame and angle your hips away from them (45 degrees). This disrupts their base and makes it harder for them to apply their weight directly. Focus on controlling their posture with the Lasso arm and using your legs to create space for sweeps, rather than direct strength contests.

    Q: When is the right time to switch from closed guard to Lasso Guard?

    The opportune moment to transition to Lasso Guard from Closed Guard is typically when your opponent attempts to posture up or break your closed guard by driving their weight forward. As they create space to stand or separate their legs, you can use that moment to insert your Lasso leg, simultaneously releasing one of your closed guard hooks to free the leg for the Lasso. This transition should be fluid, not a forced movement.
