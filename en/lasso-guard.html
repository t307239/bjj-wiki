Lasso Guard is an open guard position where one leg wraps around the opponent's arm, creating a control point. It aims to restrict their posture and set up sweeps or submissions from a distance.

White belts often struggle because they lack the understanding of how to create structure and leverage with their hips and core. They tend to rely on raw strength, which is inefficient and risky.

The core insight is using your hips to drive into the opponent's shoulder, creating a fulcrum with your leg that dictates their upper body movement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated open guard position, establish a cross-collar grip (right hand grips opponent's left collar) and a sleeve grip (left hand grips opponent's right sleeve).
    2. Shift your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's hips.
    3. Bring your right leg up, aiming to place the outside of your right thigh just above the opponent's right elbow.
    4. Simultaneously, initiate a strong hip escape to your left, driving your hips forward and into the opponent's chest.
    5. As your hips drive forward, actively wrap your right leg around the opponent's right arm, ensuring your shin is behind their elbow and your foot is pointing towards their head.
    6. Secure the Lasso grip by crossing your right foot over your left shin (or gripping your own shin/ankle with your right hand for added control).
    7. Maintain tension in your right leg by pushing your heel away from your body, while keeping your left foot planted on their hip for base and mobility.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Valgus Collapse: Attempting to secure the Lasso by forcing your knee inward with your leg already around the opponent's arm. This can cause the medial collateral ligament (MCL) in the knee to stretch or tear due to excessive valgus stress.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Gripping your own shin or ankle too tightly while your opponent is posturing heavily, causing your elbow to flare out and your shoulder joint to be compressed. This can lead to rotator cuff strain or labral tears.
    • Lower Back Strain: Hunching your back and pulling with your arms to create the Lasso, instead of using hip drive. This places extreme flexion and shear forces on the lumbar spine, risking disc herniation or muscle pulls.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Execution (0% resistance): Practice the hip escape and leg wrap motion without an opponent. Focus on the hip angle and leg placement. (20 reps)
    2. Dummy/Pillow Drill (0% resistance): Use a dummy or large pillow to practice securing the Lasso grip and maintaining leg tension. (20 reps)
    3. Partner Static Guard (25% resistance): Partner lies on their back. Practice entering Lasso Guard and securing the grip, with partner offering minimal resistance to your hip movement. (10 reps per side)
    4. Partner Light Guard Retention (50% resistance): Partner attempts to posture up slightly. Focus on maintaining Lasso tension and using your free leg to control distance. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partner Controlled Guard Pass Attempt (75% resistance): Partner attempts a very slow, controlled guard pass. Practice holding the Lasso and using it to impede their movement, focusing on hip drive and leg tension. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling - Entry Focus (90% resistance): In rolling, focus solely on successfully entering the Lasso Guard from a neutral or closed guard position. Do not attempt submissions or sweeps yet. (3 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent is postured up and trying to pass your guard.
    • When opponent is close and attempting to establish a strong base.
    • When you have achieved a strong closed guard and are transitioning to an open guard control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Postural Reset: Opponent breaks the Lasso grip by stepping their trapped arm across their body towards their opposite hip, then driving their elbow back to create space. This requires immediate hip adjustment and potentially switching to De La Riva.
    • Arm-Drag Entry: Opponent uses the Lasso to control their arm, then you use your free hand to grab their sleeve on the same side as the Lasso leg and perform an arm-drag, pulling their arm across their body to transition to a back take or sweep.
    • Stack Pass Attempt: Opponent attempts to 'stack' you by driving their weight directly down onto your hips. You counter by pushing off their hips with your free foot and using your Lasso leg to create a frame, then attempting a sweep like a scissor sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to do the Lasso Guard?

    Your shoulder is likely hurting because you are over-relying on pulling with your arms to create the Lasso effect, instead of using your hips and core to drive into your opponent. This causes your shoulder joint to become compressed and strained. Focus on pushing your hips forward and creating a 'fulcrum' with your Lasso leg, rather than pulling your opponent's arm towards you. Ensure your elbow stays tucked to your side when securing the grip.

    Q: How can I use Lasso Guard against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, the key is to use your hips and leg leverage to disrupt their base and posture. Instead of trying to pull them down, focus on driving your hips forward and creating a frame with your Lasso leg against their shoulder and bicep. Use your free leg to maintain distance and control their hips, preventing them from 'stacking' you. Aim to off-balance them and initiate sweeps by using their size against them.

    Q: The optimal time to transition from Closed Guard to Lasso Guard is when your opponent is posturing up significantly, attempting to break your guard or establish a strong base to pass. If they are too low and their weight is directly on your hips, it's harder to set up the Lasso. Look for the moment they create space by posturing, allowing you to perform the hip escape and leg wrap effectively.
