Lasso Guard: A Biomechanically Accurate Guide for BJJ White Belts

Lasso Guard is an open guard position where one leg wraps around the opponent's arm, creating a strong control point. It aims to disrupt their posture and create submission opportunities or sweeps.

White belts often struggle because they lack the understanding of frames and leverage, leading to uncontrolled limb entanglement. They might also grip too high or too low, compromising the structure.

The key mechanical insight is using your hip and leg to create a wedge, isolating the opponent's arm while maintaining a strong base with your other leg and core.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From guard, establish a collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left arm) with your left hand. Your opponent is in front of you, knees bent.
    2. Initiate a slight hip escape to your left, creating space. Your left knee should track towards your left shoulder.
    3. Swing your right leg up and over your opponent's left arm. Your shin should be parallel to the mat, across their upper back or shoulder blade.
    4. Simultaneously, grip your own right shin or ankle with your right hand, creating a "lasso" effect. This secures the arm isolation.
    5. Use your left leg to hook your opponent's right hip or thigh, maintaining distance and preventing them from passing.
    6. Keep your hips elevated slightly off the mat, actively pushing into your opponent's chest with your left foot's sole (if applicable) or shin.
    7. Your right leg acts as a lever; the deeper the wrap around their arm, the more control you have over their posture and arm.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping the opponent's biceps too tightly with your lasso leg: This can lead to a shoulder impingement injury (rotator cuff strain) if the opponent forcefully pulls their arm out or applies pressure. The correct movement is to maintain a grip on your own shin/ankle, using the leg to hug the arm without excessive direct pressure on the opponent's bicep.
    • Allowing your hips to drop flat to the mat while in Lasso Guard: This compromises your base and can lead to a knee injury (ACL/MCL strain) if the opponent stacks you or drives forward aggressively. Maintain an elevated hip position, actively pushing against the opponent's hips or chest.
    • Extending your lasso arm fully without a solid frame: This leaves your elbow vulnerable to hyperextension injuries if the opponent attempts a pass or submission. Keep a slight bend in your elbow and use your other hand to frame on their hip or knee.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice establishing the Lasso Guard grip and leg wrap on a dummy or training partner's stationary arm (0% resistance). 10 reps.
    2. Solo drill: Practice hip escapes and bringing the lasso leg over the arm, focusing on the shin position (0% resistance). 10 reps.
    3. Partner drill: With a compliant partner, practice establishing the Lasso Guard and holding the position, focusing on grip security and leg placement (25% resistance). 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drill: Practice moving from a closed guard or half guard into the Lasso Guard, with the partner offering minimal resistance to the transition (50% resistance). 5 reps per side.
    5. Partner drill: Practice basic Lasso Guard sweeps (e.g., pendulum sweep) against a partner who is resisting the sweep attempt but not actively passing (75% resistance). 5 reps per side.
    6. Live rolling: Integrate Lasso Guard attempts into rolling, focusing on setting it up and maintaining control, accepting that passes will happen as you learn (90% resistance). 3 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is postured up and reaching over your legs to pass.
    • When you have established a strong closed guard and are looking to transition to an open guard control.
    • When the opponent is heavy on top, and you need to create space and control their arm to prevent a pass.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Opponent posts their free hand on your hip and drives forward: Maintain your lasso grip, lower your hips slightly, and use your free leg to push their hip away. This creates a frame to prevent the stack.
    • Opponent pulls their trapped arm out forcefully: Immediately transition your lasso leg to their thigh or hip to maintain control, or switch to a different guard like Spider Guard or De La Riva Guard.
    • Opponent attempts to step their leg over your hips to pass: Use your free leg to hook their passing leg, and use your lasso arm to control their shoulder, preventing them from completing the pass.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to grip my shin in Lasso Guard?

    This usually happens because you're gripping your shin too far down, forcing your wrist into an extreme extension. Instead, aim to grip higher up on your shin or even your ankle. This allows for a more neutral wrist position and better leverage, reducing strain and improving your ability to control the opponent's arm.

    Q: How can I use Lasso Guard effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on using your hips and core to create angles and leverage, rather than pure strength. Keep your lasso leg tight around their arm and use your free leg to control their hips or base. Your goal is to disrupt their weight distribution and prevent them from using their size effectively, creating openings for sweeps or submissions.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Closed Guard to Lasso Guard?

    The ideal time to transition is when the opponent is postured up and reaching over your legs, attempting to break your guard or create a passing lane. This is when their arm is most exposed. Initiate a Hip Escape to create space, then swing your leg over to establish the lasso, simultaneously using your other leg to control their hips.
