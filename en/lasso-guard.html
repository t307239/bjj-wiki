Lasso Guard: A White Belt's Introduction to BJJ Control

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

Lasso Guard originates from an open guard position, aiming to entangle one of the opponent's arms and control their posture for sweeps or submissions.

Many white belts struggle by over-relying on strength or incorrect framing, leading to an easily broken guard and potential injury.

The core principle is using your leg to create a constant, dynamic lever against their arm, limiting their mobility and setting up attacks.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or shin-on-shin open guard, secure a deep collar grip with your non-lassoing arm, pulling their posture towards you.
    2. With your lassoing leg, clear their arm and position your shin *across* the front of their biceps, perpendicular to their arm's length.
    3. Simultaneously, wrap your lassoing foot *behind* their elbow and hook it firmly, creating a figure-four grip with your leg around their arm.
    4. Establish a grip on their sleeve or wrist with your lassoing hand, keeping your elbow tucked to your chest to maintain tension.
    5. Drive your hips back slightly, creating space and maintaining the tension on their trapped arm via the leg and hand grip.
    6. Use your free leg to create a base, either posting your foot on their hip or maintaining a closed guard structure to prevent them from easily passing.
    7. Shift your weight to your hips, using the lasso to pull their trapped arm across their body, forcing an off-balance posture.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your lassoing leg: This can hyperextend the opponent's elbow, risking ligament tears (e.g., UCL) in their arm.
    • Allowing the opponent to step over your lassoing leg: This exposes your knee and can lead to knee-on-belly transitions or knee bar attempts against you.
    • Gripping the opponent's gi too loosely with your lassoing hand: This allows them to easily pull their arm out, breaking the lasso and leaving you vulnerable to passes.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of leg positioning and foot hook: 10 repetitions per leg, 0% resistance.
    2. Solo drilling of grip acquisition and leg entanglement: 10 repetitions per side, 0% resistance.
    3. Partner drilling: Entry into Lasso Guard with no opponent reaction (simulated opponent), 10 repetitions per side, 25% resistance.
    4. Partner drilling: Maintaining Lasso Guard against a static opponent who attempts to posture up, 5 minutes, 50% resistance.
    5. Partner drilling: Transitioning from Lasso Guard to a sweep (e.g., scissor sweep) against a resisting opponent, 5 minutes, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling: Attempting to establish and utilize Lasso Guard during sparring, 3 x 5 minute rounds, 90% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is posturing up and their arm is within reach, creating an opportunity to trap it.
    • When the opponent attempts a guard pass and their arm is exposed, allowing for a reactive lasso.
    • When you have established a strong seated guard and can control the distance to set up the entanglement.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass: The opponent drives their weight forward, compressing your guard and attempting to pass by driving their chest into your hips.
    • Standing Up: The opponent stands up and uses their weight and grip to break the lasso and pass.
    • Arm Pull-Out: The opponent uses a strong grip on their trapped arm and forward pressure to pull it out of the entanglement.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to set up Lasso Guard?

    Your knee might hurt if you are rotating your femur incorrectly or if your opponent is pushing into your leg with significant pressure. Ensure your lassoing leg's hip is externally rotated, and your shin is perpendicular to their arm. Avoid letting your knee collapse inwards; instead, keep your shin bone the primary point of contact, not the kneecap itself.

    Q: How do I keep my Lasso Guard tight against a bigger opponent who tries to step out?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on establishing a strong collar grip to pull them into your guard and create the initial entanglement. Once the lasso is set, use your hips to drive *towards* the trapped arm, creating a lever. Your free leg should be actively posting on their hip to control distance and prevent them from easily stepping out.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Lasso Guard to an attack?

    The optimal time to transition is when the opponent’s posture is broken and their trapped arm is pulled across their body, creating an imbalance. This is when they are most vulnerable to sweeps like the scissor sweep or submissions like the Triangle Choke. Continuously monitor their posture and react to opportunities as they arise.
