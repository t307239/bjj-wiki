The Lasso Guard is an open guard position where one leg wraps around the opponent's arm and over their shoulder, creating a controlling "lasso" effect.

White belts often struggle with Lasso Guard due to a lack of understanding of leverage and a tendency to rely on brute strength, leading to poor positioning and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is using the wrapped leg as a lever to control the opponent's posture and create openings, rather than simply trying to hold them down.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a closed guard or butterfly guard, establish a sleeve grip with your left hand on the opponent's right sleeve, palm facing your body.
    2. Simultaneously, with your right leg, step your foot over the opponent's right shoulder, aiming to place it high on their upper back or neck area.
    3. As your right leg swings over, use your left hand to grip the opponent's right elbow, keeping their arm bent at approximately 90 degrees.
    4. Immediately transition your left hand from the sleeve to grip your own right shin or ankle, creating a "figure-four" grip with your right arm and left leg.
    5. Tilt your hips to your left by lifting your left hip off the mat, creating a strong base and pulling the opponent's arm across your body.
    6. Extend your right leg slightly to create upward pressure, keeping the opponent's arm trapped and their posture broken.
    7. Maintain a strong connection with your grips and keep your core engaged to control their upper body rotation and prevent them from posturing up.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive inward pressure with your right leg when the opponent is postured up: This can hyperextend the opponent's elbow joint, potentially tearing the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) or causing a sprain.
    • Forcing the left sleeve grip too high on the opponent's arm: This can lead to your own wrist being twisted unnaturally, risking a sprain or even a fracture.
    • Allowing the opponent to stack you by not maintaining hip angle: This can lead to spinal compression or disc injury if the opponent drives their weight downwards without proper base.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the leg and arm movement to achieve the Lasso Guard position without resistance (5 reps each side).
    2. Partner drill (static): With a compliant partner, practice establishing the Lasso Guard grips and body position, holding for 5 seconds (10 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (controlled movement): Practice moving your hips and creating leverage while maintaining the Lasso Guard, with the partner offering minimal resistance to posture up (10 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    4. Transition drill: Practice transitioning into Lasso Guard from closed guard and then attempting a sweep or submission (e.g., triangle choke), with the partner defending passively (15 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    5. Flow drilling: Practice chaining Lasso Guard entries and sweeps with a partner who is actively but not aggressively defending (20 reps total, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Implement the Lasso Guard during sparring, focusing on establishing the position and working for sweeps or submissions (5-minute rounds, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is postured up and controlling your hips from open guard.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard by stepping their legs close to your hips.
    • When you have established a strong sleeve grip and can control the opponent's arm.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stacking Pass: The opponent can try to "stack" you by driving their weight forward and down. To counter, maintain your hip angle and extend your Lasso leg to create space, then transition to a sweep or submission.
    • Arm Escape: The opponent can attempt to pull their trapped arm out. To counter, tighten your Lasso grip and use your hips to drive their arm across your body, preventing them from straightening it.
    • Posture Up: The opponent can try to regain their posture. To counter, use your Lasso leg and grips to pull their arm across your body and break their posture down, then use your hips to create angles for attacks.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to grip my shin in Lasso Guard?

    Your wrist is likely hurting because you are gripping your shin with an extended or flexed wrist, putting undue stress on the joint. Ensure your wrist remains neutral or slightly flexed, gripping your shin with the pads of your fingers. Focus on keeping your forearm parallel to the mat and engaging your bicep to pull your shin towards you, rather than just bending your wrist.

    Q: How can I use Lasso Guard effectively against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on using your Lasso leg to control their posture by pulling their trapped arm across your body and using your hips to create off-balancing angles. Instead of trying to lift them, use the Lasso to break their base and create openings for sweeps like the technical stand-up or a sweep that utilizes their forward momentum against them.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from closed guard to Lasso Guard?

    The optimal time to transition to Lasso Guard from Closed Guard is when the opponent is attempting to open your guard or is posturing up significantly. Look for moments when they extend their arms to create space or attempt to step their legs inside your guard. This is when their arm is most vulnerable to being trapped and controlled by the Lasso.
