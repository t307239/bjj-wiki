This sweep originates from Closed Guard and aims to invert the opponent's base, allowing you to establish a dominant top position. White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage and hip mechanics.

The key insight is using your hips and core to create an unbalancing moment, rather than relying solely on upper body strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong closed guard grip: Right hand controls opponent's left sleeve (e.g., fist grip at the wrist), left hand controls opponent's left collar (e.g., thumb-in grip below the lapel). Maintain a tight guard by squeezing knees together.
    2. Shift your hips to your right: Angle your pelvis approximately 45 degrees to your right, creating space under your left hip.
    3. Underhook opponent's left arm: Slide your right arm (the one controlling the sleeve) under their left armpit, securing a grip on their far shoulder blade or upper back.
    4. Wedge your left shin across opponent's left hip bone: Ensure your left knee is pointed slightly towards the ceiling and your shin is perpendicular to their body.
    5. Initiate the sweep by driving your hips up and to your left: Simultaneously, pull their left arm across your body with your right arm and push their left leg away with your wedged left shin.
    6. As their weight shifts, rotate your body counter-clockwise: Your goal is to bring your left shoulder towards the mat while your hips drive upwards and over.
    7. Immediately transition to mount or side control: As your hips clear their base, drive your left knee to the mat and your right knee towards their hip to secure a dominant top position. Maintain control of their collar and sleeve.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly lifting opponent's arm: Attempting to pull their arm straight up with only arm strength can strain the shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint), potentially leading to tears or dislocations.
    • Jamming knee directly into opponent's hip: Driving your knee bone directly into their hip bone can cause knee cap pain or ligament strain if their body is not properly positioned.
    • Overextending the back during the hip drive: Arching the lower back excessively while trying to generate power can lead to lumbar strain or disc compression.
    • Releasing grips prematurely: Letting go of the collar or sleeve grip before the opponent's base is fully broken will allow them to recover their balance, negating the sweep and potentially leaving you in a vulnerable position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of hip movement and shin placement: Practice shifting your hips and wedging your shin without an opponent. 10 repetitions each side.
    2. Partner drill: Static position, focus on grip acquisition and hip angle. Partner offers minimal resistance. 5 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner drill: Controlled sweep attempt with light resistance. Focus on the sequence and body mechanics. Partner allows the sweep to happen but resists slightly. 5 repetitions per side.
    4. Partner drill: Increasing resistance. Partner actively tries to defend the sweep. Focus on timing and power generation. 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Situational sparring: Start from closed guard, allow opponent to attempt a pass. Execute the Overhead Sweep as a defensive counter. 3 minutes.
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Overhead Sweep into your game. Focus on creating opportunities and executing the sweep when the timing is right. 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating a space to attack their base.
    • Opponent is attempting to break your guard by stacking or pressuring forward.
    • Opponent has their weight distributed forward, making them susceptible to an inversion.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If the sweep is initiated but not completed, immediately hip escape to regain a strong closed guard or transition to an open guard.
    • Stack Pass Defense: If the opponent attempts to stack you during the sweep, drive your hips into their chest and drive your legs upwards to create space, then attempt to pass.
    • Leg Over the Head: As the sweep is executed, if the opponent's leg comes over your head, use your free hand to control their ankle and transition to a leg lock or sweep from that position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try the Overhead Sweep in BJJ?

    Your lower back likely hurts because you are overextending your lumbar spine to generate power. Instead, focus on driving your hips upward and forward using your glutes and core. Imagine scooping your opponent over with your hips, not lifting them with your back.

    Q: How can I perform the Overhead Sweep in BJJ against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise timing are crucial. Focus on getting your shin deep across their hip bone and wedging yourself tightly. Use your grip to pull their upper body weight towards you as you drive your hips up and over. The key is to break their base with a strong unbalancing moment, not brute force.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Overhead Sweep in BJJ from closed guard?

    The optimal time to use the Overhead Sweep is when your opponent is postured up and their weight is distributed forward, creating an opportunity to invert their base. If they are too low or too far back, the sweep will be less effective and harder to execute. Look for the moment they are actively trying to pressure into your guard.
