Beginner's Guide to the BJJ Ankle Lock

BLUE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Ankle Lock is a submission that targets the ankle joint, primarily through hyperextension or hyperflexion. It can be applied from various positions, often initiated when an opponent is attempting to pass your guard or escape a compromised position.

White belts often struggle with the Ankle Lock due to a lack of understanding of joint mechanics and a tendency to rely on brute strength. This leads to inefficient application and increased risk of injury to both parties.

The key mechanical insight is to isolate the ankle and use your body as a lever, creating a controlled rotation and extension rather than a forceful pull. This maximizes pressure on the opponent's ankle while minimizing strain on your own body.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure a "figure-four" grip around the opponent's ankle. Your forearm (the one closer to their foot) should be cupped around the Achilles tendon. Your other hand grips your own forearm, creating a solid handle.
    2. Body Position: Lie on your back, perpendicular to your opponent's leg. Your hips should be elevated off the mat, creating space for leverage. Your chest should be driving into their thigh.
    3. Leg Isolation: Your non-gripping leg should be extended and pressuring their hip or thigh, preventing them from turning their body away. This isolates the attacking leg.
    4. Weight Transfer: Begin to sit up, transferring your body weight onto your hips and into the opponent's thigh. This anchors your position and applies pressure.
    5. Leverage Creation: Simultaneously, pull your figure-four grip towards your chest while extending your hips upwards. This creates the bending force on the ankle.
    6. Ankle Rotation: Gently rotate your hips outwards (away from the opponent's body) while maintaining the grip and hip extension. This motion specifically targets the ankle's ligaments.
    7. Finishing Pressure: Continue the controlled hip extension and outward rotation until the opponent taps. The pressure should feel like a controlled, uncomfortable stretch in their ankle, not a violent wrench.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Grip & Force Application: Grabbing the foot or shin and pulling upwards forcefully. This can damage the opponent's knee ligaments (ACL/MCL) by causing excessive internal rotation or valgus stress, and your own shoulder by creating a shearing force.
    • Excessive Hip Extension without Isolation: Arching your back and extending your hips aggressively without properly isolating the opponent's leg and controlling their hips. This can lead to hyperextension or hyperflexion of the opponent's knee, damaging the ACL or PCL, and also strain your own lower back.
    • Allowing Opponent to Turn: Not maintaining control of the opponent's hips or base, allowing them to turn their body into the lock. This can cause the opponent's ankle to twist unnaturally, potentially tearing ligaments like the ATFL or PTFL, and can also lead to your own knee or hip being compromised if you're pulled into an awkward position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip Practice: Practice the figure-four grip on your own leg, focusing on hand placement and forearm engagement. (50 reps per side).
    2. Body Position Drill: Lie on your back and practice achieving the perpendicular body position with hip elevation and chest pressure. (25 reps).
    3. Static Isolation Drill: With a partner, achieve the Ankle Lock position and hold the grip while they resist slightly (0% resistance). Focus on maintaining hip angle and leg isolation. (10 reps per side, hold for 10 seconds).
    4. Controlled Sweep and Lock: Practice transitioning from a sweep to the Ankle Lock position, applying the grip and initial hip extension. Partner offers minimal resistance (25% resistance). (5 reps per side).
    5. Live Rolling - Defensive Focus: During light sparring, actively look for opportunities to set up the Ankle Lock, but prioritize maintaining good form and avoiding excessive force. Focus on the setup and initial application. (3 rounds, 50% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling - Submission Focus: During light sparring, attempt to finish the Ankle Lock with controlled pressure. Tap early if you feel you've compromised your position or the opponent's safety. (5 rounds, 75% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg is extended in front of you.
    • When you achieve a dominant position like half-guard or closed guard and can isolate an opponent's leg.
    • During scrambles where an opponent exposes their ankle while trying to escape a submission or position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Pancake" Defense: If the attacker has your ankle, immediately flatten your body to the mat, aligning your hips with theirs. This removes the leverage needed to extend your ankle and makes it difficult for them to sit up. Keep your free leg tucked to prevent them from isolating it.
    • The "Turn-In" Escape: If the attacker has your ankle but hasn't fully isolated your leg, attempt to turn your body into the attacker, bringing your hips closer to them. This can relieve pressure and allow you to free your ankle or even transition to a better position.
    • The "Stomp and Explode" (Advanced/Risky): In certain situations, if the attacker's grip is not fully secure, you might be able to stomp your free foot down hard on the mat to create a sudden burst of momentum. This can help you pull your ankle free or even shift their weight, potentially creating an opportunity for you to escape. This requires precise timing and is risky for both parties.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do an Ankle Lock?

    The pain you're feeling is likely not from the opponent's submission, but from your own body's incorrect mechanics. If you're grabbing too high on the shin or pulling with your arms instead of using your hips and body weight, you're creating a shearing force on your own ankle ligaments. Focus on isolating the opponent's ankle with a figure-four grip and using your hips to create the extension, not your arms.

    Q: How do I Ankle Lock a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Do not attempt to out-muscle them. Focus on achieving a clean isolation of their ankle, ensuring their hips are not free to turn. Your body position should be perpendicular, and you must use your entire body weight, driving your hips up and rotating them outwards to create the lever. A strong figure-four grip is crucial for maintaining control.

    Q: When is the right time to go for an Ankle Lock instead of a different submission?

    The Ankle Lock is best applied when an opponent is actively trying to pass your guard or escape a dominant position, as this often leaves their legs exposed and less mobile. Look for moments when their weight is forward, and you can isolate a Single Leg. It's also a good option when you have a solid base and control, preventing them from turning into you or easily escaping the lock.
