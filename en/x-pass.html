X-Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The X-Pass is a guard passing technique initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a Closed Guard, aiming to establish side control or knee-on-belly.

White belts often struggle by trying to muscle through the guard or by not understanding the precise hip and shoulder mechanics needed to break the leg entanglement.

The core mechanical insight is creating a strong base and using your hips to drive *through* the opponent's legs, rather than trying to lift or pull them apart.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent is in closed guard. Grip both of their ankles with your hands, thumbs inside the cuffs of their gi pants.
    2. Post your left knee on the mat, slightly behind and to the left of their hip, creating a base.
    3. Drive your right knee forward, aiming to place it on the mat in line with their torso, creating an 'X' shape with your legs.
    4. Simultaneously, drive your hips forward and down, keeping your chest low and close to their body.
    5. Use your ankles grips to pull their legs slightly apart as your hips drive forward, breaking the closed guard.
    6. Shift your weight onto your right knee and left foot base, bringing your left knee forward.
    7. Once the guard is broken, secure a cross-face grip with your right hand on their head/neck and your left hand on their far hip to transition to side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grasping the opponent's ankles too loosely: This allows them to easily pull their feet out, compromising your base and potentially leading to a sweep attempt.
    • Incorrect knee placement: Driving the right knee too far forward or too wide can hyper-extend your knee joint or allow the opponent to easily hook your leg, risking ACL or meniscus injury.
    • Trying to 'muscle' the guard open: This engages insufficient musculature and puts excessive strain on your lower back and hips, risking sprains or disc issues.
    • Not committing the hips: Failing to drive the hips forward leaves your base unstable, making you susceptible to sweeps and risking ankle sprains from awkward foot positions.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation (0%): Practice the hip drive and knee placement motion without a partner. Focus on the 'X' shape with your legs and hip movement. (20 reps)
    2. Partner Assisted (25%): With a compliant partner, have them maintain a loose closed guard. Practice grip acquisition, hip drive, and knee placement. They offer minimal resistance. (15 reps per side)
    3. Controlled Entry (50%): Partner maintains a slightly tighter guard. Focus on breaking the ankles and driving hips forward to break the guard. Partner offers light resistance to the leg break. (10 reps per side)
    4. Guard Break Focus (75%): Partner actively tries to maintain closed guard. You focus solely on breaking the guard using the X-Pass mechanics. Partner may resist slightly more with leg pressure. (8 reps per side)
    5. Pass Execution (90%): Execute the X-Pass and immediately transition to side control. Partner offers moderate resistance to the pass. (6 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling (100%): Attempt the X-Pass during live rolling. Focus on correct timing and mechanics, accepting that resistance will be high. (Focus on 3 attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent has a tight, high closed guard.
    • Opponent attempts to create space by extending their legs.
    • Opponent's hips are close to yours, limiting their sweeping angles.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Overhook the far leg: As the opponent drives their hips, secure an overhook on their far leg and use your shoulder to drive their hip away, creating space to recover guard.
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If the pass is initiated poorly, immediately hip escape and re-establish your guard, potentially transitioning to a different guard.
    • Knee Shield: If the opponent's knee comes too close to your face, use your shin as a knee shield to create distance and prevent the pass, then look to shrimp out.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the X-Pass?

    Knee pain during the X-Pass usually stems from improper knee placement or excessive internal rotation. When driving your right knee forward, ensure it tracks over your foot and doesn't collapse inward. Avoid letting your knee go past your toes. If you feel pain, immediately stop and reassess your hip drive and base, ensuring your weight is distributed through your hips and not solely on your knee joint.

    Q: How can I X-Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, the X-Pass relies even more heavily on precise biomechanics and less on strength. Focus on controlling their ankles *very* securely with your grips, ensuring no slack. Your hip drive must be explosive and aimed directly *through* their center of mass, not just against their legs. Use your chest to pin their hips down as you drive forward, preventing them from using their weight to resist effectively.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an X-Pass from open guard?

    The X-Pass is primarily a closed Guard Pass. While some open guard entries can mimic aspects, a true X-Pass starts with the opponent's legs wrapped around your hips. If you are in open guard and they move to close your guard, that's your cue to initiate the X-Pass mechanics. Timing is crucial; attempting it when they are already settled or actively attacking from open guard is less effective.
