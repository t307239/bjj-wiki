The X-Pass is a guard pass initiated from a standing or kneeling position aiming to bypass an opponent's Closed Guard by attacking their legs from the side.

White belts often struggle because they lack the precise hip and shoulder control, leading to predictable movements and easily defended attempts.

The core mechanic is creating a strong base while simultaneously isolating and manipulating one leg to create an opening for your body to pass.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start in a kneeling or staggered stance, facing your opponent who is in closed guard. Secure a collar grip with your right hand, palm facing down, on their left collar, and a sleeve grip with your left hand on their right sleeve.
    2. Drive your left knee forward, placing it outside your opponent's right hip, creating a base width of approximately shoulder-width apart.
    3. Rotate your torso 45 degrees to your right, bringing your right hip slightly forward and your left shoulder back, establishing an angled base.
    4. Use your collar grip to pull your opponent's upper body slightly towards your left side, breaking their posture and creating tension.
    5. With your left hand, grip your opponent's right pant leg just above the ankle, ensuring a firm hold.
    6. Drive your right knee towards the mat, sliding it along the outside of your opponent's right leg, aiming to get your knee past their hip line.
    7. Simultaneously, use your collar grip to maintain the pull, and your pant grip to control their ankle, then drive your hips forward and down, pushing their right leg away and to your right.
    8. As their right leg is pushed away, your body pivots on your left knee and right foot, allowing your right leg to swing across their body and establish side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attacking with your head down and forward: This exposes your cervical spine to potential hyperextension or flexion injuries, especially if the opponent attempts a guillotine choke or a sweep. The correct movement involves keeping your head up and looking towards your opponent's hips.
    • Gripping too loosely or incorrectly on the pant leg: A weak pant grip allows the opponent to easily retract their leg. This can result in your hand slipping, potentially causing a wrist sprain from sudden, uncontrolled movement. Always grip the pant cuff or ankle area firmly.
    • Driving your passing knee directly forward without angling your hips: This can lead to knee valgus stress (knee collapsing inward) if the opponent resists with their legs, potentially damaging the MCL. The correct technique requires a hip angle and a driving motion that clears the opponent's leg.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Movement Drill: Practice the hip angle, knee placement, and leg extension motion without a partner. Focus on smooth transitions between each step. (5 minutes, 0% resistance).
    2. Static Guard Pass: Opponent remains passive, holding closed guard. Practice securing grips, establishing your base, and executing the hip-forward drive. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    3. Passive Guard Pass with Control: Opponent offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice controlling their hips and legs as you pass. Focus on maintaining your base. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    4. Controlled Resistance Guard Pass: Opponent actively tries to maintain their guard but doesn't actively counter-sweep or submit. Focus on breaking posture and clearing the legs. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    5. Light Sparring (Guard Passing Focus): Spar with a partner focusing solely on passing the guard. The opponent can attempt to maintain guard and lightly resist. (3 rounds x 3 minutes, 90% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling (Guard Passing Focus): Incorporate the X-Pass into regular sparring rounds, aiming to use it when the opportunity arises. (5 rounds x 5 minutes, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's hips are relatively flat on the mat and they are not actively creating frames with their forearms.
    • When the opponent attempts to close their guard tightly, creating a predictable structure to target.
    • When you have established a strong grip on their collar and can use it to pull them slightly off balance.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The Knee-Shield/Leg Frame: As the passer drives their knee forward, the defender can extend their opposite leg (the one not being attacked) to create a strong frame between their hips and the passer's body, preventing the passer from clearing the leg.
    • The Re-Guard/Shrimping: If the passer's hip drive is weak, the defender can shrimp their hips away, creating space to re-establish guard or transition to a different position.
    • The Sit-Up and Posture Up: As the passer attempts to drive their knee past, the defender can use their arms to push away the passer's head and shoulders while simultaneously sit-up, breaking the passer's control and potentially creating an opportunity to attack.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the X-Pass?

    Your knee pain likely stems from driving your passing knee directly forward without sufficient hip angle and rotation. This creates excessive shear force on the knee joint, particularly the ACL and meniscus. To fix this, ensure you are rotating your hips 45 degrees and driving your knee *past* the opponent's hip line, not directly into it. Think of your knee as a rudder guiding your body through the pass, not a battering ram.

    Q: How can I X-Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are key. Focus heavily on breaking their posture with your collar grip *before* initiating the pass. Ensure your base is wide and stable, and drive your hips forward with explosive power once you have control of their leg. Do not try to muscle through; instead, use their weight against them by creating angles and using your hip drive to unbalance them.

    Q: When is the best time to switch from an X-Pass attempt to another pass?

    The ideal time to switch is when you feel significant resistance to your primary X-Pass mechanics, such as the opponent successfully framing or extending their leg strongly. If you've driven your knee forward and their leg is still securely trapped, but you can't get your body past, it's time to reassess. Look for opportunities to transition to a knee-slice or pressure pass if they have defended the leg clear, or even reset if they've created a strong counter.
