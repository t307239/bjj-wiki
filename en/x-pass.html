X-Pass Technique Guide for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The X-Pass is a guard passing technique initiated from a standing or kneeling position, aiming to bypass an opponent's Closed Guard and secure side control.

White belts often struggle by applying excessive force or incorrect body angles, leading to ineffective passes and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is establishing a strong leg overhook and using hip pressure to collapse the opponent's base, not brute strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a kneeling or staggered stance, secure a deep cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (same side) with your left hand.
    2. Place your left knee on the mat inside your opponent's right knee, creating a "figure four" leg entanglement with your opponent's legs.
    3. Your right leg should be extended, hip hinged, with your foot planted outside your opponent's left hip, establishing your X-frame.
    4. Drive your hips forward and down, aligning your torso over your opponent's chest and shoulders.
    5. Apply downward pressure with your right forearm on your opponent's right knee to prevent them from extending their leg.
    6. Simultaneously, use your left arm to control their left sleeve or wrist, preventing them from posting.
    7. Shift your weight onto your opponent's chest, driving your left knee forward to clear their legs as you secure side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to force your opponent's legs open with straight arms: This can hyperextend your elbows or shoulders, damaging ligaments and tendons.
    • Allowing your hips to remain high and distant from your opponent's: This creates a large gap that allows them to recover guard or execute sweeps, risking knee or ankle injuries from awkward positions.
    • Not securing a strong leg overhook: Failing to place your left knee inside their right knee leaves your own leg vulnerable to a sweep or submission, potentially twisting your knee.
    • Letting your opponent's hips escape your control: This can lead to them bridging or shrimping out, potentially injuring your hips or lower back from sudden, uncontrolled movements.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the X-pass footwork, hip drive, and grip placement without a partner (5 minutes, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Execute the full X-pass sequence with a compliant partner, focusing on smooth transitions and correct body mechanics (10 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    3. Light pressure drill: Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the hip pressure and leg control (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    4. Dynamic drilling: Partner starts to actively defend, forcing you to adjust your angles and grips, but without attempting submissions (5 minutes, 75% resistance).
    5. Controlled sparring (guard pass focus): Attempt the X-pass in a live roll, but with the understanding that guard recovery is allowed (5 minutes, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the X-pass into your regular sparring sessions, focusing on timing and adaptation (full round, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts to close their guard after you've established a good base.
    • When your opponent is flat on their back, making it difficult for them to shrimp or bridge effectively.
    • When you can establish a strong leg overhook and hip pressure before they can fully lock their guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg recovery (shrimping): As the X-pass attempt begins, actively shrimp your hips away and bring your knee inside your opponent's chest to re-establish guard.
    • Hip escape and base adjustment: If the overhook is weak, quickly escape your hips to the side and establish a strong base to prevent the pass.
    • Posting and framing: Use your hands to frame against your opponent's chest or hips to create space and prevent their hip drive, allowing for guard recovery.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the X-Pass?

    Knee pain during the X-Pass often stems from not establishing a proper "figure four" leg entanglement. If your supporting leg's knee is not positioned inside your opponent's hip joint, or if you're collapsing inward without proper hip control, you can torque your knee joint. Ensure your knee is inside their hip and drive your hips forward to create a stable base, not leverage that twists your own knee.

    Q: How can I X-Pass a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, brute strength is less effective. Focus on precise biomechanics and leverage. Secure a deep collar grip to control their posture and use your extended leg to create a strong, stable base against their hip. Drive your hips forward and down, collapsing their base with your weight distribution rather than trying to muscle your way through. Your goal is to off-balance them and create an opening, not to overpower them.

    Q: When is the X-Pass NOT a good option?

    The X-Pass is less effective when your opponent has excellent hip mobility and actively counters your leg entanglement with strong frames or by shrimping effectively. It's also difficult if they maintain a very tight and high guard, preventing you from establishing the necessary leg overhook and hip pressure. If they can consistently turn into you or create space, other passing techniques might be more suitable.
