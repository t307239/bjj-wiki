Mastering the X-Pass in BJJ: A White Belt's Blueprint

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The X-Pass is a dynamic guard pass initiated from a standing or kneeling position, aiming to clear the opponent's legs and establish Side Control or mount.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and proper limb mechanics, leading to stalled passes or submission attempts against them.

The core principle is creating a tight, forward-pressure structure that forces the opponent to react defensively, allowing you to bypass their guard.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a kneeling position facing your opponent's closed guard, establish a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a same-side sleeve grip with your left hand.
    2. Drive your left knee forward, placing it on the mat slightly outside your opponent's left hip, creating a base width of approximately shoulder-width.
    3. Simultaneously, drive your hips forward and down, angling your torso to be chest-to-chest with your opponent, maintaining spinal neutrality.
    4. Use your right hand to pull your opponent's collar downwards and slightly across their body, creating a leverage point.
    5. With your left arm, apply pressure to your opponent's left sleeve, preventing them from creating a strong frame or pushing you away.
    6. Step your right leg forward and bring your right knee to the mat, aligning it with your opponent's left hip and maintaining a tight chest-to-chest connection.
    7. Shift your weight forward onto your left knee and right shin, driving your hips into your opponent's torso as you begin to peel their legs apart with your right hand.
    8. Continue to drive forward, using your chest and hips to control their base, and begin to move your right leg over their hips to secure side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attacking the X-Pass by reaching deep under the opponent's legs without establishing a strong chest-to-chest connection: This exposes your neck and arms to chokes (e.g., triangle choke) and armbars, as your head is not protected and your base is unstable.
    • Trying to 'muscle' the X-Pass by pulling the opponent's legs apart without forward hip pressure: This can lead to knee hyperextension injuries for you if the opponent pushes back, or a loss of balance and potential ankle lock attacks as your base is compromised.
    • Planting your feet too wide or too close when stepping into the pass: A wide base offers poor mobility, while a narrow base makes you unstable and susceptible to sweeps; always aim for shoulder-width with knees driving forward.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip drive and knee placement motion 20 times per side, focusing on the weight transfer and spinal angle, with 0% resistance.
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice steps 1-3 of the biomechanics section 10 times, focusing on grip and hip angle, with 0% resistance.
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Execute steps 1-5 with a partner actively trying to maintain closed guard, focusing on maintaining chest-to-chest pressure and clearing their arms, 10 reps per side, 25% resistance.
    4. Partner drilling (controlled pass): Progress to completing the entire X-Pass sequence against a partner who offers minimal resistance to leg clearing, focusing on hip pressure and stepping over, 5 reps per side, 50% resistance.
    5. Live rolling (positional sparring): Attempt the X-Pass against a resisting opponent from closed guard, focusing on timing and commitment to the pass, 3 rounds of 2 minutes, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling (full sparring): Integrate the X-Pass into regular sparring rounds, aiming to use it when opportunities arise, 5 rounds of 5 minutes, 90-100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's hips are relatively close to yours in closed guard, preventing them from creating a strong frame.
    • When the opponent's legs are slightly open or they are attempting to transition their guard, creating a momentary gap.
    • When you have established a strong collar grip, giving you leverage to begin the pass.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If the passer overcommits to their forward pressure, immediately hip escape to the side and re-establish guard by bringing your knees back between you and the passer.
    • Knee Shield/Push Away: As the passer drives their knee forward, actively use your shin to push their head and chest away, creating space to either recover guard or transition to a different guard.
    • Underhook and Bridge: If the passer attempts to step their leg over your hips, secure an underhook on their arm and bridge your hips upwards to create space and potentially roll them or recover guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to X-Pass?

    Your back pain likely stems from a lack of proper hip engagement and an overreliance on spinal flexion. When performing the X-Pass, the primary force should come from driving your hips forward, not by rounding your lower back. Ensure your spine remains neutral and your hips are angled to create forward pressure. Practice hip hinging drills to strengthen your posterior chain and improve this crucial movement pattern.

    Q: How can I X-Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paraMount. Focus on establishing a very tight chest-to-chest connection to negate their size advantage. Use your grips to control their posture and pull their upper body towards you. Drive your hips forward with significant force to create a wedge, making it difficult for them to maintain their base. Do not attempt to 'muscle' through; instead, use their weight against them by controlling their structure.

    Q: When is the best time to switch to a knee-slice pass after attempting an X-Pass?

    The optimal time to transition from an X-Pass attempt to a knee-slice pass is when you have successfully driven your hips forward, creating a strong chest-to-chest connection, but the opponent has effectively blocked your leg from coming over their hips. If their legs remain tightly locked or they have managed to create a strong inside frame, you can use your forward pressure to transition your lead knee (the one on the mat) to a knee-slice position by sliding it across their hips.
