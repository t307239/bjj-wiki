X-Pass: A Biomechanically Precise BJJ Guide for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The X-Pass is a guard passing technique initiated from your opponent's Closed Guard, aiming to break their posture and establish side control.

White belts often struggle by forcing the pass with raw strength, neglecting the crucial hip mechanics and weight distribution.

Success hinges on using your hips to drive through their legs, creating space and leverage, not just pushing their knees.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand, palm facing up, thumb inside the gi.
    2. With your left hand, establish a strong sleeve grip on your opponent's right sleeve, pinching your elbow to your side.
    3. Drive your chest towards your opponent's sternum, initiating a slight forward pressure.
    4. Step your right foot outside your opponent's left hip, creating a 45-degree angle between your bodies.
    5. Simultaneously, drive your left knee down to the mat, keeping your left foot in front of your opponent's hip.
    6. Execute an X-shape with your legs: your right foot is outside their hip, and your left shin is across their torso, perpendicular to their body.
    7. Drive your hips forward and down, angling your body to cut across their hips, aiming to flatten them out.
    8. Shift your weight from your hips onto your chest and shoulder, pinning their torso to the mat.
    9. Release your grips and establish side control, ensuring your chest is heavy on their chest.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly driving your head forward: This can hyperextend your cervical spine, risking a neck injury, or cause you to lose balance and fall onto your face. The correct action is to keep your head up and drive your chest into their sternum.
    • Grabbing the opponent's pants too low: This can lead to your knee getting caught and twisted, potentially tearing the ACL or MCL in your knee joint. Instead, grip the sleeve firmly and keep your knee outside their hip.
    • Pushing their knees outwards with your hands: This can strain your shoulder or elbow joints and offers little leverage, making it easy for them to recover guard. Use your hips to drive through and break their posture.
    • Allowing your hips to sag: This reduces your base and makes you vulnerable to sweeps, potentially leading to an ankle sprain if you're forced to move your foot quickly. Maintain a strong, forward-driving hip posture.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip sequence and leg X-shape positioning without a partner. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner assisted: With a compliant partner, practice the initial steps of breaking posture and stepping into the X-pass position. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    3. Controlled pass: Have your partner maintain a loose closed guard and practice the full X-Pass sequence with controlled movement. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    4. Guard pass scenario: Your partner attempts to maintain a closed guard, and you work to break it and execute the X-Pass. (10 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    5. Light rolling: Incorporate the X-Pass as a primary passing option during light rolling rounds. (5 minutes, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the X-Pass in a full-intensity rolling session, focusing on execution and timing. (5 minutes, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent's closed guard with their hips close to yours.
    • Opponent has their knees tightly locked, making it difficult to break their posture.
    • You have established a strong cross-collar and sleeve grip, allowing for posture control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard recovery (scissor sweep): If the passer's hips are too low, the opponent can bring their legs up and scissor-sweep by pushing the passer's hip away with their foot and sweeping the leg that is on the outside.
    • Hip escape and re-guard: If the passer fails to establish a strong chest-to-chest connection, the opponent can hip escape and re-establish their guard by bringing their knees back towards their chest.
    • Knee shield: If the passer's leg is not positioned correctly across the torso, the opponent can create a knee shield by bringing their free leg up and across the passer's chest, preventing them from driving through.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the X-Pass against someone who is very flexible?

    Your knee might be hurting because you are allowing your knee to twist inward when you drive forward, especially if their legs are very mobile. This can put excessive strain on your ACL and MCL. To fix this, focus on keeping your knee tracking directly over your foot, and ensure your shin is across their torso, not angled in a way that forces your knee to bend unnaturally.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, raw strength won't work. You need to rely on precise leverage and hip movement. Focus on getting your grips first to control their posture. Then, use your hips to drive *under* their center of gravity, not just push their knees. The key is to use your body weight and angle to unbalance them, rather than trying to overpower them physically.

    Q: You should consider switching from a knee-slice to an X-Pass when you've established good grips but can't get your knee to the inside effectively due to their tight guard. If their hips are close and their knees are locked, and you can secure a solid cross-collar and sleeve grip, the X-Pass becomes a viable option to break their posture and drive through their base.
