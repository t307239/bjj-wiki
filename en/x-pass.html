X-Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The X-Pass is a guard passing technique designed to break an opponent's Closed Guard and establish side control. It's initiated from standing or kneeling, aiming to create an angle to pass the legs. White belts often struggle because they lack a solid base and understand the importance of hip pressure, leading to them being swept or controlled.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure opponent's right pant leg with your left hand (cross-grip on the thigh). Secure opponent's left sleeve with your right hand (same-side grip).
    2. Stance: Kneel on your right knee, with your left leg extended forward, foot flat on the mat, creating a '4' shape with your legs.
    3. Hip Angle: Drive your hips down and slightly to your left, perpendicular to the opponent's hips.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your extended left leg and your right knee, pushing your hips forward.
    5. Head Position: Place your head on the opponent's right hip or lower abdomen, creating a strong post.
    6. Leg Drive: Use your left leg to drive forward and slightly inward, clearing the opponent's guard.
    7. Final Control: As you drive through, use your right hand to control the opponent's posture or push their hips away to clear their legs and transition to side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Rushing the pass by not establishing a strong base: This can lead to your knee or ankle ligaments (e.g., ACL, MCL, ATFL) being overstretched or torn if the opponent hip escapes or shrimps effectively.
    • Pulling the opponent's head forward aggressively without proper hip control: This can strain the cervical spine (neck) and shoulder joints (glenohumeral joint) due to hyperextension or impingement.
    • Trying to 'muscle' through the guard without creating the correct angle: This can cause hip impingement or groin strains from forcing the hips into an unnatural position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo movement practice: Perform the leg and hip movement sequence 20 times on each side, focusing on the '4' shape and hip drive.
    2. Partnered drilling (no resistance): With a willing partner, practice the grips, leg placement, and hip drive 10 reps each side, focusing on feeling the correct body position.
    3. Partnered drilling (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance (e.g., lightly holds your arms). Execute the X-Pass 10 reps each side, concentrating on driving through the guard.
    4. Guard retention focus: Partner attempts to maintain closed guard. You work to break it and execute the X-Pass 5 reps each side, focusing on control.
    5. Light sparring (start from guard pass scenario): Begin a round with you in the guard passer's position. Attempt the X-Pass with 50% resistance from your partner.
    6. Live rolling (focus on X-Pass): During a live roll, dedicate rounds to attempting the X-Pass whenever your partner establishes closed guard, aiming for 75-90% effort.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent has their knees tightly clasped, forming a strong closed guard.
    • Opponent attempts to open their guard but hasn't fully established an open guard structure.
    • Opponent is posturing up slightly, creating a small opening.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Shrimp: If the passer drives forward without establishing a strong angle, the defender can shrimp their hips away and escape the pressure, re-establishing guard.
    • Knee Shield / Push Knee: The defender can use their knee to push the passer's hip or shoulder away, creating space to re-guard or transition.
    • Flower Sweep / Scissor Sweep: If the passer commits too heavily to driving forward and leaves their base unstable, the defender can use a sweep to invert their position and gain top control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my hip get stuck when I try the X-Pass?

    Your hip gets stuck because you're not creating a sufficient angle. Instead of driving straight forward, you need to angle your hips perpendicular to your opponent's. Think of your hips as the steering wheel of a car, turning the car (your body) around the opponent's hips.

    Q: How can I X-Pass someone much bigger than me?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are crucial. Focus on using your grips to control their posture and hip movement. Instead of trying to drive through them, use your hip angle to create a wedge, driving your hips *under* their center of gravity and using your extended leg to clear their legs with precise timing.

    Q: When should I start the X-Pass, and when should I abandon it?

    Start the X-Pass when you see a clear opportunity to grip their legs and establish your kneeling base. Abandon it if your opponent successfully defends your initial grip or angle attempt, or if they begin to create a strong counter-pressure that threatens your base. Transition to another pass or reset.
