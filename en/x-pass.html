X-Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The X-Pass is a fundamental guard passing technique initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a Closed Guard. It aims to break the opponent's guard and establish a dominant side-control position. White belts often struggle by over-relying on brute strength instead of precise weight distribution and hip movement.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand, palm facing down, thumb digging into the opponent's sternum.
    2. Secure a strong cross-sleeve grip with your left hand on the opponent's right sleeve, near the wrist, with your palm facing your own body.
    3. Lower your base by bending your knees and hips, bringing your chest towards the opponent's thighs, creating a compact posture.
    4. Drive your right knee forward and slightly across the opponent's centerline, aiming to place it on the mat just outside their right hip.
    5. Simultaneously, begin to shift your weight forward and to your right, pushing your hips towards the opponent's left hip.
    6. Use your left arm to peel the opponent's right arm away from your neck or body, creating space and breaking their guard structure.
    7. As you drive your right knee forward and your hips to the right, rotate your torso to your left, bringing your left shoulder to the mat behind the opponent's head.
    8. Continue to drive your hips forward and down, flattening the opponent and securing side control, maintaining pressure through your chest and hips.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to rip the legs apart with arm strength alone: This can cause rotator cuff tears in your shoulders or biceps tendinitis as you strain against the opponent's hip structure.
    • Driving your knee directly into the opponent's groin: This is illegal and can cause serious injury to the opponent's genitalia and your own knee if they react defensively.
    • Hunching over with your back rounded: This puts immense strain on your lumbar spine, leading to potential disc herniations or muscle spasms.
    • Releasing grips prematurely: This allows the opponent to re-establish guard or initiate a sweep, often leaving you in a compromised, off-balance position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the movement of the X-Pass without a partner, focusing on hip placement and knee drive. (50 reps)
    2. Mirror drilling: With a partner, drill the X-Pass movement without resistance, focusing on grip control and body positioning. (25 reps each side)
    3. Static guard pass: Partner maintains a closed guard, and you execute the X-Pass to pass. Partner offers minimal resistance to guard retention. (5 reps each side)
    4. Light resistance guard pass: Partner actively attempts to maintain closed guard, offering light resistance to your passing attempts. (10 reps each side)
    5. Controlled pass with guard retention: Partner actively attempts to retain guard and sweep, but not with full commitment. (15 reps each side)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the X-Pass during rolling with full resistance, focusing on applying the learned biomechanics. (5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent has their knees tightly clasped in closed guard, limiting their mobility.
    • When the opponent attempts to create distance or a tight frame to prevent you from closing the gap.
    • When you have established solid grips on the collar and sleeve, controlling their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention (Legs): The opponent can keep their knees tight together, pushing your hips away with their feet to prevent you from getting your knee inside.
    • Hip Escape and Framing: If you overcommit to the pass, the opponent can shrimp their hips and frame with their forearms to create space and potentially recover guard or sweep.
    • Sweeps from Closed Guard: A skilled opponent can use their leg strength and hip movement to initiate a sweep, often by elevating your hips or turning you.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to X-Pass?

    Your knee might hurt because you are driving it directly into your opponent's hip bone or pushing it at an unnatural angle. Ensure your knee is driving forward and slightly across their centerline, landing on the mat outside their hip. Maintain a stable base and keep your knee aligned with your hip and ankle to avoid hyperextension or twisting.

    Q: How can I X-Pass against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on precision and leverage over strength. Prioritize breaking their posture with your collar and sleeve grips before initiating the pass. Use your hip mobility to get your knee inside their hip line and drive your weight forward, not just upwards. Think of it as 'fitting' yourself around their frame rather than overpowering it.
