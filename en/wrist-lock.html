Mastering the Wrist Lock in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Wrist Lock is a submission that targets the radiocarpal joint, hyperextending it to cause pain and tap. It's often applied from guard or Side Control when an opponent's arm is isolated.

Many white belts struggle because they rely on brute strength and don't understand the precise angles needed to isolate the joint. This leads to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key is to control the opponent's entire arm and use your body's leverage to create a stable fulcrum, isolating the wrist's natural range of motion.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an "S" grip on the opponent's wrist with your right hand: thumb inside their palm, four fingers wrapped around the back. Your left hand grips their elbow joint, creating a frame.
    2. Shift your hips 45 degrees to your left, creating a wedge with your left hip against their right shoulder.
    3. Pull your opponent's elbow towards your right hip, straightening their arm across your chest.
    4. With your left hand, maintain a firm grip on their elbow, preventing them from bending it.
    5. Your right hand, gripping the wrist, begins to rotate your knuckles upwards towards the ceiling.
    6. Simultaneously, lean your torso forward, driving your chest into their upper arm, creating forward pressure.
    7. Continue the knuckle-upward rotation of your right hand while maintaining the forward lean and elbow control until submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure by "pulling hard" with your right hand alone: This can cause hyperextension of the metacarpals or strain the ulnar collateral ligament of the wrist, leading to sprains or fractures. The correct method involves body leverage, not just arm strength.
    • Not controlling the opponent's elbow with your left hand: This allows them to bend their arm, negating the wrist lock and potentially leading to a counter-sweep or escape. The left hand must act as a rigid frame.
    • Rotating your wrist in an uncontrolled arc: This can snap the opponent's wrist in an unintended direction, causing fracture or dislocation. The rotation must be a controlled, deliberate upward motion of your knuckles.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip changes and hip movements without a partner. Focus on the sequence and the feeling of creating the frame. (50 reps).
    2. Static partner drill: With a compliant partner in closed guard, execute the technique slowly, focusing on control and proper angles. Partner provides no resistance. (20 reps per side).
    3. Light resistance drill: Partner offers slight resistance to elbow control but does not actively defend the wrist. Focus on maintaining your frames. (15 reps per side).
    4. Dynamic resistance drill: Partner actively tries to pull their arm out or bend their elbow. You must maintain your frames and hip angle. (10 reps per side).
    5. Controlled submission attempt: Partner allows the submission to be applied to the point of tap, but does not tap early. Focus on feeling the correct pressure. (5 reps per side).
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Wrist Lock from closed guard when the opportunity arises, with 75% resistance from your partner. Focus on successful setup and execution.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent's arm is extended across your body from closed guard.
    • Opponent is posturing up and leaving an arm exposed in side control.
    • Opponent attempts to stack pass your guard, creating an opportunity to isolate an arm.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Elbow bend defense: If your elbow is not firmly controlled, the opponent can bend their elbow to relieve pressure. Maintain a rigid frame with your non-attacking arm.
    • Arm escape: If your grip on the wrist is loose or your body position is off, the opponent can pull their arm free. Focus on securing the grip and using your hips to drive forward.
    • Posture up and break grip: If the opponent's grip is weak, they can try to posture up and break your grip. Maintain your frames and use your legs to control their hips.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a BJJ Wrist Lock from closed guard?

    Your wrist is likely hurting because you are trying to apply the lock using only your hands, creating a shearing force on your own wrist. The correct biomechanical application involves using your hips and torso to create leverage, isolating the opponent's wrist and hyperextending it through a controlled rotation of your knuckles upwards, not by bending your own wrist.

    Q: How can I Wrist Lock a much bigger opponent from closed guard in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your leverage is even more critical. Focus on tightening your Closed Guard and using your hips to create a strong base. When you initiate the lock, drive your hips *into* their arm and use your torso to create the forward pressure, rather than trying to pull their arm across your body with just your arms. Your goal is to make their arm the lever and your body the fulcrum.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Wrist Lock from the closed guard in BJJ?

    The optimal time to attempt a Wrist Lock from closed guard is when the opponent's arm is extended across your chest, typically when they are posturing up or trying to pass your guard. If their arm is bent or too close to their body, the angle is not correct. Look for opportunities where you can isolate one arm and prevent them from bringing it back to a defensive position.
