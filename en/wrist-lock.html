Mastering the Wrist Lock in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Wrist Lock is a joint lock targeting the opponent's wrist, typically applied from dominant or controlling positions. It achieves a submission by hyperextending or hyperflexing the wrist joint.

White belts often fail by trying to force the submission with raw strength rather than understanding the precise leverage and body mechanics required.

The key mechanical insight is to isolate the opponent's wrist and create a fulcrum point with your own body, using precise angles to apply torque.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish control of the opponent's wrist with your dominant hand's "baseball bat grip": thumb on top, fingers wrapped around the metacarpals.
    2. Use your non-dominant hand to cup the opponent's forearm just below the elbow, creating a stabilizing "shelf".
    3. Position your hips at a 90-degree angle to the opponent's body, ensuring your weight is anchored.
    4. Drive your chest into the opponent's shoulder, collapsing their posture and preventing them from posturing up.
    5. Rotate your torso away from the opponent's arm, creating tension on their wrist.
    6. Simultaneously, lift your non-dominant arm's "shelf" upwards while lowering your dominant arm's grip slightly.
    7. Maintain a stable base with your knees bent and feet flat, ensuring your pelvis remains the pivot point.
    8. Apply gradual, controlled pressure by extending your hips and drawing your elbows towards your body, creating a lever action.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive upward force with the "shelf" hand against a resisting opponent's bicep can cause a hyperextension injury to their elbow joint's ulnar collateral ligament (UCL).
    • Grabbing the opponent's wrist too loosely and attempting to "muscle" the lock can result in a posterior dislocation of the radiocarpal joint (wrist).
    • Attempting to finish the Wrist Lock by arching your back aggressively instead of using hip extension can strain your lumbar spine and lead to disc compression.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hand and body positioning without a partner, focusing on grip accuracy and hip angle. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Execute the technique slowly with a compliant partner, focusing on smooth transitions. (20 reps per side, 10% resistance)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to arm movement, allowing you to feel the pressure build. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled drilling: Partner actively tries to defend but allows the submission to be applied if executed correctly. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Scenario drilling: Practice the Wrist Lock from a specific guard pass or control position, with partner offering moderate defense. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Wrist Lock during sparring, prioritizing technique over speed and always tapping early if caught. (5 attempts per round, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From Closed Guard: When the opponent's arm is extended and you can isolate their wrist.
    • During Guard Passing: When an opponent leaves their arm exposed during a guard pass attempt like a Torreando.
    • From Side Control: When the opponent attempts to shrimp away and exposes their wrist in an attempt to create space.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Tap": The immediate and safest counter is to verbally or physically signal submission before injury occurs.
    • "Arm Extension": If the Wrist Lock is being applied, the defender can attempt to extend their arm fully, creating slack in the joint and reducing leverage.
    • "Wrist Rotation": While the Wrist Lock is being applied, the defender can try to rotate their wrist inward (towards the thumb side of the lock), disrupting the torque.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Wrist Lock on someone?

    Your wrist is likely hurting because you are not properly isolating the opponent's wrist. Instead of creating a fulcrum with your body and applying torque to their joint, you are likely putting pressure on your own wrist or attempting to bend their wrist with your fingers alone. Focus on cupping their forearm to stabilize and using your hip and torso rotation to create the leverage.

    Q: How do I apply a Wrist Lock against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, raw strength is not your ally. You must rely heavily on biomechanics and leverage. Focus on completely isolating their wrist and forearm, ensuring no part of their larger limb can provide resistance. Use your hips and torso rotation to create the torque, rather than trying to overpower their grip. A tight "baseball bat grip" on their wrist is crucial.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Wrist Lock in BJJ if I'm a white belt?

    As a white belt, the best times to attempt a Wrist Lock are when the opponent's arm is already extended and somewhat isolated, making it easier to secure the grip and apply leverage. This often happens when they are attempting to posture up from your guard, or when you've successfully controlled an arm during a Guard Pass and they are trying to retract it. Avoid trying to force it from a tangled or defensive position.
