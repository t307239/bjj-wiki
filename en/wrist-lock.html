The Wrist Lock is a joint lock targeting the opponent's wrist, applied from various control positions like Closed Guard or side control to elicit a tap.

White belts often fail by applying excessive, uncontrolled force, leading to injury or ineffective submission.

The key is isolating the wrist's hyperextension point by controlling the forearm and applying leverage through your hips and shoulders, not raw grip strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's wrist with your dominant hand. Your palm faces up, fingers pointing towards their elbow.
    2. Cup your opponent's wrist with your thumb on the inside, wrapping your fingers around the back of their hand.
    3. Insert your non-dominant hand underneath their forearm, palm up, fingers pointing towards their elbow, creating a "sandwich" grip.
    4. Your non-dominant thumb should be inside the grip, pressing against their forearm.
    5. Begin to close your guard, tightening your legs around their torso, driving their elbow down towards your hip.
    6. Simultaneously, lean back slightly, extending your hips away from their body, creating a fulcrum with your hips.
    7. Apply upward pressure with your non-dominant hand on their forearm and a slight downward pull with your dominant hand on their wrist, isolating the wrist joint.
    8. Increase the angle of hyperextension by gently lifting your hips and tilting your torso back, forcing the tap.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure with straight arms by pushing your hips forward: This can cause a hyperextension injury to the opponent's elbow joint (ligament tear) or your own shoulder (rotator cuff strain).
    • Gripping too tightly around the opponent's entire fist without isolating the wrist: This allows them to resist by tensing their grip, risking a sprain in your own wrist or finger joints.
    • Rushing the submission by pulling upwards without establishing the proper hip angle and forearm control: This can lead to a valgus stress injury at the opponent's elbow or a tear in their wrist ligaments.
    • Not communicating or checking for a tap: This is a severe safety violation and can result in serious injury to the opponent's wrist or elbow.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Wrist Isolation Drill: Practice the grip and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the "sandwich" grip and hip angle. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    2. Static Wrist Lock Drill: With a partner, from closed guard, they offer no resistance. You apply the grip and hip movement to achieve the submission angle. (5 reps per side, 10% resistance)
    3. Controlled Wrist Lock Drill: Partner offers light resistance, focusing on maintaining their posture. You apply the technique slowly, ensuring proper mechanics. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Assisted Wrist Lock Drill: Partner actively tries to defend by posturing up or turning their wrist, but allows you to work the submission. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Partial Resistance Wrist Lock Drill: Partner attempts to escape the guard and defend the submission, but prioritizes safety and tap. (3 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling Scenario (Controlled): During rolling, if the opportunity arises from closed guard, attempt the wrist lock with full awareness of your partner's tap. (Focus on timing and opportunity, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From closed guard when the opponent tries to create space by posturing up.
    • From side control when the opponent bridges or tries to shrimp away, exposing their arm.
    • When an opponent attempts to pass your guard and leaves their arm extended and vulnerable.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Straightening the arm and creating a strong frame with your elbow: This prevents the hyperextension by keeping your elbow joint stable and resisting the inward rotation.
    • Turning the wrist inwards towards your body: This rotates the forearm and wrist to relieve pressure on the joint and break the attacking grip.
    • Pulling the arm across your body and tucking your elbow: This brings the joint into a more neutral position, making it harder to isolate and apply leverage.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a BJJ Wrist Lock from closed guard?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are gripping too tightly around your opponent's entire fist instead of isolating their wrist and forearm. This causes your own metacarpals and phalanges to absorb excessive pressure. Focus on cupping their wrist with your palm and using your forearm to control their radial and ulnar bones, applying pressure to the joint itself.

    Q: How can I apply a Wrist Lock against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, raw grip strength is ineffective. You must leverage their size against them. Focus on using your hips to create a strong fulcrum by leaning back and extending your hips away. Control their forearm with your non-dominant hand, and use your dominant hand to isolate the wrist, then gently hyperextend. Their own weight and momentum will assist the submission.

    Q: When is the best time to go for a Wrist Lock in BJJ, and how do I avoid giving up position?

    The best time is when your opponent is postured up, creating an opening in your closed guard, or when they are trying to escape a submission and expose their arm. Avoid committing to the wrist lock if it compromises your base or guard retention. If they defend effectively, immediately transition to a sweep or another submission rather than forcing a bad position.
