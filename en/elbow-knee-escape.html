The Elbow-Knee Escape is a foundational BJJ technique used primarily to escape the Closed Guard when your opponent has established strong control.

White belts often fail by trying to force their way out with brute strength, neglecting fundamental body positioning and leverage.

The key insight is to use controlled hip movement and weight distribution to create space, breaking your opponent's posture and grip.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish grips: Cross-collar grip on opponent's gi top with your right hand, and sleeve grip on opponent's right sleeve with your left hand.
    2. Angle your hips: Rotate your hips 45 degrees to your left, ensuring your left hip is closer to the mat than your right.
    3. Break posture: Use your cross-collar grip to pull your opponent's head and upper body towards your left side, creating a slight lean.
    4. Control the knee: With your right hand, reach across your opponent's body and grip their right pant leg, just above the knee.
    5. Create space: Simultaneously, extend your left leg, pushing off the mat, while driving your right knee forward and slightly inward.
    6. Hip escape: As your right knee drives forward, simultaneously hip escape to your left. This creates a gap between your hips and your opponent's.
    7. Secure base: Bring your left knee into your chest and place your left foot flat on the mat for base. Your right knee should now be free.
    8. Transition: Once your right knee is free, you can establish a guard recovery or transition to a more advantageous position like half-guard.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect knee drive: Driving your right knee straight up or outwards instead of slightly inward and forward can twist your knee joint, leading to MCL or LCL sprains.
    • Correct: Drive your right knee forward and slightly across your body, aiming to create space under your opponent's hips.
    • Forcing the escape with arms: Trying to pry your opponent's legs open with arm strength alone, neglecting hip movement, can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears.
    • Correct: Focus on hip rotation and creating space with your legs and core, using arm grips primarily for posture control.
    • Not creating sufficient hip angle: Remaining square to your opponent (0-degree hip angle) prevents effective hip escape and can result in your opponent stacking you, risking lumbar spine compression.
    • Correct: Achieve a minimum 45-degree hip angle to allow your hips to move freely and create the necessary space.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0%): Practice the hip angle, knee drive, and hip escape motion without a partner. Focus on the precise sequence and feel. (10 reps per side)
    2. Passive partner drill (25%): Your partner lies on their back, legs in closed guard. You practice the grip establishment, posture break, and hip escape. Partner offers minimal resistance. (5 reps per side)
    3. Controlled movement drill (50%): Partner maintains a light closed guard. You execute the Elbow-Knee Escape. Partner resists slightly but allows you to create space and hip escape. (5 reps per side)
    4. Guard recovery drill (75%): After successfully creating space and hip escaping, immediately practice recovering your guard (e.g., bringing your left knee to your chest and establishing a basic guard). Partner attempts to re-establish guard or pass. (5 reps per side)
    5. Simulated escape drill (90%): Partner applies a moderately tight closed guard. You execute the Elbow-Knee Escape, aiming to create space and break their control. Partner offers more resistance but doesn't actively try to submit or pass aggressively. (3 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Implement the Elbow-Knee Escape during live rolling when in closed guard. Focus on applying the mechanics correctly rather than just escaping. (Attempt 3-5 times per roll)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent has a tight closed guard and you are struggling to break their posture.
    • When your opponent is attempting to set up a sweep or submission from closed guard.
    • When you feel a slight opening or your opponent's weight distribution shifts, creating a brief window of opportunity.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Pass: If the attacker fails to create sufficient hip angle and drives their knee directly into your chest, you can stack their weight by leaning forward and driving your hips into their shoulders, potentially leading to a pass.
    • Leg Pull to Sweep: If the attacker's leg drive is weak, you can pull their hip towards you and use your free leg to sweep their base, transitioning to a dominant position.
    • Re-establishing Guard: If the attacker creates space but doesn't commit to the escape, you can quickly bring your legs back together and re-establish a strong closed or open guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my hip hurt when I try the Elbow-Knee Escape?

    Hip pain during the Elbow-Knee Escape often stems from an incorrect hip angle and insufficient forward knee drive. If you keep your hips too square to your opponent and only push your knee outward, you create torque on your hip joint. Ensure you rotate your hips at least 45 degrees and drive your knee forward and slightly across your body to create space, minimizing stress on the joint.

    Q: How do I Elbow-Knee Escape against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maximizing your leverage. The key is a significant hip angle and precise timing. Instead of trying to muscle them, use their weight against you. When they lean forward to maintain control, create your hip angle and drive your knee forward to create space, then immediately Hip Escape to the side. Leverage your entire body weight for the hip escape, not just arm strength.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Elbow-Knee Escape from closed guard?

    The optimal time to attempt the Elbow-Knee Escape is when your opponent makes a mistake in their weight distribution or posture. Look for moments when they lean too far forward, creating an opportunity to break their base, or when they momentarily relax their grip. Trying to force the escape when they have perfect control is inefficient and can lead to them capitalizing on your movement.
