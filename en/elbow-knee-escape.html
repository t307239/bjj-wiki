The Elbow-Knee Escape is a fundamental BJJ technique used to create space and escape a dominant position, most commonly Side Control or mount.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute force rather than understanding the precise body mechanics required to shift weight and leverage.

The key is creating a platform with your elbow and knee to bridge and shrimp, effectively moving your hips away from the pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, establish a strong "frame" by placing your forearm across your opponent's neck and your other hand on their hip, palm down, fingers pointing away.
    2. Lower your hips to the mat, creating a wider base and planting your feet hip-width apart.
    3. Drive your right knee (if opponent is on your left) towards your chest, tucking your foot close to your glutes.
    4. Simultaneously, plant your right elbow firmly on the mat, creating a "post" that your body will pivot around.
    5. Initiate a "bridge" by extending your hips upwards and slightly backward, using your planted elbow and feet as anchors.
    6. As your hips lift, "shrimp" your hips to your left (away from the opponent's pressure), moving your pelvis perpendicular to their chest.
    7. Maintain the frame with your arms to prevent them from re-establishing dominant pressure as you create space.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Knee Drive: Driving the knee straight up with a bent ankle instead of tucking the foot close to the glutes. This can strain the knee joint and lead to ACL or MCL tears by placing rotational stress on the knee.
    • Over-Arching the Back: Trying to lift the hips solely by hyperextending the lumbar spine without engaging the glutes and core. This can cause lower back pain and muscle strains.
    • Premature Hip Escape: Attempting to shrimp the hips before a sufficient bridge has been initiated, leading to a loss of base and allowing the opponent to maintain or advance their position, potentially causing shoulder impingement if they drive forward.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Shrimping: Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat. Practice shrimping your hips to one side, then the other, focusing on creating space. (100 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Elbow Post & Knee Drive: From a side control bottom position (on your back), practice planting your elbow and driving your knee to your chest. (50 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    3. Bridging and Shrimping (No Partner): On your back, practice the full sequence of bridging and shrimping without any partner. Focus on the coordinated movement. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    4. Partner Assisted Drill: Have a partner lie on top of you in side control. They should provide minimal resistance (e.g., just lie there). Execute the Elbow-Knee Escape. (25 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    5. Controlled Partner Drill: With a partner in side control, they apply light pressure. Execute the escape, aiming to get to guard. Partner offers slight resistance to prevent immediate escape. (20 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling: Incorporate the Elbow-Knee Escape when you find yourself in side control or mount. Focus on executing the mechanics correctly, even if you don't achieve a full escape initially. (5 rounds, 75-90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When trapped in side control and your opponent is applying significant chest pressure.
    • When you feel your opponent is about to transition to mount from side control.
    • When you are in the bottom of mount and your opponent is posturing up, creating a small window of space.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stack Pass" Defense: If your opponent attempts to stack pass immediately after you shrimp, maintain your frames and drive your hips back towards them, creating a "wall" to prevent them from collapsing on you.
    • "Knee-on-Belly" Transition: If the opponent attempts to move to knee-on-belly as you escape, continue your shrimp and try to bring your guard back up, trapping their leg.
    • "Guard Recovery" (Full Guard): If you successfully create enough space, focus on bringing both knees between you and your opponent to re-establish closed guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try the Elbow-Knee Escape?

    Your lower back pain likely stems from over-relying on lumbar hyperextension rather than engaging your glutes and core for the bridge. Ensure you are planting your feet firmly and driving your hips up and back by squeezing your glutes, not just arching your spine. Think of it as pushing the mat away with your feet and hips simultaneously.

    Q: How do I Elbow-Knee Escape against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your frames become even more critical. Use your forearms and hands to create as much distance as possible, preventing them from "cutting off" your escape. Focus on the precise timing of your bridge and shrimp; a slight delay can allow their weight to crush your platform. A well-timed, explosive shrimp is key to moving your hips away before they can fully re-apply pressure.

    Q: Should I always try to get back to closed guard after an Elbow-Knee Escape?

    Not necessarily. The primary goal of the Elbow-Knee Escape is to create space and improve your position. While Closed Guard is a strong option, you might also end up in half guard, butterfly guard, or even a scrambled position. Focus on the escape mechanics first, then assess the best guard to establish or continue your defense from based on your opponent's reaction.
