The Americana: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide to the BJJ Shoulder Lock

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Americana is a shoulder lock applied from top positions like side-control or Mount, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's arm. White belts often struggle by forcing the movement instead of using leverage and body positioning.

The key is understanding that you are controlling the opponent's entire arm structure, not just trying to bend their elbow. This creates a fulcrum with your own body, making the submission efficient and safe when applied correctly.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, establish a "figure-four" grip on the opponent's arm: your right hand grips your own left wrist, and your left forearm is placed under the opponent's elbow.
    2. Position your chest directly over the opponent's upper chest, ensuring your hips are slightly lower than their shoulders, creating a stable base.
    3. Your left knee should be tucked towards the opponent's hip to prevent them from shrimping away, while your right leg extends to maintain base and prevent them from bridging.
    4. Begin to "stack" your weight forward, driving your shoulder into the opponent's bicep area to pin their arm to the mat.
    5. Slowly lift your hips off the mat while simultaneously driving your chest down and forward, keeping the opponent's arm trapped between your chest and your own forearm.
    6. As you lift your hips, your left hand (gripping your own wrist) will pull your right elbow downwards, creating the rotational force on the shoulder joint.
    7. Maintain constant pressure with your chest and shoulder on the opponent's bicep to prevent them from escaping the "figure-four" grip or turning into you.
    8. The submission is achieved when the opponent feels a sharp pressure in their shoulder and taps to avoid hyperextension.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure by solely using arm strength without proper body positioning: This can lead to tearing the rotator cuff or biceps tendon due to excessive, uncontrolled force.
    • Allowing the opponent's elbow to flare out sideways during the submission attempt: This strains the medial collateral ligament (MCL) of the elbow and the shoulder capsule.
    • Rushing the submission by trying to "snap" the arm into place: This risks tearing the labrum or causing a dislocation of the glenohumeral joint.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the "figure-four" grip and body positioning without a partner, focusing on the hip and chest alignment. (10 reps per side)
    2. Static drilling with a compliant partner: Partner lies on their back, you practice the grip and body mechanics without any resistance. (10 reps per side)
    3. Controlled application with minimal resistance: Partner actively resists by trying to pull their arm out, but you focus on maintaining the correct body position and grip. (25% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    4. Partner attempts to defend the Americana: Partner actively tries to defend by tucking their elbow or turning into you. You focus on maintaining control and finishing the submission. (50% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    5. Live rolling scenario: Attempt the Americana from side-control during rolling, focusing on proper setup and execution rather than immediate tap. (75% resistance, 3 attempts per side)
    6. Full resistance rolling: Attempt the Americana in a live roll with full resistance, prioritizing technique and understanding the timing. (100% resistance, as opportunities arise)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established solid side-control and the opponent's arm is extended away from their body.
    • As a follow-up submission from a failed armbar attempt where the opponent has "stacked" their weight onto you.
    • When the opponent tries to bridge or shrimp out of side-control, leaving their arm exposed.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Elbow Tuck and Turn-in: The opponent tucks their elbow tightly to their body and rotates their hips towards you, relieving pressure on the shoulder. This requires the attacker to adjust their angle or switch to another submission.
    • "Stalling" the Americana (Stacking the Attacker): The opponent attempts to "stack" their weight by bridging their hips and driving their shoulders into your chest, making it difficult to lift your hips and finish the lock. The attacker must maintain base and potentially switch to a different control position.
    • "The Escape": The opponent attempts to pull their arm out by "swimming" their arm underneath their body and turning their elbow towards the mat. The attacker must maintain a strong "figure-four" grip and keep their chest pinned to the opponent's bicep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Americana BJJ submission?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are gripping your own wrist too tightly or incorrectly. Instead of a rigid, tense grip, aim for a relaxed "figure-four" where your left forearm supports your right wrist. The pressure should come from driving your right elbow down and your chest into the opponent's bicep, not from squeezing your own hands together. Relax your grip slightly and focus on the body mechanics.

    Q: How do I finish the Americana BJJ submission against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body positioning are even more critical. Ensure your chest is firmly planted on their bicep to prevent them from shrimping. Drive your hips up powerfully while keeping your head down, creating a strong fulcrum. Focus on controlling their entire arm structure by pinning their elbow to the mat with your forearm; don't try to simply bend their arm.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Americana in BJJ, and what if my opponent defends it?

    The Americana is best attempted when you have established dominant side-control and their arm is somewhat extended. If your opponent defends by tucking their elbow or turning in, don't force the submission. You can often transition to a Kimura grip or use their defensive movement to improve your control and potentially set up a different attack, like a knee-on-belly or a transition to mount.
