Mastering the Americana in BJJ: A White Belt's Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Americana is a shoulder lock applied from various positions, most commonly side-control or Mount, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's shoulder joint.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of control over their own body and their opponent's limbs, leading to sloppy execution and potential injury.

The key is to isolate the arm and use your body weight to create a controlled lever, preventing the opponent from escaping by turning into you.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, establish a secure grip on the opponent's far wrist with your near hand (e.g., right hand grips left wrist).
    2. Place your other arm (e.g., left arm) under the opponent's armpit, securing a "figure-four" grip by cupping your own wrist with your free hand (e.g., left hand cups right wrist).
    3. Shift your hips to your left, creating a base wider than your opponent's shoulders and perpendicular to their torso.
    4. Drive your chest into the opponent's chest, preventing them from bridging or turning into you.
    5. Keep the opponent's elbow bent at approximately 90 degrees, ensuring it remains on the mat.
    6. Slowly lift the opponent's wrist upwards towards their head while simultaneously driving your hips down and slightly forward.
    7. Apply pressure by continuing to lift the wrist, creating a controlled external rotation and hyperextension of the glenohumeral (shoulder) joint.
    8. Maintain a stable base and keep your weight distributed evenly over your hips and chest throughout the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive upward force to the arm before isolating it properly can lead to a torn rotator cuff or labrum injury, as the shoulder joint is not yet stabilized.
    • Failing to keep the opponent's elbow bent and on the mat allows them to straighten their arm, increasing the risk of a torn UCL (ulnar collateral ligament) or elbow hyperextension.
    • Grasping the opponent's wrist too loosely or allowing your figure-four grip to slip compromises the lever, leading to ineffective pressure and potential strain on your own shoulder or elbow.
    • Not maintaining a stable base and driving your hips down can result in the opponent escaping or you losing control, risking knee or hip injuries from awkward positions.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip sequence and hip movement without a partner. Focus on the figure-four grip and hip angle. (10 reps per side)
    2. Static partner drill: With a compliant partner lying on their back, practice securing the Americana from side-control. Partner offers no resistance. (10 reps per side)
    3. Light resistance drill: Partner actively tries to resist by framing or turning slightly. Focus on maintaining control and applying the technique slowly. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    4. Controlled positional drilling: Start from side-control. Partner attempts to escape by bridging or shrimping. You focus on maintaining the Americana grip and position. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    5. Flow rolling with focus: During rolling, actively look for opportunities to attack the Americana. Partner defends but does not actively try to injure you. (2-minute rounds, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Americana during regular rolling. Focus on proper execution and understanding the opponent's reactions. (5-minute rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established solid side-control and the opponent is flat on their back.
    • When the opponent attempts to bridge or shrimp out of side-control, exposing their arm.
    • When the opponent posts their arm on the mat to defend a guard pass or sweep, creating an opportunity.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Escape by bridging: If the opponent attempts the Americana from side-control, bridge your hips into them and simultaneously try to tuck your elbow to your side to relieve pressure.
    • Re-guarding: If the opponent is slow to secure the figure-four grip, use your free arm to frame against their face or shoulder and shrimp your hips back to establish guard.
    • Turning into the attacker: If the opponent has the wrist grip but not the figure-four, try to turn your body into the attacker's chest, collapsing the angle and relieving pressure on your shoulder.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Americana?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you grasp the opponent's wrist too tightly with your fingers instead of using your forearm and the palm of your hand to create a stable grip. This puts excessive torque on your own wrist. To fix this, ensure your grip is firm but that your forearm is the primary point of contact, supporting the pressure, and that your wrist remains relatively neutral.

    Q: How do I apply the Americana against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body positioning are critical. Focus on isolating their arm completely by pinning their elbow to the mat with your chest and hips. Ensure your figure-four grip is tight and your hips are low and heavy, creating a strong base. Lift their wrist upwards towards their head, using your entire body weight to create the lever, rather than relying solely on upper body strength.

    Q: When is the best time to try for an Americana in BJJ?

    The Americana is most effective when your opponent is relatively static and you have established dominant control, particularly in side-control or mount. It's an excellent submission to attack when your opponent tries to create space by bridging or shrimping, as these movements can expose their arm. Avoid attempting it when the opponent is actively scrambling or when you lack a secure base, as this can lead to an easy escape for them.

    In-Depth: Mastering the Americana in BJJ: A White Belt's Guide

    Biomechanics & Physics

    The Americana, fundamentally, is a kimura variant that targets the shoulder joint's external rotation and abduction capabilities. The core mechanics revolve around creating a fulcrum with the opponent's elbow, which is ideally kept at a 90-degree bend and pinned to the mat. Your opponent's wrist is the resistance point, and your body weight acts as the force application. The "figure-four" grip on their arm isolates the humerus and scapula. When you lift their wrist upwards towards their head, you are applying a supraphysiologic external rotation force to the glenohumeral joint. Simultaneously, driving your hips down and slightly forward creates a stable base and applies downward pressure on their torso, preventing them from bridging or "swimming" their arm out. The bent elbow acts as the pivot point for this lever system. If the elbow is too straight, the force is distributed more broadly and is less efficient. Conversely, if the elbow is too bent, it can compromise the lock's effectiveness and potentially put undue stress on the elbow joint itself, rather than the shoulder.

    Common Mistakes (Specific to This Technique)

    • Allowing the opponent's elbow to straighten: If the opponent's arm is extended, the lock becomes less effective as the fulcrum is no longer stable. This often happens if the attacker's chest pressure isn't maintained, allowing the opponent to straighten their limb and create space.
    • Not controlling the opponent's hips: If the opponent can effectively bridge or shrimp, they can disrupt your base and create angles to escape the lock. Failure to establish a strong, perpendicular base and maintain chest-to-chest connection will lead to the opponent escaping.
    • Lifting the wrist too high or too quickly: While the goal is to lift the wrist, doing so with excessive force or to an extreme degree can lead to a sloppy tap or, worse, injury. The pressure should build progressively, allowing the opponent to feel the lock and tap before it becomes dangerous.

    Variations & Counters

    The Americana is a foundational submission that often serves as a gateway to understanding other shoulder locks. From side control, if the opponent defends by "stacking" their weight or turning their hips into you, it can lead to a transition to a kimura. If they manage to post their free hand to create space, you might transition to a cross-face for further control or to set up a knee-on-belly. Conversely, when facing an Americana attempt, white belts should understand the importance of "pulling the arm" to reduce the angle and prevent the necessary hyperextension. Aggressively turning into the attacker, while difficult with proper Americana mechanics, is a common counter attempt that can lead to scrambles or opportunities for the defender to establish guard. A proficient defender might also try to relieve pressure by driving their elbow down towards the mat, effectively shortening the lever arm.

    Drilling Recommendations

    Begin by drilling the Americana from side control with zero resistance for 10-15 repetitions per side. Focus solely on achieving the correct grip, establishing posture, and the controlled motion of lifting the wrist. Progress to drilling with a training partner who offers light, passive resistance – essentially, they allow you to establish the grip but resist slightly as you apply pressure. Perform 5-8 repetitions per side in this phase, ensuring your partner can tap comfortably. The next step involves a partner offering moderate resistance, meaning they actively try to prevent you from securing the figure-four grip or turning their hips. Perform 3-5 repetitions per side, focusing on maintaining control and finishing the submission. Avoid live rolling until you can consistently execute the Americana against moderate resistance.
