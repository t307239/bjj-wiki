Americana: The Essential BJJ Shoulder Lock for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Americana is a shoulder lock applied from dominant positions like Side Control or mount, aiming to hyperextend the opponent's arm. White belts often struggle due to a lack of control over their own limbs and a tendency to force the submission rather than isolate the mechanics.

The key to a successful Americana lies in controlling the opponent's posture and creating a strong, stable base, preventing them from posturing up or bridging out.

Understanding the fulcrum point – the opponent's elbow – and using your body weight to apply leverage is paraMount.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side control, establish a "head and arm" control: Your left arm (if on their right side) secures their head by placing your bicep against their temple, thumb pointing upwards. Your right arm slides under their head, cupping the back of their neck.
    2. Pin their right arm across their body, perpendicular to their torso, by placing your right knee on their right bicep to prevent them from pulling it away. Your left shin should be across their hips, creating a tight base.
    3. Secure the "figure-four" grip: Reach your left arm (the one by their head) around their trapped right arm. Your left hand grips your own right wrist, forming a locked "4" shape. Your left thumb should point towards your own head.
    4. Apply downward pressure with your left forearm on their upper arm, just above the elbow, to keep it pinned to the mat. This acts as your "frame" against their shoulder.
    5. Shift your hips towards their head, lowering your base by bending your left knee and extending your right leg. This compresses their shoulder joint.
    6. Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, leaning your chest into their shoulder. Your head should be positioned on their chest, facing their feet, to prevent them from bridging.
    7. Apply gentle, controlled pressure by lifting your right elbow upwards while simultaneously pressing your left forearm down. This creates internal rotation and hyperextension of the shoulder joint.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly gripping the wrist or forearm: This allows the opponent to rotate their arm, leading to a potential elbow hyperextension injury (valgus stress on the elbow joint). The correct grip must be on your own wrist, creating a stable figure-four.
    • Rushing the finish with excessive upward force: This can cause a rotator cuff tear or a shoulder dislocation by forcing the humerus out of the glenoid fossa. Instead, focus on isolating the arm and applying controlled pressure.
    • Allowing the opponent to bridge or posture up: This breaks the biomechanical leverage of the Americana, often resulting in the attacker's arm being trapped or their structure collapsing, risking shoulder impingement or a strained neck.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the grip transitions and body positioning from side control on an imaginary opponent. Focus on the smoothness of the figure-four. (10 reps each side)
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from side control, focusing on establishing the grips and controlling their arm. (10 reps each side)
    3. Controlled application (25% resistance): Partner allows you to establish the Americana grip and applies minimal resistance to the arm movement. Focus on the body mechanics of the shift and pressure. (5 reps each side)
    4. Adding posture control (50% resistance): Partner attempts to posture up or bridge minimally. Focus on maintaining your base and controlling their upper body. (5 reps each side)
    5. Simulating escapes (75% resistance): Partner attempts a basic escape (e.g., shrimp escape). You maintain the Americana grip and transition to a defensive posture, or re-establish control. (3 reps each side)
    6. Live rolling application (90-100% resistance): Attempt the Americana from side control or mount during rolling. Focus on correct setup and execution, not necessarily finishing. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established a solid side control position and the opponent's arm is trapped across their body.
    • When you are in mount and can isolate one of the opponent's arms, preventing them from defending with their free limbs.
    • As a follow-up to a failed submission or sweep where you end up in a dominant position with their arm exposed.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Defense by "framing" with the free arm: If the opponent attempts the Americana from side control, the defender can attempt to "frame" their free arm (the one not being attacked) between their own head and the attacker's arm, creating space to prevent the figure-four grip.
    • "Shrimping" to create space: If the attacker is slow to lock up the figure-four, the defender can "shrimp" their hips away, creating enough space to pull their arm out or to start to turn their body towards the attacker.
    • "Stacking" to relieve pressure: The defender can attempt to "stack" their weight onto the attacker by driving their hips forward and their head down, compressing the attacker's structure and relieving pressure on the shoulder joint.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Americana?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are gripping your own forearm or hand too loosely, or you are allowing your wrist to bend excessively. For a stable and pain-free grip, ensure your "figure-four" is tight, with your left hand gripping your right wrist firmly. Your left thumb should point towards your head, and your right forearm should be parallel to the opponent's upper arm, not angled downwards, which stresses your wrist. Focus on using your elbow and shoulder to drive the pressure, not just your wrist.

    Q: How can I Americana a much bigger opponent if they are strong?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and technique are even more critical. Do not try to overpower them. Focus on isolating their arm completely by using your shin on their bicep and your knee on their shoulder to prevent them from posturing. Once you have the figure-four grip, use your body weight and hip angle to compress their shoulder. Shift your hips towards their head and lower your base; this creates a strong fulcrum point. Lean your chest into their shoulder and apply the upward pressure with your elbow while maintaining a tight frame with your other arm.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an Americana from side control?

    The optimal time to attempt an Americana from side control is when you have successfully "flattened out" your opponent, meaning their back is against the mat and their hips are not mobile. This is often after a successful posture control or a Guard Pass. Ensure their trapped arm is perpendicular to their body and you have established your shin-on-bicep control. If they are actively posturing or have mobility, it's better to focus on improving your control or transitioning to another submission.
