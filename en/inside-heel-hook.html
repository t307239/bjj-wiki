Inside Heel Hook: A Biomechanically Precise BJJ Guide for White Belts

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Inside Heel Hook is a powerful submission originating from various positions where you can isolate an opponent's leg, most commonly from the 50-50 guard or Ashi Garami variations.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of the required hip rotation and knee flexion, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is creating a strong "figure four" grip on the leg while simultaneously driving your hips forward to hyperextend the opponent's knee and ankle.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a "figure four" grip on the opponent's heel: Your forearm cups the back of their heel, and your other arm's bicep presses against their Achilles tendon.
    2. Your opponent's foot is trapped between your armpit and your chest, with their heel pointed towards your hips.
    3. Position your hips so they are perpendicular to the opponent's leg you are attacking.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly upward, creating a fulcrum point at your hip.
    5. Simultaneously, rotate your hips internally, causing your chest to move towards their knee.
    6. Flex your attacking leg at the knee, bringing your heel towards your own glute, which further isolates their heel.
    7. Apply pressure by straightening your attacking leg and extending your hips, creating torque on the ankle and knee.
    8. Maintain a strong chest-to-chest connection to prevent them from escaping or turning into you.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure before establishing the figure-four grip: This can lead to the opponent easily escaping or turning their foot, potentially damaging your own ankle or knee ligaments if you are not anchored.
    • Pushing the opponent's knee away instead of driving hips forward: This creates a weak lever and puts excessive strain on your own shoulder and elbow joints, risking a tear.
    • Arching your back excessively to create leverage: This strains your lumbar spine and offers no biomechanical advantage for the heel hook, often resulting in lower back pain.
    • Attempting the heel hook from a compromised position like open guard without proper control: This opens you up to sweeps and passes, and if the opponent pulls their leg out incorrectly, it can cause knee ligament tears for both parties.
    • Not understanding the "breaking" point: The submission is applied through hyperextension. Forcing it without proper mechanics can lead to uncontrolled joint damage for the opponent.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the figure-four grip and hip positioning with no resistance. Focus on the feeling of the grip and the angle of your hips (10 reps each side).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a willing partner lying down, practice securing the figure-four grip and moving your hips to the correct perpendicular angle. Focus on the smooth transition of weight (10 reps each side).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to leg isolation. Practice securing the grip and starting the hip drive. Focus on maintaining control as they resist (10 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (controlled submission): Practice the full Inside Heel Hook motion, but stop immediately upon feeling any tension in the opponent's ankle or knee. Partner taps early. Focus on correct finishing mechanics (10 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Inside Heel Hook from a pre-established position (e.g., starting in 50-50). Opponent is aware and taps early to any submission attempt. Focus on timing and transition (5 attempts per round, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (full intention): Attempt the Inside Heel Hook in a live rolling scenario. Opponent defends and attempts to escape. Focus on applying the technique with full commitment and recognizing when to finish or transition (10% of rolling time, 90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent attempts a guard pass and their leg becomes exposed and available for isolation.
    • From the 50-50 guard, when you have established control of their heel and ankle.
    • During scrambles where you can quickly secure a leg and transition to a heel hook position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Escape: The opponent immediately pulls their leg out of the "figure four" by extending their knee and stepping their foot away, often followed by a hip escape.
    • Turning In: The opponent turns their body towards your attacking leg, attempting to relieve pressure on their ankle and potentially create a sweep or pass.
    • Stack Pass Defense: The opponent attempts to "stack" their weight onto your hips, driving their hips forward to break your control and flatten you out.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do the Inside Heel Hook?

    Your ankle likely hurts because you are not creating a proper "figure four" grip, allowing the opponent's heel to slip. This forces you to use your own ankle as a pivot point, which is not designed for that kind of torque. Ensure your forearm is cupping the back of their heel and your bicep is pressing into their Achilles tendon, trapping the foot firmly between your armpit and chest.

    Q: How can I finish an Inside Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on securing a deep "figure four" grip, ensuring their heel is completely trapped. Drive your hips forward with maximum force and rotate your own hips internally. The key is to make their leg the lever and your hips the fulcrum, creating a strong hyperextension of their ankle and knee without needing brute strength.

    Q: Attempt the Inside Heel Hook when you have achieved a dominant leg entanglement, such as the 50-50 guard, and have isolated their heel. Avoid it if their leg is not securely trapped, if you are in a compromised position where they can easily stack you, or if you are unsure of the correct mechanics, as this increases injury risk for both practitioners.
