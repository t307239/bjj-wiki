The Inside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the knee joint, initiated typically from a 50-50 guard or similar leg entanglement. Its goal is to hyperextend the opponent's knee, forcing a tap.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of the precise angles and pressure required, leading to uncontrolled movements and potential injury to themselves or their partner.

The key mechanical insight is isolating the opponent's heel and creating a fulcrum with your hips to apply rotational torque directly to the knee, bypassing the ankle's natural flexibility.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a position where you have control of your opponent's leg, such as the 50-50 guard. Your hips should be elevated, creating space beneath your opponent's leg.
    2. Secure a "Grip" on the opponent's heel: Place your forearm across the front of their ankle bone, securing your own wrist with your other hand (this is the "figure-four" grip).
    3. Position your opponent's leg: Ensure their knee is bent and their foot is facing away from your body, perpendicular to your torso.
    4. Hip angle: Rotate your hips 45 degrees away from the opponent's leg you are attacking. This sets up the lever.
    5. Weight transfer: Shift your weight onto your hips, driving them down and slightly forward, creating pressure into your opponent's thigh.
    6. Knee drive: Drive your own knee (on the same side as the attacked leg) towards the mat, creating a stable base and preventing them from easily escaping.
    7. Apply the lock: Squeeze your figure-four grip and extend your hips backward while simultaneously driving your own knee downwards. This creates a rotational force on the opponent's knee joint.
    8. Maintain control: Keep your hips pinned to their thigh to prevent them from posturing up or turning out of the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Hyperextending your own knee: Attempting to 'kick' your leg out to finish the heel hook can lead to a knee hyperextension injury on yourself if your opponent resists strongly or if you lose control of the leg. The correct movement is a hip extension and a controlled pull, not a kick.
    • Applying pressure too early or without proper control: Grabbing the heel and immediately trying to 'pull' can cause the opponent's knee to twist awkwardly, leading to ACL or MCL tears. You must establish the figure-four grip and hip positioning first before applying finishing pressure.
    • Allowing the opponent to bridge or stack: If your hips are not properly angled and your base is too narrow, the opponent can bridge or stack into you, potentially injuring your own knee or ankle. Ensure your hips are driving into their thigh and your supporting knee is grounded.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation Drill (0% resistance): Practice the grip (figure-four on your own leg) and hip movement (rotation and drive) 20 times per side. Focus on the feeling of the lever.
    2. Partner Assisted Control (25% resistance): Partner lies on their back. You practice securing the figure-four grip and achieving the correct hip angle without applying any pressure. Partner offers minimal resistance to grip. 10 reps per side.
    3. Controlled Leg Placement (50% resistance): From 50-50, practice securing the grip and positioning the leg. Partner offers slight resistance to leg movement. 15 reps per side.
    4. Partial Submission Drill (75% resistance): Execute the entire sequence, applying just enough pressure to feel the tension in the knee joint. Partner taps immediately upon feeling any significant pressure. 10 reps per side.
    5. Flow Drill (90% resistance): Practice the submission from the entry to the finish, allowing for minor adjustments and reactions from the partner. Partner defends minimally and taps when the lock is fully applied. 5 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (100% resistance): Attempt the Inside Heel Hook in a controlled sparring situation. Focus on correct setup and execution, prioritizing safety and tapping early if caught. 1-minute rounds, 5 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • 50-50 Guard: When you have established a strong 50-50 guard with control of your opponent's heel and ankle.
    • Broken Ashi Garami: If your opponent's leg is isolated and you can secure the inside heel hook grip before they can recover.
    • Transitioning from other Leg Entanglements: When an opponent attempts to pass or escape other leg entanglements, creating an opening for the Inside Heel Hook.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Heel Peel" Escape: As the opponent secures the figure-four grip, immediately rotate your hips towards their attacking leg and use your free leg to push their hip away. This breaks the hip angle and relieves pressure.
    • The "Stack Pass" Defense: If the opponent attempts to drive their hips down, bridge your hips up and forward, stacking into their chest. This closes the distance and makes it difficult for them to create the necessary lever.
    • The "Turn-Out" Escape: As pressure is applied, rotate your entire body away from the attacking leg, attempting to face your opponent. This often allows you to relieve the rotational pressure on your knee and potentially escape the entanglement.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Inside Heel Hook as a white belt?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not isolating the heel correctly or applying pressure too early. The Inside Heel Hook targets the rotational stability of the knee. Ensure you have a firm figure-four grip on the heel and ankle, and that your hips are angled correctly (45 degrees away) before driving your hips down and extending. This creates a controlled lever, rather than a wild twist.

    Q: How can I apply an Inside Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are even more critical. Focus on securing a deep figure-four grip on their heel and ankle, ensuring their foot is perpendicular to your body. Use your hips to create a strong fulcrum by angling them 45 degrees away and driving them into their thigh. Your own body weight and the angle of your hips will generate the necessary torque, rather than relying on raw strength.

    Q: What is the difference between an Inside and Outside Heel Hook in BJJ?

    The primary difference lies in the orientation of the opponent's leg and the direction of the applied force. An Inside Heel Hook targets the knee when the opponent's foot is facing away from your body, and the force is applied rotationally inward. An Outside Heel Hook is applied when the opponent's foot is facing towards your body, and the force is applied rotationally outward, often hyperextending the knee in the opposite direction.
