Inside Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Inside Heel Hook targets the opponent's heel from specific leg entanglement positions. It achieves a submission by hyperextending the knee and rotating the ankle joint.

White belts often fail due to a lack of control over their own body and a tendency to 'muscle' the submission rather than using leverage.

The key mechanical insight is controlling the opponent's hip and knee while isolating the heel, creating a fulcrum for rotation and extension.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard or similar leg entanglement, establish a "figure-four" grip on the opponent's leg: your right arm's bicep hooks under their thigh, and your left arm's bicep hooks under their calf, with your hands gripping your own biceps.
    2. Your hips should be elevated, creating space between your torso and their hip, with your left hip angled slightly towards their head.
    3. Drive your right knee into the opponent's hip to prevent them from turning out and to maintain superior control.
    4. Slice your left foot across the opponent's ankle, placing it on the outside of their heel to prevent them from rotating their foot out.
    5. Rotate your hips 90 degrees counter-clockwise, so your chest faces their knee.
    6. Begin to extend your hips away from their body while simultaneously curling your heels towards your glutes, creating torque.
    7. Apply pressure by extending your hips forward and squeezing your heels together, drawing their heel into the "V" of your elbows and hands, which isolates the ankle and knee.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying pressure by "pulling hard" on the opponent's foot: This can cause a severe ACL or MCL tear in the opponent's knee due to uncontrolled internal or external rotation and valgus stress.
    • Failing to control the opponent's hip with your knee: This allows them to turn out, potentially twisting your own knee or ankle, or escaping the position entirely.
    • Not isolating the heel properly, leading to a "loose" grip: This can result in an uncontrolled torque on the opponent's ankle, causing a sprain or fracture, while also risking injury to yourself if their leg slips.
    • Attempting to crank the knee without hip extension: This places excessive shearing force on the knee joint, leading to ligament damage.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0%): Practice the "figure-four" grip and hip elevation. Focus on hip angle and knee placement. (20 reps)
    2. Partner positional drilling (25%): Partner lies on their back. You establish the 50-50 guard and practice isolating the leg and securing the "figure-four" grip. Partner offers no resistance. (10 reps per side)
    3. Controlled entry (50%): Start from the 50-50 guard. Partner allows you to secure the "figure-four" grip and hip control. You apply slow, controlled extension and rotation. Partner taps immediately upon feeling any pressure. (5 reps per side)
    4. Adding hip escape (75%): Partner tries to escape by bridging or turning. You maintain hip control and execute the submission with moderate pressure. Partner taps. (5 reps per side)
    5. Controlled rolling (90%): Roll with a partner who understands leg lock defense. You actively seek the Inside Heel Hook. Partner defends, and you work to secure the position and apply submission. Taps are frequent. (3 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Incorporate the Inside Heel Hook into your regular rolling. Be mindful of your partner's safety and tap early to submissions. (5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard and have secured control of the opponent's leg.
    • When your opponent attempts to turn into you from a leg entanglement, creating an opportunity for isolation.
    • When you can successfully "climb" your leg over their hip from a guard position, enabling a clean entry into the 50-50.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Hide" Defense: As the opponent attempts the figure-four grip, immediately bring your free leg across their hips and push their torso away, creating space to escape the entanglement. This prevents the isolation.
    • The "Turn Out" Defense: If the opponent secures the figure-four but you still have mobility, aggressively turn your body away from the submission, rotating your hips and feet to face the direction of the attack. This can relieve pressure and allow you to break the grip.
    • The "Straighten and Pull" Defense: If the opponent has a loose figure-four, try to actively straighten your trapped leg and pull it towards your body, creating slack and making it difficult for them to apply rotational force. This also allows you to potentially escape the entanglement.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the Inside Heel Hook?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not isolating the opponent's heel correctly. This means the rotational force intended for the ankle is being transferred to the knee joint, causing shearing stress. Ensure your "figure-four" grip is tight, your hips are controlling their hip, and you are extending your hips *before* applying the rotational torque to their ankle.

    Q: How do I Inside Heel Hook against a bigger opponent who has a strong base?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on hip control and leverage rather than brute strength. Use your right knee to drive into their hip to prevent them from posturing up or turning. Your left foot should actively slice across their ankle to prevent them from escaping. The key is to get your hips to the correct angle to generate torque, rather than trying to overpower their limb.

    Q: When is the best time to go for an Inside Heel Hook in a roll?

    The Inside Heel Hook is best attempted when you have achieved a dominant leg entanglement, most commonly the 50-50 guard. It's also a good option when your opponent is actively trying to turn into you from a vulnerable leg position, as this often creates the necessary alignment for a clean entry. Avoid attempting it when you are out of position or lack control, as this increases the risk of injury to both parties.
