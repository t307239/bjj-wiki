The 50/50 Guard is an open guard position where you control one of your opponent's legs with both of yours, creating a strong base for attacks and sweeps.

White belts often struggle by creating a loose structure, allowing their opponent to easily pass or escape the entanglement.

The core principle is to create a strong, stable 'figure-four' lock on the opponent's leg, preventing them from posturing or rotating, while maintaining your own base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated guard or knee shield, secure a standard 'inside leg hook' on your opponent's right leg with your left leg, your left shin crossing behind their knee and your left foot hooking their hip.
    2. Simultaneously, bring your right leg over your opponent's right leg, placing your right shin across the front of their right thigh, just above the knee.
    3. Your right foot should hook the outside of your opponent's right ankle, creating a figure-four lock with your own legs around their leg.
    4. Maintain a strong grip on your opponent's right ankle with your right hand, pulling it towards your chest to control their base and prevent them from stepping out.
    5. Use your left arm to secure a sleeve grip (e.g., right opponent's right sleeve) or a collar grip to control their posture and prevent them from posturing up.
    6. Keep your hips anchored to the mat, creating a stable base and preventing them from easily stacking you.
    7. Drive your left knee towards your opponent's left hip to maintain distance and control their rotational movement, forcing them to face you.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'pull' their leg forcefully without proper hip connection: This can lead to elbow hyperextension or shoulder dislocation if the opponent posts their weight unexpectedly.

    The correct movement is to use your shin and hip to control their leg's angle, not just brute pulling force.

    • Allowing your opponent to stack you by not maintaining hip connection: This can cause lumbar spine compression injuries or knee ligament damage if they drive their weight directly into your hips.

    Always maintain hip contact and drive your knee towards their opposite hip to prevent stacking.

    • Releasing your grip on the ankle prematurely when transitioning: This can result in a knee ligament tear if they violently spin or step out while your leg is still entangled.

    Maintain a secure ankle grip until your next position or sweep is fully established.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg entanglement and grip acquisition from a seated position on the mat. 10 reps per side.
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner lies on their back. You enter the 50/50 guard and establish grips. Partner remains passive. 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to your entanglement and grip acquisition. Focus on maintaining the figure-four. 25% resistance. 5 reps per side.
    4. Sweep attempt drilling: From the established 50/50, practice initiating a basic sweep (e.g., hip bump sweep) while the partner offers light defense. 50% resistance. 3 reps per side.
    5. Guard retention drilling: Partner attempts to break your 50/50 guard and pass. You focus on maintaining the entanglement and re-establishing grips. 75% resistance. 3 mins.
    6. Live rolling: Implement the 50/50 guard during rolling, focusing on establishing it and attempting sweeps or submissions. 90% resistance. 5 mins.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up in your closed guard, creating an opportunity to isolate a leg.
    • When your opponent attempts a pass that allows you to catch their leg, such as a knee-slice or torreando pass.
    • When you are in your open guard and your opponent is standing or kneeling, and you can effectively trap one of their legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Pummeling: If the opponent tries to extract their leg, use your free leg (the one not in the figure-four) to pummel in between their legs, creating space to escape or transition.
    • Hip Escape and Shrimp: If the opponent successfully stacks you, perform a strong hip escape and shrimp motion away from their weight to relieve pressure and re-establish your base.
    • Transition to Single Leg X-Guard: If the opponent defends the 50/50 by putting pressure on your hips, you can often transition to a Single Leg X-Guard by switching your leg hooks and controlling their ankle with your feet on their hips.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to get into 50/50 Guard?

    Knee pain in the 50/50 Guard often stems from your knee cap not being aligned with your shin and ankle. When you secure the figure-four, ensure your shin is directly across the opponent's thigh, not angled. Also, avoid letting your knee buckle inward; keep it slightly bowed out to protect the medial collateral ligament (MCL). If you feel pain, immediately release the position and check your alignment.

    Q: How do I use 50/50 Guard against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is leverage and hip control, not brute strength. Focus on maintaining a tight figure-four lock on their leg and using your hips to control their base. Drive your knee into their opposite hip to prevent them from posturing and using their weight advantage. Secure a strong collar or sleeve grip to break down their posture and create opportunities for sweeps.

    Q: When should I transition out of 50/50 Guard if I'm not getting a sweep?

    If your opponent is effectively neutralizing your sweeps by maintaining a strong base or is successfully defending your attempts to create openings, it's time to consider transitioning. Look for cues like them driving their weight heavily onto your hips, or if they manage to get their hips away from your entanglement. A common transition is to the Single Leg X-Guard or a sweep attempt that uses their defensive posture against them, like a leg drag sweep.
