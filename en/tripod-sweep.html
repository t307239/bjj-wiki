The Tripod Sweep starts from Closed Guard and aims to off-balance your opponent to achieve a dominant top position.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or poor body positioning, leading to inefficiency and injury.

The key is to use your legs to create a stable tripod, then use your hips and core to generate leverage for the sweep.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a double sleeve grip: Right hand grips opponent's right sleeve at the wrist, left hand grips opponent's left sleeve at the wrist. Keep elbows tucked.
    2. Establish right leg hook: Insert your right foot's sole into the opponent's left hip crease, with your knee pointing slightly towards their right shoulder.
    3. Establish left leg tripod base: Place your left foot flat on the mat, directly beside your opponent's left hip, forming a tripod with your right foot and left hand.
    4. Control opponent's posture: Use your sleeve grips to pull their upper body slightly forward, breaking their base.
    5. Hip elevation and angle: Elevate your hips off the mat by contracting your glutes and lower back. Angle your hips towards your opponent's left side.
    6. Sweep motion: Simultaneously extend your right leg out to the side (away from your opponent's body) while driving your hips forward and upward.
    7. Maintain tripod pressure: Keep your left hand firmly planted on the mat and your left foot anchored to maintain balance and pressure as you sweep.
    8. Follow through to top position: As your opponent falls, immediately shift your weight onto their chest and secure side control or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting the sweep by simply pushing with your hands and legs: This puts excessive strain on the shoulder joints and elbow ligaments, risking rotator cuff tears or sprains from hyper-extension.
    • Rolling your hips outwards without elevating them: This can lead to lumbar spine hyperextension injuries, especially if the opponent resists with weight.
    • Gripping too wide on the sleeves or failing to maintain sleeve control: This allows the opponent to easily escape the grip, potentially causing your arms to flail and injure your wrists or elbows.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the body mechanics of the sweep without a partner (hip elevation, leg extension, tripod setup). 10 reps.
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): Partner lies on their back in your closed guard. Practice setting grips, leg hooks, and the tripod motion without any resistance. 10 reps.
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the hip and leg movements. Focus on correct weight transfer. 10 reps.
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the sweep by posturing up or shifting weight. Focus on maintaining control and timing. 15 reps.
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner actively defends the sweep with controlled resistance, simulating a live roll scenario. Focus on finishing the sweep and transitioning. 15 reps.
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Spar with a partner, attempting the Tripod Sweep when the opportunity arises. Focus on successful execution and follow-through. 3 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent's weight is forward, creating an opportunity to break their posture.
    • Opponent's hips are close to yours, allowing for effective leg hook insertion.
    • Opponent attempts to pass your guard and their base is unstable.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent stacks: If the opponent begins to stack you, immediately release the sleeve grips, widen your base with your feet on their hips, and push them away to regain space.
    • Opponent flattens your guard: If the opponent flattens your guard, immediately reset by bringing your knees back to your chest and re-establishing closed guard.
    • Opponent escapes the sweep: If the sweep is initiated but the opponent escapes, immediately transition to another sweep or submission from guard, or reset to closed guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Tripod Sweep?

    Wrist pain often occurs if you're gripping the opponent's sleeves too loosely or too wide. This forces your wrists to bend backward to maintain control, straining the ligaments. Always grip the opponent's sleeve at the wrist, keeping your elbows tucked and your forearms tight to your body for a strong, controlled grip.

    Q: How do I Tripod Sweep against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise timing are crucial. Focus on breaking their posture *before* initiating the sweep. Use your sleeve grips to pull their upper body forward, then quickly insert your leg hook and establish your tripod base. Elevate your hips aggressively and drive them in the direction of the sweep, using their weight against them.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Tripod Sweep in BJJ?

    The Tripod Sweep is most effective when the opponent's weight is forward and they are close to you. This typically happens when they are posturing up, trying to pass your guard, or are in a kneeling position. If they are postured high and far away, other sweeps might be more suitable.
