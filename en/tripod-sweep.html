Tripod Sweep: Your First BJJ Sweep

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This sweep originates from Closed Guard, aiming to invert your opponent and create an angle to sweep them over. It leverages your legs to control their base and initiate movement.

White belts often struggle by trying to muscle the sweep rather than using precise body mechanics and timing. This leads to wasted energy and potential injury.

The key is to create a strong, stable tripod with your legs and hips, then use a controlled lifting motion to destabilize their base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish Double Sleeve Grip: With your opponent in your closed guard, secure a grip on both of their sleeves, just above the elbow. Your thumbs should be pointing upwards.
    2. Control One Pant Leg: While maintaining sleeve grips, reach one hand under their arm and grip their pants near the knee of the leg you intend to sweep with. This grip should be firm, ensuring you can control their leg.
    3. Break Posture: Pull both sleeves downwards and slightly towards your chest, while simultaneously pushing your hips slightly away to break their posture and bring their weight forward.
    4. Place Foot on Hip: As you break their posture, take the foot on the same side as your pant grip and place it firmly on their hip, directly on the bone. This creates your first point of contact for the tripod.
    5. Extend and Lift Leg: While maintaining sleeve control and the hip-to-foot connection, begin to extend the leg that is on their hip, creating upward pressure. Simultaneously, lift the opposite leg towards your chest.
    6. Drive Knee to Chest: As you extend the hip leg, drive the knee of the leg you are lifting towards their chest, forcing their upper body further forward.
    7. Initiate the Sweep: With your opponent's weight now heavily on their front foot and their base compromised by your tripod, use your sleeve grips to pull them forward and over your extended hip leg. Simultaneously, push off their hip with your foot.
    8. Rotate and Follow: As they fall, rotate your hips to the side and bring your legs through to establish a superior position, ideally side control or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to lift the opponent's entire body with just your arms: This can strain your shoulders and elbows, as the primary lifting force should come from your hips and legs. The correct movement involves using your legs to create leverage and the sweep is initiated by destabilizing their base, not brute strength.
    • Hyperextending the knee on the hip: Placing the foot on the hip and then aggressively straightening the leg can put excessive pressure on the knee joint, potentially leading to a torn ACL or MCL. Instead, maintain a slight bend in the knee of the leg on the hip and use it as a stable pivot point.
    • Allowing your legs to become tangled or crossed during the sweep motion: This can lead to ankle or knee injuries if the opponent falls awkwardly or if you try to force the sweep with a compromised base. Ensure your legs maintain a tripod-like structure and your hips remain mobile throughout the movement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drilling (0% resistance): Practice the grip acquisition, posture break, foot-on-hip placement, and leg extension on yourself. Focus on the smooth transition between each step. (20 reps)
    2. Partner Assisted (25% resistance): With a partner lying passively on their back, practice the entire sweep motion. Focus on maintaining your grips and executing the leg mechanics correctly. (10 reps per side)
    3. Light Resistance (50% resistance): Your partner will lightly resist your attempts to break posture and establish grips. Focus on maintaining control and executing the sweep when the opportunity arises. (10 reps per side)
    4. Controlled Resistance (75% resistance): Your partner will actively try to defend your grips and prevent you from establishing the tripod. Focus on adapting your grips and timing. (8 reps per side)
    5. Dynamic Resistance (90% resistance): Your partner will move and react more realistically, attempting to counter your attempts. Focus on maintaining your structure and executing the sweep under pressure. (6 reps per side)
    6. Live Rolling (100% resistance): Attempt the Tripod Sweep during sparring. Focus on applying the technique learned and adapting to your partner's reactions. (5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent has their knees close to their chest, making it difficult for them to establish a solid base.
    • When your opponent is posturing up and leaning forward, creating an opportunity to break their posture further.
    • When your opponent tries to pass your guard by stacking you, as this often leaves them vulnerable to a tripod sweep.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If your opponent begins to establish the tripod, immediately hip escape to create space and re-establish your closed guard or transition to an open guard. This prevents them from completing the sweep.
    • Stacking and Pressure Passing: If you feel the opponent attempting to lift your leg, aggressively stack your weight onto them and drive forward. This can disrupt their base and allow you to transition to a pressure pass.
    • Knee-on-Hip Defense: As the opponent places their foot on your hip, use your own knee to block their foot or push it away. Simultaneously, use your hands to push their head away and maintain your base.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the Tripod Sweep?

    Your knee might hurt if you are overextending the leg that is placed on the opponent's hip. This leg needs to act as a stable pivot, not a lever that is fully straightened. Maintain a slight bend in that knee throughout the sweep to protect the joint. Also, ensure your hips are mobile enough to create the angle, preventing undue stress on your knee.

    Q: How can I Tripod Sweep against a much bigger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus more on precise timing and posture control. Instead of trying to lift them, concentrate on breaking their posture so their weight is forward. Use your sleeve grips to pull their upper body down and towards you, making their weight distribution unstable. The leg on their hip becomes a fulcrum to pivot them over, not to lift their mass.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from a failed Tripod Sweep attempt?

    If your opponent successfully defends the sweep, do not force it. Instead, use the momentum of their defense to transition. For example, if they stack you, use that pressure to drive into a Guard Pass like a knee slice. If they escape your leg, immediately try to re-establish your closed guard or transition to a sweep like the scissor sweep if their base is still compromised.
