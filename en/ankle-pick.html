The Ankle Pick is a single-leg takedown initiated from a standing position, aiming to secure one of your opponent's ankles and drive them to the mat.

White belts often struggle by lunging forward without establishing control or by leaving their own base too exposed, leading to missed opportunities and potential injury.

The key insight is to use your head as a wedge and your hips as a lever to off-balance and elevate the opponent's leg, not just pull.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent stands in a staggered stance, weight primarily on their lead leg. Your lead leg is slightly forward, maintaining a base width of approximately shoulder-width.
    2. Secure a Collar Grip with your lead hand, palm facing away from you, thumb inside the lapel. Your trail hand secures a Sleeve Grip on the opponent's lead arm, palm facing towards you.
    3. Lower your level by bending your knees, keeping your back relatively straight and head up. Your hips should be lower than your opponent's hips.
    4. Drive your lead shoulder into the opponent's chest, creating a slight forward pressure and initiating a head-to-hip connection. Your head should be on the same side as the arm you are gripping.
    5. As you drive forward, simultaneously step your trail leg slightly past your lead leg, towards the outside of your opponent's lead leg.
    6. With your trail hand, reach down and secure a firm Grip on the opponent's lead ankle, specifically gripping the Achilles tendon area with your thumb on the inside.
    7. Drive your head into the opponent's chest while simultaneously lifting their secured ankle upwards and slightly across their body. Your hips should drive forward and slightly upward, off-balancing them.
    8. Continue the forward drive and upward lift of the ankle, guiding the opponent to the mat by their ankle. Maintain your base and control throughout the movement.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly lunging forward with an outstretched arm to grab the ankle: This exposes your cervical spine to hyperextension and anterior neck structures to compression, risking neck injury (e.g., stinger or whiplash).
    • Attempting to pull the ankle with straight legs and a rounded back: This puts excessive strain on the lumbar spine, risking disc herniation or muscle pulls.
    • Over-extending your own knee when driving forward: This can lead to knee ligament sprains (ACL/MCL) or patellar tendonitis.
    • Grabbing the foot instead of the ankle: This provides less leverage and can lead to wrist sprains for the attacker if the opponent resists by pulling their foot away.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling: Practice the entire sequence of grips, level change, head drive, and ankle pick motion without a partner. Focus on body mechanics. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner Static Control: Partner stands passively, allows you to establish grips and execute the level change and simulated ankle pick motion without resisting movement. Focus on grip security and body position. (10 reps, 10% resistance)
    3. Partner Controlled Movement (No Takedown): Partner maintains a balanced stance and slightly resists your initial forward pressure, allowing you to practice the level change and head drive. No actual ankle grab or lift. (15 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner Controlled Movement (Simulated Ankle Pick): Partner maintains a balanced stance and allows you to secure the ankle grip and perform the lift, but does not actively defend or fall. Focus on the feeling of elevation. (15 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Light Resistance Takedown Practice: Partner offers light resistance to your grips and forward drive, allowing you to complete the ankle pick and guide them to the mat. Focus on maintaining balance and control. (10 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling (Takedown Focus): Attempt the ankle pick during live rolling, prioritizing technique over success. If the opportunity arises, go for it. Focus on recognizing the opening and executing the movement. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is in a staggered stance with their lead leg forward and weight distributed.
    • Opponent is posturing up and reaching for your collar or sleeves.
    • Opponent is attempting to create distance with their feet.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Stance Switch" Defense: As the attacker initiates the level change, the defender immediately widens their base and switches their stance to a more square, balanced position, making the ankle pick ineffective.
    • The "Head Pull" Defense: As the attacker drives their head into the chest, the defender actively pulls their head away and uses their free hand to push the attacker's head away, disrupting the wedge.
    • The "Leg Retreat" Defense: As the ankle is being secured, the defender quickly steps their lead leg backward and pivots their hips away, creating space and breaking the grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle pick feel weak and I can't lift my opponent's leg?

    This often happens because you are trying to lift with your arms and back instead of using your hips as the primary lever. Ensure your head is firmly wedged into their chest, creating a connection. Then, drive your hips forward and slightly upward while simultaneously lifting the ankle. Think of it as using your entire body as a single unit to off-balance and elevate.

    Q: How can I use the ankle pick against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are even more critical. Focus on their lead leg and their weight distribution. Wait for them to commit their weight forward or shift it onto their lead leg. Your level change needs to be lower and your head drive more forceful. The goal is to off-balance them so their weight is no longer supporting their leg, allowing you to lift.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an ankle pick in BJJ, and what if they defend?

    The ankle pick is best attempted when your opponent is in a staggered stance, especially when they are posturing up or reaching for grips. If they defend by pulling their leg back, don't commit to the full lift. Instead, use the momentum to transition into a single-leg takedown or a sweep if you are on the bottom. The initial level change and grip can often set up other attacks.
