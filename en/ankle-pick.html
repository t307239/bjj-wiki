Ankle Pick: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Ankle Pick is a dynamic takedown initiated from a standing position, typically when your opponent is postured up or has their weight slightly forward.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or incorrect weight transfer, leading to balance loss and potential injury.

The key is to use your opponent's forward momentum against them by creating a strong, stable base and driving through them as you secure the ankle.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Stance and Grip: Assume a staggered stance, left foot forward, right foot back. Secure a collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left sleeve) with your left hand.
    2. Initial Movement: Take a small step forward with your left foot, pulling slightly on the collar grip to draw your opponent forward.
    3. Posting the Leg: As your opponent reacts to the pull, step your right foot across and slightly behind your left foot, posting it near their left foot.
    4. Hip Angle and Drive: Lower your hips by bending your knees, angling your pelvis forward and slightly to the left, creating a strong base.
    5. Ankle Grip: Simultaneously, reach your right hand down and grip their left ankle with your hand on the inside of their ankle bone (medial malleolus).
    6. Weight Transfer and Sweep: Drive your hips forward and slightly upward, transferring your weight into your opponent's center of mass, while simultaneously sweeping their ankle upwards and off the mat.
    7. Completion: As their ankle is lifted and they lose balance, continue driving forward to complete the takedown, landing in a dominant position like side-control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Hyperextending Knee: Lunging forward without posting your leg correctly can lead to your lead knee collapsing inward, causing a valgus stress injury to the medial collateral ligament (MCL) and meniscus. Always maintain a bent knee and stable base.
    • Neck Strain: Pulling too aggressively on the collar without proper hip engagement can cause your cervical spine to hyperextend or flex excessively, risking disc injury. Focus on driving with your hips and legs, not just your arms.
    • Ankle/Foot Injury: Attempting to lift the opponent's ankle with a straight leg or twisting your own ankle can lead to sprains or fractures. Keep your supporting leg bent and drive with your hips, securing the ankle with a firm grip.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Footwork and Grip Practice: Practice the staggered stance, stepping, and hip angling without a partner. (10 reps each side, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo Ankle Grab and Drive: Stand with a dummy or heavy bag. Practice the ankle grip and hip drive motion. (10 reps each side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partnered Static Drill: With a compliant partner, practice the grip sequence, stepping, and hip drive. Partner offers minimal resistance. (5 reps each side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partnered Dynamic Drill: Partner offers slight resistance to the collar pull, allowing you to execute the ankle pick. Focus on the timing of the leg post and hip drive. (5 reps each side, 50% resistance)
    5. Controlled Takedown Practice: Execute the ankle pick with a partner who understands the technique and allows the takedown to be completed safely. Focus on clean execution and weight transfer. (5 reps each side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling (Takedown Focus): Attempt the ankle pick in live rolling scenarios, focusing on timing and commitment. Understand that not every attempt will be successful. (5-10 attempts, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured high and leaning forward, creating an opening.
    • Immediately after they attempt a standing sweep or pass, as their weight may be shifted.
    • When you've secured a strong collar and sleeve grip and can initiate a pull.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Whizzer: As the opponent reaches for your ankle, counter by posting your free arm (the one not gripping the ankle) over their shoulder and hooking your armpit around their head. This creates a strong frame and prevents them from lifting your leg. Simultaneously, drive your hips back to create space and potentially transition to a single leg.
    • Sprawl and Circle: If they commit to the ankle pick, immediately sprawl your hips back and extend your legs, placing your weight on their back. Circle your hips away from their grip to break their control and create an escape. You can then look to establish a dominant position.
    • Defensive Foot Placement: As they step to post their leg, quickly bring your gripping leg back and plant your foot firmly on the mat, creating a stable base. This prevents them from isolating your ankle and allows you to defend by stepping out or circling away.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try the Ankle Pick?

    Your ankle may hurt because you are attempting to lift your opponent's ankle with a straight leg, or you are not maintaining a stable base with your supporting leg. This can lead to awkward twisting forces on your own ankle. Ensure your supporting leg remains bent and your hips drive the motion, rather than your leg muscles solely. Grip the ankle firmly on the inside, close to the bone.

    Q: How can I Ankle Pick a much bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, the Ankle Pick relies even more heavily on timing and leverage than brute strength. Focus on using their size and forward momentum against them. Secure a strong collar grip and pull to draw them forward. As they step to maintain balance, quickly post your leg and drive your hips through. Your goal is to destabilize their base, not to overpower them physically.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an Ankle Pick in a grappling exchange?

    The Ankle Pick is most effective when your opponent is slightly off-balance and moving forward. This often occurs after they've committed to a forward movement like a pass attempt or a strike. It's also a good option when they are postured high, presenting an accessible ankle. Avoid attempting it when they are rooted, have a strong base, or are actively pulling you down.
