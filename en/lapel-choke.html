This technique is typically initiated from the Closed Guard position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the opponent's brain. It's a fundamental submission for controlling an opponent from the bottom.

Many white belts struggle because they try to force the choke with arm strength alone, neglecting the crucial body mechanics. This leads to inefficiency and potential injury.

The key is using your hips and posture to create the necessary leverage, turning a simple grip into a powerful choke. Think of it as a 'crank' powered by your entire body.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a high closed guard. Your knees should be tight around the opponent's torso, feet crossed behind their back.
    2. Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand on the opponent's left lapel, thumb inside the collar. Your left hand grips the opponent's right lapel, thumb inside.
    3. Arch your back, creating space by driving your hips upwards and slightly forward. This breaks the opponent's posture.
    4. While maintaining the arch, drive your elbows down and slightly outwards, pulling the lapels taut across the opponent's neck.
    5. Simultaneously, bring your knees closer together, squeezing them to further restrict the opponent's ability to create space or posture up.
    6. Rotate your wrists inwards, as if trying to turn a doorknob, to increase the pressure on the carotid arteries.
    7. Maintain head control by keeping your head tight to the opponent's chest or shoulder to prevent them from posturing up or turning their head.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke by simply pulling with your arms: This can strain your shoulder joints (glenohumeral joint) and elbow joints (humeroulnar joint) due to hyperflexion/extension under load. The correct method involves hip drive and torso rotation, not brute arm force.
    • Gripping the lapels too low or too wide: This often leads to the lapels slipping, or worse, the opponent can easily posture up. It also reduces the leverage available for the choke. Always aim for grips that are high on the collarbone and relatively close together.
    • Not creating sufficient hip elevation: Failing to drive your hips up and forward allows the opponent to maintain posture and pressure, negating the choke's effectiveness. This can lead to your own spine being compressed. Focus on the posterior pelvic tilt and hip thrust.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo grip and posture drill: Practice securing the grips and arching your back, holding for 10 seconds. 10 repetitions.
    2. Partnered static drill (0% resistance): From closed guard, partner remains completely still. Execute the choke mechanics, hold for 5 seconds. 10 repetitions.
    3. Partnered light resistance drill (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice hip drive and grip adjustment. Execute choke, hold. 8 repetitions.
    4. Partnered posture control drill (50% resistance): Partner attempts to posture up slightly. Focus on maintaining your hip elevation and grip security. Execute choke. 6 repetitions.
    5. Flow drill (75% resistance): Partner attempts to defend by posturing, creating frames, or turning. Focus on smooth transitions and maintaining control. Execute choke when opportunity arises. 5 repetitions.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Attempt the choke during sparring. Focus on applying the learned mechanics, not just finishing the submission. 3-5 minute rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured down, with their back rounded, making it difficult for them to create frames.
    • When you have successfully locked your guard and the opponent is trying to stack you or is in a compromised posture.
    • When the opponent is attempting to pass your guard and their head is within reach and their collar is accessible.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Posture Up and Frame: The opponent can immediately try to straighten their back, widen their base, and push their hips away from yours. They should also attempt to break your grip by prying your hands off their lapels.
    • Neck Crank Defense: If the choke is being applied incorrectly and feels like a neck crank, the opponent can attempt to turn their head towards the side of the choke, creating space and relieving pressure. They should also try to create frames with their elbows against your hips.
    • Guard Break: The opponent can attempt to break your closed guard by posting their hands on your hips and driving their weight forward, or by using their elbows to create space between your knees. This is often combined with posturing up.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Lapel Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain during the Lapel Choke often stems from gripping the lapels too tightly with the palm side of your hand, creating a sharp flexion at the wrist. Instead, try to cup the lapel and rotate your forearm, using the muscles of your forearm and shoulder to create the choking pressure. Your thumbs should be inside the collar, and your fingers should cup the fabric, allowing for a more natural and less strained grip.

    Q: How do I Lapel Choke a much bigger opponent from closed guard in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your body mechanics are even more critical. Focus on creating significant hip elevation to break their posture, making them lighter on top. Use your legs to squeeze their hips together, limiting their ability to create space. The choke itself needs to be applied with a strong rotation of your wrists and a tightening of your grip, driven by your torso, rather than trying to overpower their mass with your arms.

    Q: When should I NOT attempt a Lapel Choke from closed guard in BJJ?

    You should avoid attempting a Lapel Choke when the opponent has excellent posture and is actively posturing up, making it difficult to secure your grips or create leverage. Also, if they have their elbows firmly planted on your hips or are actively creating frames, it signals they are prepared to defend this specific attack. In these situations, focus on maintaining your guard and transitioning to a sweep or a different submission that better suits the positional dynamics.
