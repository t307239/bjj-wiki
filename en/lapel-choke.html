Lapel Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide to BJJ

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Lapel Choke is typically applied from Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain. It's a fundamental submission that teaches control and pressure.

White belts often struggle with this choke due to a lack of understanding of leverage and proper grip engagement. They tend to pull with their arms instead of using their body.

The key mechanical insight is to use your hips and chest to create a tightening mechanism, rather than solely relying on bicep strength. This maximizes efficiency and reduces strain.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a 'figure-four' grip on the opponent's gi lapels: right hand grips the opponent's left lapel, left hand grips the opponent's right lapel. Fingers should be deep inside the collar.
    2. Ensure your hips are elevated slightly, creating a small gap between your lower back and the mat. This allows for better hip drive.
    3. Your knees should be tight to your opponent's sides, maintaining a secure closed guard. Ankles crossed behind their back.
    4. Begin to drive your hips upwards towards your opponent's chest, simultaneously crunching your upper body inwards.
    5. As your hips drive up, use your forearms to pull the lapels across the sides of your opponent's neck, digging them into the carotid arteries.
    6. Your elbows should be tucked in close to your body, acting as anchors to prevent the lapels from slipping.
    7. Continue to drive your hips and squeeze your chest inwards, effectively cinching the lapels against the opponent's neck for the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive downward pressure with your hands on the lapels can strain your wrists and elbows. This can lead to ligamentous tears in the wrist joint (radiocarpal or ulnocarpal joints) from hyperextension or ulnar deviation, and can also cause biceps tendonitis. The correct movement involves driving your hips and squeezing with your chest, not pulling with your arms.
    • Attempting to 'muscle' the choke by simply pulling with your arms can lead to shoulder impingement. This occurs when the humerus head is compressed against the acromion process during forceful adduction and internal rotation, damaging the rotator cuff. Instead, focus on hip elevation and chest compression.
    • Overextending your neck backwards to create space can strain your cervical spine. This can result in facet joint irritation or even disc compression, leading to neck pain and potential nerve impingement. Maintain a neutral head position and use your hips for leverage.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip acquisition and hip drive motion without a partner. Focus on feeling the movement. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a compliant partner in your closed guard, practice the full choke mechanics, focusing on grip depth and hip movement. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to refine your hip drive and pressure application. (20 reps)
    4. Flow drilling: Execute the choke as a sequence within a series of other movements from closed guard, focusing on smooth transitions. (30 reps)
    5. Controlled sparring (90% resistance): Attempt the choke during rolling, but tap early if the submission is not achieved to avoid injury. (5 minutes)
    6. Live sparring (100% resistance): Apply the choke during live rolling, focusing on timing and correct execution under pressure. (10 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing down towards you in your closed guard.
    • When you have established a strong, deep grip on their lapels.
    • When your hips are mobile and you can effectively drive them upwards.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Grip Break: The opponent can quickly reach inside your grip and peel your fingers off their lapels, or use their thumbs to break your grip. The counter is to maintain tight posture and prevent them from reaching your grips.
    • Posture Up and Squeeze: The opponent can attempt to posture up and squeeze their shoulders together, making it difficult to cinch the lapels. The counter is to maintain tight hips and drive them down while squeezing.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Lapel Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain during a Lapel Choke often stems from gripping too tightly with your fingers and trying to 'pull' the choke closed with your arms. This puts excessive strain on your wrist joint, forcing it into awkward angles. The correct biomechanical approach is to use your hips to drive upwards and your chest to squeeze inwards, using your arms primarily to guide the lapels. This transfers the power generation to larger muscle groups and reduces stress on your wrists.

    Q: How can I apply a Lapel Choke against a bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paraMount for the Lapel Choke. Focus on obtaining very deep grips inside their collar, almost to the shoulder. As you drive your hips up, ensure your knees are tight to their ribs, preventing them from posturing up. The key is to create a tight 'frame' with your legs and hips, then use your upper body to cinch the lapels across their neck. Don't try to match their strength; instead, use your body weight and leverage to create the choke.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Lapel Choke, and what if my opponent defends by bridging?

    The optimal time to attempt a Lapel Choke is when your opponent has committed to posturing down, creating a compact target. If they bridge to defend, this can actually be advantageous. As they bridge, drive your hips up into their bridge, creating a powerful upward force that compresses the lapels against their neck. This upward hip thrust combined with a slight inward chest squeeze can often finish the choke even as they attempt to escape.
