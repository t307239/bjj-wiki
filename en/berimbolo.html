Berimbolo: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Berimbolo is a dynamic guard transition, typically initiated from open guard, aiming to achieve a back-Mount position. It involves a specific leg entanglement and hip rotation.

White belts often struggle with the Berimbolo due to a lack of body awareness and an overreliance on brute strength, leading to inefficient movement and potential injury.

The core principle is using your hips and legs to 'swim' around your opponent's base, creating momentum and leverage for the sweep and Back Take.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Establish a deep collar grip with your left hand, pulling your opponent's collar towards your chest. Your right hand should grip their right sleeve at the wrist.
    2. Base: Your left knee should be on the mat, pointed towards your opponent's left hip. Your right leg is extended, with your right foot flat on the mat to establish a pivot point.
    3. Hip Angle: Rotate your hips 45 degrees away from your opponent, tucking your left hip towards the mat.
    4. Leg Drive: Drive your opponent forward slightly with your collar grip, initiating their weight shift.
    5. Hooking Leg: As your opponent moves forward, swing your right leg up and over their hips, aiming to hook their left thigh with your right hamstring.
    6. Pelvic Rotation: Simultaneously, aggressively rotate your hips and torso towards your right, using your left knee as a pivot point on the mat.
    7. Sweeping Motion: Your left leg now acts as a base while your right leg drives your opponent over your left hip, initiating the sweep.
    8. Transition to Back: As they fall, continue your hip rotation and bring your left leg into a position to secure the back mount, ensuring your left shin is across their body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Injury: Attempting to 'jump' your hips over your opponent's legs without proper hip rotation can cause your knee to twist laterally under load. This can tear the ACL or MCL.
    • Correct Movement: Focus on rotating your hips and driving your opponent forward to create space, rather than trying to force your body over them.
    • Ankle Sprain: Planting your right foot flat on the mat and then twisting your body without releasing the plant can lead to an ankle roll.
    • Correct Movement: Ensure your right foot can pivot freely on the mat as you rotate your hips; a slight lift and repositioning is often necessary.
    • Lower Back Strain: Arching your lower back excessively while trying to 'pull' your opponent over can strain your lumbar spine, especially if your core is not engaged.
    • Correct Movement: Maintain a neutral spine and use your hips and legs to generate the sweeping power, keeping your core tight.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Rotation Drill: Practice the hip rotation and pelvic tucking motion without any partner or resistance. Focus on smooth, controlled movement. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Leg Hook Drill: Practice swinging your right leg over an imaginary opponent's hips and hooking the thigh. (50 reps per leg)
    3. Partnered Hip Push and Leg Swing: With a willing partner (0% resistance), have them gently push you forward. Practice your hip rotation and leg hook as they shift their weight. (10 reps)
    4. Assisted Berimbolo Setup: Your partner (25% resistance) allows you to establish grips and initiates a slight weight shift. You practice the initial hip rotation and leg swing. (10 reps)
    5. Controlled Berimbolo Sweep: Your partner (50% resistance) allows the sweep to happen more naturally, focusing on not resisting the fall. You execute the full Berimbolo sweep. (10 reps)
    6. Live Berimbolo Attempt: In live rolling (75% resistance), attempt the Berimbolo from a suitable open guard position. Focus on setting up and executing one full Berimbolo attempt. (3 attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up significantly from your closed guard, creating a space to attack their legs.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your open guard, and you can use their forward momentum against them.
    • When you have established a strong De La Riva guard or Reverse De La Riva guard and can transition to the Berimbolo setup.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The 'Sit Out' Counter: If your opponent initiates the Berimbolo hip rotation, immediately sit up and drive your hips to the mat, creating a strong base. Extend your leg that is being hooked and push away to break the entanglement.
    • The 'Leg Kick Out' Counter: As your opponent's hooking leg comes over, quickly extend your leg and kick it out, disrupting their base and balance. Simultaneously, step your other leg forward to establish a strong base.
    • The 'Stall and Base' Counter: If you feel the Berimbolo sweep starting, immediately flatten your back to the mat and drive your weight into your opponent's hips. Use your arms to push their hips away and create space to stand up or reset.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the Berimbolo?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are forcing your body over your opponent's legs without generating sufficient hip rotation and leverage. This can cause your knee to twist laterally under load, potentially damaging ligaments like the ACL or MCL. Focus on driving your opponent forward to create space and rotating your hips like a 'door hinge' rather than trying to jump your hips over.

    Q: How can I do the Berimbolo against a much bigger opponent who doesn't move much?

    Against a larger, less mobile opponent, focus on using their weight against them. Establish a strong collar grip and pull them forward to break their posture. This forward momentum is key. Once they shift their weight, use aggressive hip rotation and drive your hooking leg deep to initiate the sweep. Don't rely on speed; use precise timing and leverage.

    Q: Is the Berimbolo only for attacking the back, or can I sweep with it?

    While the Berimbolo is primarily known for its back-taking potential, the initial mechanics are a powerful sweep. By rotating your hips and driving your opponent over your base leg, you can achieve a sweep and end up in a dominant top position, such as Side Control or mount, if they defend the back take effectively. The transition to back control is the most common outcome.
