Your First Berimbolo: A White Belt's Precise Guide

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Berimbolo is a dynamic guard retention and sweep technique that initiates from a Closed Guard or open guard position, aiming to transition to a back-take. White belts often struggle due to a lack of fundamental body mechanics and an impulse to force the movement. The core insight is using your hips to create space and leverage, effectively "flipping" yourself into position rather than "pulling" your opponent.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong closed guard with both feet hooking inside your opponent's upper thighs, knees at approximately 90-degree flexion. Maintain a tight posture, preventing your opponent from posturing up.
    2. Secure a "figure-four" grip on your opponent's left arm with your right arm (right hand grips opponent's wrist, left hand grips opponent's elbow). Simultaneously, hook your left foot behind your opponent's right hip crease, ensuring your heel is deep.
    3. With your left hand, establish a "collar grip" on the opponent's gi collar on the same side as the trapped arm (e.g., left hand on opponent's left collar).
    4. Initiate the "spin" by driving your hips towards your right, simultaneously pushing off your left foot which is hooked behind their hip. Your chest should move towards your opponent's left hip.
    5. As your hips drive right, extend your right leg (the one controlling their arm) to create a "frame" against their hip. This prevents them from following your rotation directly.
    6. Continue the hip rotation and drive your left shoulder towards the mat. Your right leg should now be extended across their body, acting as a "hook" to prevent them from turning into you.
    7. As you complete the rotation, your left foot will naturally swing around their hips, and your right foot will come off their arm to hook behind their knee or thigh, establishing a "back-hook" position.
    8. Transition to a "body triangle" or "seatbelt grip" on their back, maintaining control as you finish the sweep or secure the back mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to "pull" your opponent over by solely using arm strength, while they are postured upright, can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears as their weight is not properly supported.
    • "Kicking" your leg out aggressively to create space without hip rotation can strain your knee ligaments (ACL/MCL) as the torque is applied directly to the joint.
    • Spinning with a wide, uncontrolled base, allowing your opponent to "stack" you directly onto your head or neck, can cause cervical spine injury.
    • Failing to maintain a tight grip on the collar or sleeve and allowing your opponent to "frame" your face with their hands can lead to elbow hyperextension if they push off forcefully.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip rotation and leg swing motion without a partner. Focus on the smooth transition of your feet and hips. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner lying on their back, practice the grips and the initial hip drive. Your partner remains passive. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the hip engagement and leg movement. They do not actively defend. (15 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the initial leg entanglement but does not actively "pass" or resist the spin. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner actively defends the leg entanglement and attempts to resist the spin by maintaining their base. Focus on creating space with your hips. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the Berimbolo in a controlled rolling scenario. Focus on execution, not necessarily completion. Your partner will defend as they normally would. (3 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating a clear space to initiate.
    • When your opponent attempts to "stack" your closed guard, providing an opportunity to spin away.
    • When you have secured a strong grip on an arm and collar, creating a "control point" for the rotation.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stacking": If the Berimbolo attempt is slow, your opponent can drive their hips forward and "stack" you, putting pressure on your spine and head. The counter is to keep your hips moving and avoid allowing them to get directly on top.
    • "The Wall": If you fail to create enough hip space, your opponent can "frame" with their hands on your hips or thighs and prevent your rotation. The counter is to ensure your hips are driving away, not just spinning in place.
    • "The Scramble": If the Berimbolo is initiated poorly, your opponent can "scramble" to a better position, like side control or mount. The counter is to commit to the movement and transition smoothly to a dominant position once the spin is initiated.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Berimbolo?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are "kicking" your leg out to create space instead of driving your hips. This places rotational stress on your knee ligaments. To fix this, focus on initiating the spin with your hips and using your legs as levers once the hip rotation is underway. Ensure your foot is deeply hooked behind the opponent's hip crease before initiating the spin.

    Q: How do I Berimbolo against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Focus on isolating one arm with a strong figure-four grip and using your collar grip to control their posture. Drive your hips hard away from the arm you are controlling, creating space. Instead of trying to "pull" them over, use your body weight and momentum to "flip" yourself into position behind them. Make sure your hooks are deep.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Berimbolo from open guard?

    From open guard, the Berimbolo is best attempted when your opponent is actively trying to pass your guard and their weight is forward. Specifically, when they are driving their knee in for a knee-slice or trying to "stack" you. The key is to have your feet in a position to "climb" their body, similar to a De La Riva or Spider Guard setup, before initiating the spin. You need to have a solid grip on their leg and collar.
