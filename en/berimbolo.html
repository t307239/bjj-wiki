The Berimbolo: A Biomechanically Accurate Guide for BJJ White Belts

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Berimbolo is a dynamic guard transition originating primarily from open guard positions like De La Riva or Reverse De La Riva. It aims to quickly invert the body and secure a back-Mount position.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of body awareness and a tendency to force movements rather than flow. This leads to inefficient mechanics and increased injury risk.

The core insight is using your leg as a lever and your hips as a fulcrum to generate rotational momentum, rather than relying solely on upper body strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Establish a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left sleeve) with your left hand. Your opponent is in your guard, likely on their knees or attempting to pass.
    2. Leg Placement: Hook your right leg over the opponent's left hip, ensuring your shin is perpendicular to their torso. Your left leg remains in a De La Riva hook position on their right leg.
    3. Hip Angle: Angle your hips towards your left, creating a slight diagonal line from your shoulders to your right foot. This sets up the rotation.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your right hip, pushing off your left foot. Imagine driving your right hip towards the mat.
    5. Rotation Initiation: Use your collar and sleeve grips to pull your opponent's upper body towards your left. Simultaneously, extend your right leg to create space.
    6. Inversion: Drive your left knee towards your chest, tucking it in as you continue the hip rotation. Your right foot should now be sweeping across the opponent's back.
    7. Hooking the Back: As your body inverts, your right leg will naturally swing around to hook the opponent's far hip or waist. Your left leg remains controlling their near hip.
    8. Securing Position: Drive your hips forward and up, aiming to get your chest onto their back. Your right leg is now the primary hook securing your back control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Rotation: Attempting to force the Berimbolo by twisting the knee inward while the foot is trapped can lead to ACL or meniscus tears. The correct movement involves the hip and torso driving the rotation, not the knee joint itself.
    • Ankle Twisting: Overextending your ankle during the sweep, especially if your foot is caught under the opponent's weight, can result in a severe ankle sprain or fracture. Maintain a stable ankle and let your leg movement be driven by hip rotation.
    • Neck Strain: Grabbing the opponent's head or neck with an unstable base during the inversion can cause cervical strain or hyperextension. Focus on controlling the upper body with collar and sleeve grips, and maintain a strong base with your hips.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Rotation Drill: Lie on your back. Practice the hip rotation and body angling motion without any opponent or grips. Focus on feeling the movement through your core and hips. (50 reps)
    2. Solo Leg Swing Drill: From your back, practice hooking your leg over a pad or pillow, then swinging it around to simulate the back hook. Focus on the arc of the motion. (50 reps per leg)
    3. Partner Resistance Drill (0%): With a partner lying on their back, practice the initial leg hook and hip angling. They remain completely passive. Focus on grip acquisition and body positioning. (10 reps)
    4. Partner Resistance Drill (25%): Partner provides minimal, static resistance. Practice the full Berimbolo sequence, focusing on the timing of the hip drive and rotation. They offer no active defense. (10 reps)
    5. Partner Resistance Drill (50%): Partner offers light, passive resistance to the sweep and transition. Focus on maintaining control and completing the back take. They will not actively counter. (10 reps)
    6. Live Rolling (90%): Spar with a partner who understands you are drilling Berimbolo. They will attempt to defend, but will not actively hunt for submissions or extreme counters. Focus on smooth execution and maintaining control. (5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured up and reaching over your legs.
    • When the opponent attempts a knee-slice or pressure pass from your open guard.
    • When you have established a strong De La Riva or Reverse De La Riva hook and the opponent is leaning heavily on that side.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Standing Up/Pivoting: If the opponent feels the rotation, they can quickly stand and pivot their hips away. Your counter is to immediately disengage the Berimbolo attempt and re-establish a guard, or transition to a different sweep.
    • Stacking: The opponent can attempt to stack your hips forward, collapsing your base. Your counter is to use your grips to pull their weight back towards you, creating space to re-adjust your guard or attempt an alternative sweep.
    • Kneeling Base: If the opponent recognizes the Berimbolo and sinks their hips low to maintain a strong kneeling base, the Berimbolo becomes significantly harder. Your counter is to abandon the Berimbolo and transition to a different guard retention or sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Berimbolo?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you're trying to force the rotation with your knee joint itself, rather than using your hips and torso as the primary drivers. The Berimbolo is a rotational movement initiated by hip angling and weight transfer. Ensure your knee stays in a neutral position and the movement originates from your core and pelvis.

    Q: How can I do the Berimbolo against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and timing are paramount. Focus on securing deep collar and sleeve grips to control their posture and prevent them from easily shucking your legs. Use your hip rotation to create the initial separation and momentum, then drive your hips forward to get underneath their weight. Do not rely on brute strength; use their size against them by creating off-balancing angles.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Berimbolo in a match?

    The Berimbolo is most effective when the opponent is actively trying to pass your guard and is leaning forward or postured up, creating an opening. It's also a great option when they commit to a specific pass, like a knee-slice or toreando, as their weight distribution can be exploited for the transition. Avoid attempting it when the opponent is very low and heavy, or has already established a strong, static base.
