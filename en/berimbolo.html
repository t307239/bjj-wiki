The Berimbolo is a dynamic guard transition originating from closed or open guard, aiming to secure a back-Mount position.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of foundational body mechanics and a tendency to rush the movement.

Success hinges on understanding hip rotation and weight transfer to create leverage, not brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent's left leg is inside your closed guard. Secure a deep collar grip with your right hand (under their chin) and a sleeve grip on their left arm with your left hand.
    2. Maintain a closed guard posture, keeping your knees tight to your chest and your hips elevated slightly off the mat.
    3. Initiate a slight hip bump towards your left, creating a small space between your hips and the opponent's.
    4. As you bump, simultaneously release your left foot from their hip and place it on the mat behind their left knee, acting as a pivot point.
    5. Grip the opponent's left ankle with your right hand, ensuring a firm hold on the Achilles tendon area.
    6. Execute a powerful hip rotation to your right, driving your hips off the mat and tucking your left leg underneath the opponent's right leg.
    7. As your hips rotate, your left knee should sweep across the opponent's back, guiding them towards the mat while you maintain control of their ankle.
    8. Transition to a seatbelt grip on their back, securing hooks with your legs to achieve back-mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your leg to hook the opponent's leg: This can lead to a knee hyperextension injury (ACL/MCL tear) as the opponent's weight drives down. The correct action is to pivot on your foot and sweep the leg across.
    • Releasing grips prematurely or too late: This can cause shoulder dislocation or AC joint separation if you lose balance and fall awkwardly. Always maintain at least one strong connection.
    • Attempting to 'muscle' the Berimbolo without hip rotation: This can result in a strained lower back or hip flexor injury. Focus on using your core and hip power to generate the rotation.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the hip rotation and leg sweep motion without a partner, focusing on the timing and coordination of your hips and legs. 50 repetitions.
    2. Partner assisted drilling (25% resistance): Have a partner lie on their back. You practice the grip setup and the initial hip bump and leg hook. Partner provides minimal assistance. 20 repetitions per side.
    3. Controlled transition drilling (50% resistance): From closed guard, have your partner lightly resist the initial hip bump. You execute the Berimbolo motion, and your partner allows you to sweep their leg. Focus on smooth transitions. 15 repetitions per side.
    4. Half-speed simulated Berimbolo (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the Berimbolo by framing and posturing up. You still aim to complete the sweep, but the focus is on maintaining control and reacting to their defenses. 10 repetitions per side.
    5. Live Berimbolo attempts (90% resistance): In a rolling scenario, initiate the Berimbolo from closed guard. Your partner will actively defend. Focus on completing the transition or recognizing when to disengage safely. 5 repetitions.
    6. Full rolling (100% resistance): Attempt the Berimbolo during live rolling. Focus on applying the technique with correct biomechanics and adapting to your opponent's reactions. Aim for 2-3 successful transitions in a 5-minute round.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent postures up significantly from closed guard, creating space.
    • When the opponent attempts to stack pass, their weight shifts forward.
    • When the opponent's base is narrow and unstable, making them susceptible to a sweep.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posting the hook leg: As the attacker attempts to hook your leg, firmly post your foot on the mat and extend your leg to prevent it from being swept. This maintains your base and prevents the hip rotation.
    • Stacking and pressure: As the attacker initiates the hip bump, aggressively stack your weight onto them, driving your chest into their chest and head. This collapses their structure and makes the Berimbolo difficult to complete.
    • Re-guarding and framing: Immediately create frames with your arms against the attacker's hips and shoulders as they attempt to roll. Use your free leg to re-establish guard or create distance.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to Berimbolo?

    Shoulder pain often occurs because you are releasing your grips too early or not maintaining a strong connection to your opponent's body. When you attempt the hip rotation, if your grips are weak, your body can become disconnected, leading to a fall that strains your shoulder. Always ensure you have a secure grip on the ankle and either a collar or sleeve grip throughout the initial rotation.

    Q: How can I do the Berimbolo if my opponent is much bigger than me?

    For larger opponents, the Berimbolo relies even more heavily on precise timing and leverage rather than strength. Focus on creating a significant hip bump to generate space, and use your legs to aggressively sweep their base. Your goal is to disrupt their balance by getting underneath their center of gravity. A strong ankle grip is crucial for controlling their movement and guiding the sweep.

    Q: In open guard, a Berimbolo is best attempted when your opponent is pressing forward with weight and their hips are relatively close to yours, but not yet past your legs. Specifically, if they are trying to pass by driving forward with their chest low, you can use a leg drag grip and a strong hip bump to initiate the rotation. The key is to catch them in a forward-leaning posture before they can establish a solid base or frames.
