The Berimbolo: A Beginner's Guide to BJJ Guard Mobility

PURPLE★★★★☆Advanced
Purple Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Purple Belt Requirements

The Berimbolo is a dynamic guard transition originating from open guard, aiming to invert and secure a back-take or sweep opportunity. White belts often struggle due to a lack of body awareness and a tendency to force movements instead of flowing.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to initiate rotation, creating space for your legs to follow, rather than pushing your opponent away.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a De La Riva hook with your right leg, placing your foot on the outside of your opponent's left thigh, maintaining knee-to-chest distance. Your left leg is free, knee pointing towards the ceiling.
    2. Secure a collar grip (e.g., left hand on opponent's right collar) and a sleeve grip (e.g., right hand on opponent's left sleeve). Maintain a frame with your forearms to keep your opponent's weight slightly off you.
    3. Initiate hip elevation by driving your left foot into the mat, lifting your hips off the ground, and rotating your pelvis towards your right side.
    4. As your hips rise, bring your free left leg (the one not hooked) underneath your opponent's body, aiming to place your left knee on the mat behind their hips.
    5. Simultaneously, extend your right leg (the De La Riva hook) upwards and away from your opponent's body, creating a lever.
    6. Continue the hip rotation and drive your left knee forward, initiating a forward roll motion. Your right foot will naturally swing around your opponent's hips.
    7. As your body rotates, your right foot will land on the mat on the opposite side of your opponent, securing a position behind them, often leading to a back-take or transitioning to a leg entanglement.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly driving your head into the mat while attempting to invert: This can cause cervical spine hyperextension or compression, leading to neck pain or serious injury.
    • Overextending your knee joint when swinging your hooking leg out: This can sprain or tear the ACL or MCL, particularly if your opponent is resisting and creating awkward angles.
    • Trying to 'muscle' the Berimbolo by pushing your opponent away with your hands instead of using hip rotation: This reduces the leverage of the technique and can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strains.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Rotations: Practice lifting your hips and rotating them from a supine position, focusing on the motion of bringing one knee behind your hips. 50 reps.
    2. De La Riva Hook & Hip Lift Drill: From a partner's guard (no resistance), practice establishing the De La Riva hook and then lifting your hips and bringing your free knee behind their hips. 20 reps per side.
    3. Grounded Berimbolo Rotation: With a partner lying on their back, practice the full Berimbolo rotation motion, focusing on the hip drive and leg movement, stopping before transitioning to a sweep. 30 reps per side, 25% resistance.
    4. Berimbolo to Leg Entanglement: With a partner providing light resistance, practice the Berimbolo to transition into a basic leg entanglement (e.g., single leg X). 20 reps per side, 50% resistance.
    5. Berimbolo to Back Take Attempt: With a partner providing moderate resistance, practice the Berimbolo with the intention of securing a back take. Focus on the initial rotation and hip control. 15 reps per side, 75% resistance.
    6. Live Rolling Berimbolo Attempts: In a controlled rolling scenario, attempt the Berimbolo when the opportunity arises, focusing on executing the mechanics correctly and adapting to your opponent's reaction. 5 minutes, 90% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is postured up and has their weight forward, creating an opening for you to invert.
    • When you have established a strong De La Riva hook and can control your opponent's balance.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard, and you can use their forward momentum against them.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape and Re-establish Base: As the Berimbolo begins, the defender can hip escape to the side of the initiating leg, creating distance and preventing the rotation. This requires a quick weight shift to the opposite hip.
    • Stacking and Weight Distribution: The defender can drive their weight forward and down, stacking their opponent and collapsing the space needed for the Berimbolo rotation. This involves a forward lean and keeping the base wide.
    • Knee-Shield and Frame: The defender can use their free leg to create a strong knee shield, pushing against the initiating hip, and simultaneously frame with their hands on the opponent's shoulders or head to prevent the inversion.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the Berimbolo?

    Knee pain during the Berimbolo often stems from the hooking leg's knee joint being forced into an unnatural position due to a lack of hip mobility or incorrect rotation. Ensure your hip initiates the movement, allowing your leg to follow naturally, rather than forcing the leg out and twisting the knee. Focus on rotating your pelvis first, then let your leg follow.

    Q: How can I do the Berimbolo against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, the Berimbolo relies even more on precise timing and leverage than brute strength. Focus on establishing a deep De La Riva hook and using your hips to create a small, sharp explosive movement rather than trying to push their weight. Your goal is to get *under* their weight and behind their hips, not to move their entire mass directly.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Berimbolo in a BJJ match?

    The Berimbolo is best attempted when your opponent is postured up, creating a natural opening for you to invert, or when they are actively trying to pass your guard. Look for moments when their weight is forward, making it easier to lift your hips and initiate the rotation. Avoid attempting it when they are flat on their back or have a very strong, stable base.
