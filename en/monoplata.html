Monoplata: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Monoplata is a joint lock applied from guard, targeting the opponent's shoulder by controlling their arm with your legs. White belts often struggle by overextending or losing the arm's position, leading to escapes or ineffective attempts. The key is isolating the opponent's arm between your legs and using your hips to create the rotational pressure for the submission.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start from closed guard. Secure a grip on the opponent's far wrist with your near hand (e.g., right hand grips opponent's left wrist).
    2. Use your near leg (e.g., right leg) to hook behind the opponent's far shoulder blade, ensuring your knee is pointed towards the ceiling.
    3. Swing your far leg (e.g., left leg) over the opponent's head, aiming to place your shin across their upper back, perpendicular to their spine.
    4. Adjust your hips to be perpendicular to the opponent's spine, creating a strong base and preventing them from posturing up.
    5. Trap the opponent's arm by bringing your hooking leg (right leg) to connect with your shin leg (left leg), creating a figure-four grip with your legs around their arm and upper torso.
    6. Secure your own shin with your far hand (left hand grips opponent's left shin), creating a tight lock on their arm.
    7. Drive your hips forward and slightly upwards, rotating your torso towards the opponent's trapped arm to apply pressure to the shoulder joint.
    8. Maintain control of the opponent's wrist with your near hand to prevent them from posturing or bailing out of the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly swinging your leg over the opponent's head without controlling their wrist can allow them to posture up and escape, potentially causing hyperextension of your own knee if you twist improperly.
    • Failing to establish a strong hip angle (perpendicular to the opponent) allows them to stack you, risking spinal compression and shoulder impingement.
    • Trying to 'muscle' the submission without proper leg control can lead to the opponent's arm slipping out, potentially causing a knee-on-belly transition for them or a failed submission attempt for you.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg over and figure-four lock without a partner. Focus on hip movement and shin placement. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): Partner lies on their back. Practice establishing the initial wrist grip and swinging your leg over. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to the leg over and helps you establish the figure-four. Focus on maintaining control. (20 reps per side)
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner attempts to block your leg over and resist the figure-four. Focus on adjusting your hips and maintaining the lock. (15 reps per side)
    5. Partner drilling (75% resistance): Partner actively defends by posturing and trying to pull their arm free. Focus on hip pressure and wrist control. (10 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the Monoplata from closed guard during sparring, focusing on correct mechanics and timing. (5 attempts per roll)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent establishes a strong closed guard and you are looking to attack from the bottom.
    • When the opponent attempts a guard pass and you can secure their arm during the transition.
    • When you have achieved a strong spider guard or lasso guard position and can isolate an arm.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Pass: If the opponent fails to establish a strong hip angle, they are vulnerable to being stacked. Drive your weight forward and down, pushing their hips away with your legs.
    • Arm Pull Out: If the opponent's leg lock on your arm is loose, you can often pull your arm free by stepping your hips back and rotating your shoulder.
    • Roll Through: If the opponent commits to the submission but doesn't finish, you can sometimes roll through to escape the position and regain guard or achieve a better one.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Monoplata?

    Wrist pain usually occurs if you are gripping the opponent's wrist too tightly with your fingers extended, creating a hyperextension. Instead, aim for a "cupped" grip with your thumb wrapped around their wrist, and use your forearm and bicep to control their arm's rotation. Ensure your own wrist is in a neutral position, not bent.

    Q: How do I Monoplata against a much bigger opponent who is very strong?

    Against a larger opponent, perfect hip positioning and leg structure are paraMount. Do not rely on strength. Focus on getting your shin across their upper back and connecting your legs to create a tight figure-four. Use your hips to drive forward, creating leverage and isolating their shoulder. If they are too strong to finish, transition to a sweep or another submission.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from a failed Monoplata attempt?

    If the opponent escapes your leg lock before you apply pressure, immediately focus on regaining your guard or transitioning to a sweep. Do not stay stuck in the failed submission attempt. For example, if they pull their arm out, use that moment to shrimp your hips out and re-establish Closed Guard or transition to an X-guard.
