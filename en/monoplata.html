The Monoplata is a shoulder joint lock initiated from a guard position, typically Closed Guard, aiming to isolate and attack the opponent's arm. It transitions into a powerful submission that controls the opponent's posture and base.

White belts often struggle with the Monoplata due to an incomplete understanding of hip mechanics and leverage, leading to ineffective attempts or injury. They focus too much on brute strength rather than precise body positioning.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to create a fulcrum, driving your shin across their shoulder and controlling their scapula to generate rotational torque on the shoulder joint.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an overhook with your right arm around the opponent's left arm, trapping their elbow near your chest. Your left hand grips their left wrist, palm facing you.
    2. Simultaneously, drive your hips upwards and to your left, creating a slight angle. Your left knee should be driving into their left hip.
    3. Begin to untuck your right leg, swinging it over their left shoulder, aiming to place your shin across the back of their shoulder blade. Your right foot should be flexed, heel pointing upwards.
    4. As your right shin crosses their shoulder, drive your hips forward and slightly to the right, bringing your opponent's arm across your chest. Your left hand maintains a firm grip on their wrist.
    5. Extend your left leg, pushing their trapped arm towards your right hip. Your right leg remains firmly planted across their shoulder, acting as a pivot point.
    6. Rotate your hips to the left, simultaneously pulling their wrist towards your right hip while pushing their elbow away with your body. This creates the rotational pressure on the shoulder.
    7. Maintain a strong grip on the wrist and keep your right shin locked on their shoulder. Your hips should be tight and angled, preventing them from posturing up or escaping.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly rotating your hips: This can cause a hyperextension or tear of the opponent's shoulder capsule or rotator cuff. Maintain a controlled hip rotation, focusing on generating leverage, not force.
    • Letting their arm escape the shoulder cradle: If their arm slides off your shoulder, you risk a direct armbar attempt from them, or you might twist your own knee. Ensure your shin is firmly across their shoulder blade throughout the movement.
    • Overextending your own back: Trying to force the submission by arching your back excessively can lead to lumbar strain. Keep your core engaged and drive the submission through hip extension and rotation.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement, leg swing, and shin placement without a partner. Focus on smooth transitions and body positioning. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner lies flat on their back. Execute the full Monoplata sequence, focusing on precise grips and body angles. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the leverage. Focus on maintaining control of the arm and wrist. (5 reps per side)
    4. Guard retention with Monoplata setup: Start from closed guard. Your partner attempts to pass. Set up the Monoplata entry and hold the position. (3 minutes, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Monoplata during rolling, but stop the submission if your partner taps or if the position is lost. Focus on smooth transitions and recognizing opportunities. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full): Execute the Monoplata as you would in a competition, aiming for the submission. Be prepared to transition if the submission is defended. (5 minutes, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent has their arm extended towards you from closed guard, creating an opening.
    • When the opponent is attempting to posture up from your guard, leaving their arm vulnerable.
    • After successfully sweeping and landing in a position where you can isolate an arm across your body.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Shoulder Roll Defense: The opponent can roll their shoulders forward, tucking their chin and bringing their trapped shoulder towards their hips. This dissipates the pressure and allows them to escape.
    • Stacking Defense: The opponent can stack their weight onto you, driving their hips into your chest and attempting to flatten you out. This alleviates pressure on the shoulder and can lead to a scramble.
    • Leg Escape: The opponent can attempt to pull their trapped arm out by stepping over your hips with their free leg, creating space to disengage and reset.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do Monoplata?

    Your wrist pain is likely due to gripping their wrist too tightly with an extended or bent wrist, or failing to maintain a neutral wrist position. Ensure your grip is firm but not rigid, and keep your wrist neutral, aligned with your forearm. The pressure should be on their shoulder, not your wrist.

    Q: How can I use Monoplata against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and hip mechanics are even more critical. Focus on driving your hips forward and creating a tight angle, using your shin as a fulcrum on their shoulder. Don't try to muscle them; instead, use their weight against them by controlling their posture and rotating their arm.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to a Monoplata from closed guard?

    The optimal time to transition to a Monoplata from closed guard is when the opponent extends their arm towards you, often when they are trying to break your guard or posture up. Look for an opportunity when their elbow is bent and their arm is across your body, creating a clear entry point for your shin.
