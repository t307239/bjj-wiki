Monoplata Technique for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Monoplata is a joint lock initiated from guard, targeting the opponent's shoulder. It offers a powerful submission opportunity if executed correctly.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise body control and an over-reliance on brute force. This leads to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key insight is using your leg as a fulcrum to control the opponent's posture and create the angle for the shoulder lock, not just trapping their arm.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on the opponent's left arm with your left hand.
    2. Initiate a slight hip escape to your left, creating space to bring your left leg up and over the opponent's left shoulder.
    3. As your left leg clears the shoulder, extend your hips forward, driving your left knee towards the opponent's left hip bone, while simultaneously lowering your right leg to the mat.
    4. Secure your left shin across the opponent's upper back/neck area, ensuring your left foot is anchored near their right hip.
    5. Grip your own left shin with your right hand, creating a figure-four lock around the opponent's head and shoulder.
    6. Tilt your hips upwards and slightly to your left, creating a bowing motion with your back, to apply pressure on the opponent's shoulder joint.
    7. Keep your left elbow tucked to your ribs to prevent the opponent from posturing up, and use your right hand to guide the opponent's head away from the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your hips forward without controlling posture can lead to your opponent stacking you; this can compress your spine, risking disc injury, and allows them to pass your guard. Instead, maintain a tight connection with your left shin across their back and use your hips to drive *into* them.
    • Over-rotating your body when attempting to finish can strain your own shoulder or elbow joints; the force should be generated by hip extension and tilting, not twisting. Keep your core engaged and focus on lifting your hips smoothly.
    • Allowing the opponent to turn into you while your leg is trapped can lead to a knee-bar attempt on your own leg or allow them to escape the position; maintain a strong base and keep your hips tight to their body.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip escape, leg over the shoulder, and figure-four grip motion without a partner (10 reps per side).
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from closed guard, focusing on correct grip placement and body positioning (5 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to the leg-over-shoulder and hip-control phase (10 reps per side).
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner attempts to defend by posturing up or turning, while you focus on maintaining control and hip pressure (10 reps per side).
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to pass or escape, simulating a live roll scenario, but stopping before submission (15 reps per side).
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the Monoplata during sparring, focusing on execution and recognizing opportunities, without forcing the submission if the position is lost (5 minutes, 3 attempts total).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent extends their arm and attempts to pass your guard by reaching across.
    • Opponent is postured high in your guard, creating an opportunity to trap an arm.
    • You have established a strong closed guard and are looking to initiate a submission.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • THE STACK: If the opponent stacks you, immediately drive your hips forward and slightly upwards, using your left shin as leverage to create space and potentially transition to an armbar or sweep. This prevents spinal compression.
    • THE ESCAPE (Opponent turns in): If the opponent turns into you, release your figure-four grip and use your left leg to push their hip away, while your right hand posts on the mat to shrimp out and re-establish guard or transition to a sweep.
    • THE SWEEP (Opponent bails): If the opponent bails on the submission and tries to stand up, use your left leg to hook their hip and your right hand to control their arm, then drive your hips forward to execute a sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try the Monoplata as a white belt?

    Your shoulder is likely hurting because you are using your own body weight to force the lock, rather than the biomechanical leverage of the position. When you trap their arm with your leg and grip your shin, the pressure comes from your hips tilting upwards and driving forward, creating a rotational force on their shoulder. Ensure your hips are angled correctly and you are not twisting your own joint.

    Q: How can I do the Monoplata against a much bigger opponent with BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is precise control and timing, not strength. Focus on getting your leg *over* their shoulder and securing your shin *tightly* to their back before they can use their weight to crush you. Use your hips to create the angle and drive them off-balance. Once the figure-four grip is established, use your body weight and hip tilt to generate the submission, rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Monoplata from closed guard?

    The optimal time is when the opponent is reaching into your guard, often trying to break your posture or pass your legs. If they extend their arm and leave it within reach, you can quickly trap it with your leg. It’s also effective when they posture up high, giving you the vertical space to bring your leg over their shoulder. Avoid attempting it when they are already low and pressing their weight into you, as this makes it harder to secure the initial trap.
