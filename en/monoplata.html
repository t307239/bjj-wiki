Monoplata Technique for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Monoplata is a shoulder lock initiated from a closed guard or open guard position, aiming to isolate and attack the opponent's arm. It transitions into a shoulder crank, not a true Armbar.

White belts often struggle by focusing on pulling rather than positional control and leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. They may overextend or allow their hips to become exposed.

The core mechanical insight is using your own body weight and hip position to create a fulcrum, forcing the opponent's shoulder into a vulnerable position by controlling their arm with your leg.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (same side) with your left hand.
    2. Break the opponent's posture by pulling them forward, creating space to insert your left leg between their torso and right arm.
    3. Hook your left shin across the opponent's upper back, just below the neck, with your left foot pointing towards their right hip.
    4. Swing your right leg over the opponent's head, securing a "S" grip with your right hand on your left shin, creating a figure-four lock around their neck and shoulder.
    5. With your left knee driving forward into their shoulder blade and your right leg anchored, begin to tilt your hips to the left, aligning your spine perpendicular to the opponent's torso.
    6. Drive your left knee into their shoulder blade while simultaneously lifting your hips. This leverages their arm against their own neck.
    7. To finish, slightly extend your hips and apply upward pressure with your right shin into their shoulder socket, while keeping your back straight and core engaged.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly attempting to pull the opponent's arm across your body: This can hyperextend the opponent's elbow or shoulder, risking ligament tears in the elbow (ulnar collateral ligament) or shoulder (rotator cuff). The correct action is to use your leg as a cradle and your hips to create the leverage.
    • Allowing the opponent to stack you directly: If your hips are not angled and your base is too wide, the opponent can simply drive their weight forward, potentially injuring your spine or neck. Maintain a narrow base and ensure your hips are driving upwards, not just backwards.
    • Releasing the leg hook prematurely: If your left shin slips from the opponent's upper back, you lose the fulcrum. This can lead to a sudden release of pressure, potentially causing your knee to buckle or twist, risking ACL or meniscus tears. Ensure a secure hook before transitioning your hips.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of leg hook and hip angle: Practice inserting the left leg hook, swinging the right leg over, and achieving the figure-four grip, focusing on hip tilt. 50 reps, 0% resistance.
    2. Partner drill - static position: With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from closed guard to the Monoplata finishing position, focusing on correct body mechanics and grips. 10 reps per side, 10% resistance.
    3. Partner drill - controlled movement: Partner offers minimal resistance to posture breaks and arm positioning. Focus on smooth transitions and maintaining control. 10 reps per side, 25% resistance.
    4. Partner drill - basic defense: Partner attempts to posture up or pull their arm out. You practice maintaining the Monoplata position and adjusting your hips. 10 reps per side, 50% resistance.
    5. Live rolling - positional sparring: Start from closed guard and attempt the Monoplata. Partner defends actively but without trying to injure. Focus on applying the technique under light pressure. 5 minutes, 75% resistance.
    6. Live rolling - full sparring: Attempt the Monoplata during regular sparring. Focus on timing, transitions, and recognizing opportunities. 5 minutes, 90-100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent postures up and creates space at the hips.
    • When opponent tries to pass your closed guard by stacking you.
    • When opponent establishes a cross-collar grip and pulls you forward.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass Defense: If the opponent attempts to stack you, immediately drive your hips upward and to the side, angling your body to face them. This prevents them from driving weight directly onto your spine and allows you to potentially transition to a sweep or escape.
    • Arm Pull Out: If the opponent tries to pull their arm out, maintain a tight figure-four grip and use your hips to drive forward and to the side. This keeps their shoulder trapped and prevents them from straightening their arm.
    • Sweeping to Mount: If the opponent defends by posturing up and creating space, use the leverage of the Monoplata to sweep them over your hips. As you drive your hips up, shift your weight to your left side and use your right leg to push their hip away, transitioning to mount.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try the Monoplata as a white belt?

    This often happens when you're not using your hips correctly to create the leverage. Instead of pulling the opponent's arm, you should be driving your own hip into their shoulder blade and rotating your torso. This action isolates their shoulder joint. If you feel pain, you are likely forcing the movement with your arms or overextending your own shoulder, rather than using the biomechanical advantage of your hips and legs to trap their arm.

    Q: How do I finish the Monoplata against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on perfect hip positioning and leverage, not brute strength. Ensure your left leg hooks their upper back securely, and your right leg creates a strong figure-four around their neck and shoulder. Drive your hips upwards and slightly forward, using your body weight to create the angle. The key is to make their own shoulder the fulcrum; your hips lift and rotate to apply pressure, not your arms pulling.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Monoplata from closed guard?

    The most opportune moment is when the opponent attempts to posture up or create significant space between your hips and their torso. This usually occurs when they are trying to break your guard or establish a strong pass. By breaking their posture and securing the leg hook, you can then use their upward movement against them to initiate the Monoplata sequence, trapping their arm as they try to create distance.
