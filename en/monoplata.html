The Monoplata is a leg-based shoulder lock typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to isolate and attack the opponent's arm. White belts often struggle due to incomplete understanding of leverage and hip positioning.

The key mechanical insight is using your hip and leg as a fulcrum to break the opponent's posture and create the necessary angle for the lock.

Mastering this requires precise control of your hips and the opponent's arm, not brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a grip on the opponent's far wrist with your near hand (e.g., left hand grips opponent's right wrist).
    2. Simultaneously, use your other leg (e.g., right leg) to hook behind the opponent's shoulder and neck, controlling their posture.
    3. Drive your hips forward and slightly to the side, creating a perpendicular angle to the opponent's torso.
    4. Swing your other leg (e.g., left leg) up and over the opponent's shoulder, securing it above their bicep and below their neck.
    5. Your left shin should be pressed against the opponent's upper back, and your left foot should be tucked behind your own right knee.
    6. Grip your own left shin with your right hand, creating a tight frame around the opponent's upper arm.
    7. Keeping your hips elevated and driving forward, use your legs to pinch your knees together and your right hand to pull your left shin, creating a squeezing pressure on the shoulder joint.
    8. Maintain hip elevation and a tight frame to prevent the opponent from posturing up or bridging out.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to crank the arm by simply pulling your own leg with excessive force can damage the opponent's shoulder capsule and rotator cuff ligaments. The correct action is to drive hips forward and pinch knees together to create leverage.
    • Failing to maintain hip elevation and allowing the opponent to flatten you out can lead to knee hyperextension or ligament tears in your own knee. Always keep your hips high and engaged.
    • Not securing the opponent's far wrist properly can allow them to escape their arm and potentially transition to a sweep or pass, leaving your base compromised. Maintain a firm wrist grip throughout the setup.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and leg swing motion without a partner. Focus on the smooth transition of your legs and hip elevation. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice setting up the Monoplata from closed guard. Focus on achieving the correct leg and arm positions. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the initial hip and leg control. Focus on isolating the arm. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner begins to slightly resist the leg placement and hip movement. Focus on maintaining your frame and controlling their posture. (5 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to posture up or defend the arm. Focus on driving hips forward and maintaining the shoulder lock. (3 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% intensity): Apply the Monoplata in a controlled rolling scenario. Focus on recognizing opportunities and executing the technique under pressure. (3 attempts per roll)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is posturing up from closed guard, creating space between your bodies.
    • Opponent is attempting to pass your guard and their arm extends beyond your hip line.
    • You have secured a strong control of the opponent's arm from guard, and they are trying to pull it out.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent bridges and rolls: As the opponent bridges, drive your hips into them and use your legs to maintain the lock, preventing them from completing the roll. Keep your frame tight.
    • Opponent steps over your head: If the opponent attempts to step over your head to escape, quickly bring your free leg up to block their leg and maintain your hip control. You may need to transition to a triangle or armbar.
    • Opponent flattens you out: If the opponent flattens you, immediately drive your hips up and forward to re-establish the angle. Use your free leg to create space if needed, and re-grip your shin to secure the lock.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to finish the Monoplata?

    Your wrist pain likely stems from gripping your own shin too tightly and trying to pull yourself into the lock. The Monoplata's power comes from hip drive and leg pinch, not forearm strength. Instead of pulling, focus on driving your hips forward and squeezing your knees together while maintaining a relaxed grip on your shin. This creates leverage at the shoulder joint, not just the wrist.

    Q: How can I Monoplata against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Ensure your initial grip on their far wrist is secure to prevent them from using their size to simply power out. Focus on the hip angle; get your hips perpendicular to their torso to create a strong fulcrum. Drive your hips forward aggressively and pinch your knees tightly. This biomechanical advantage allows you to isolate their arm effectively, regardless of their size.

    Q: When should I transition if the Monoplata isn't working?

    If the opponent defends by posturing up or isolating your leg, don't force the Monoplata. Instead, use the momentum to transition to a triangle choke by bringing your free leg over their head. Alternatively, if they defend by stacking, you can use the pressure to set up an Armbar by extending your hips and rotating. The key is to recognize the defensive movement and flow into a secondary attack.
