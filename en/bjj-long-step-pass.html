Long Step Pass Guide: Footwork & Positioning

Contents

    Core Concept

    Rather than using pressure to compress the guard, the long step pass uses footwork and hip positioning to create angles and distance from the opponent's guard attacks. It requires more technical execution than pressure-based passes but provides more options once established.

    Initiating Long Step

    1. Start in passing position with stance control
    2. Break posture and control one of opponent's legs or hips
    3. Take a wide step to the side, moving your hips away from center
    4. As you step, your body angle changes relative to the opponent
    5. Complete the step by driving your hips into side control

    Step Mechanics

    The step is the critical technical element. It must be wide and controlled — not reckless. As you step, you're creating an angle where the opponent cannot establish hooks or leverage for sweeps. The step should feel like you're moving your whole body sideways, not just your foot.

    Controlling Distance

    Throughout the pass, maintain distance from the opponent's guard attacks. Keep your hips slightly back and your posture upright. This makes it harder for the opponent to catch your leg for foot lock or sweep opportunities. Many leg lock entries happen from long step failures where the passer gets too close.

    Common Finishes

    Side Control

    The most common finish. Complete your step and drive your hip into side control.

    Knee on Belly

    From the passing line, transition to knee on belly if the opponent turns away from you.

    ⚡ Pro Tip: The long step pass is particularly effective against experienced guard players who expect pressure passes. The footwork-based approach catches them off-guard because they're expecting compression, not angular movement.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do I feel like I'm losing my balance when I try to initiate the Long Step Pass and my opponent pushes back?

    When your opponent pushes back, you're likely not maintaining a strong base. Ensure your hips are lower than your opponent's hips, and actively drive your weight through your lead leg's heel, keeping your supporting knee bent and grounded to prevent your base from rising and becoming unstable.

    Q: How can I effectively keep my opponent from shrimping away when I'm setting up the Long Step Pass and my foot is near their hip?

    To prevent shrimping, use your passing leg's knee to actively pressure and control the space near their hip, preventing them from creating distance. Simultaneously, your opposite hand should grip their far leg's ankle or pant cuff, creating a strong anchor that limits their hip mobility.

    Q: What's the correct way to transition my hips after I've stepped over my opponent's legs during the Long Step Pass to avoid getting swept?

    Immediately after stepping over, drive your hips down and forward, creating a heavy pressure that pins their legs. Your chest should be in contact with their chest, and your lead leg's knee should be driving into their hip to prevent them from hip escaping or bridging.
