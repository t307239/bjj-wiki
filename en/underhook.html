The Underhook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The underhook is a fundamental positional control achieved by placing your arm underneath your opponent's arm, securing it against their torso. It is crucial for controlling distance, preventing passes, and setting up sweeps and submissions.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength instead of leverage, leading to ineffective grips and wasted energy.

The key is to get your bicep and forearm across their chest, creating a strong frame that limits their movement and allows you to dictate the engagement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a neutral standing position or guard, approach your opponent.
    2. Step your lead foot slightly forward and to the side of their lead foot, creating a 45-degree angle between your bodies.
    3. Lower your hips by bending your knees, bringing your center of gravity below theirs.
    4. Simultaneously, reach your arm (the one on their lead side) across their torso, aiming to get your bicep and forearm flush against their chest.
    5. Your opponent's arm should be trapped between your torso and your attacking arm.
    6. Secure the underhook by cupping their shoulder or upper back with your hand, OR by gripping your own bicep.
    7. Keep your shoulder tight to their chest and your hips angled away, preventing them from squaring up.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to force an underhook by reaching across with a straight arm and pushing their shoulder: This strains the rotator cuff and shoulder joint as the opponent can easily resist and create a lever to injure your shoulder.
    • The correct action is to bend your elbow and drive your bicep into their chest, creating a strong, tight frame.
    • Attempting to secure an underhook by leaning back and pulling their arm towards you: This compromises your base, allowing them to easily take you down and can strain your lower back due to hyperextension.
    • The correct action is to keep your hips forward and drive your chest into them, using your legs for base and power.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip Practice: On your own, practice the arm motion and hip angle for securing an underhook against an imaginary opponent. (50 reps)
    2. Partner Static Hold: With a partner who is standing passively, practice stepping in and securing the underhook, holding it for 5 seconds. (20 reps per side)
    3. Underhook Entry Drill: Partner A attempts to secure an underhook while Partner B resists passively. Partner A resets after each successful underhook. (30 reps per side)
    4. Underhook Control (0% Resistance): From a neutral position, Partner A attempts to secure an underhook. Once secured, Partner A maintains control for 10 seconds without attempting to move. Partner B offers no resistance. (15 reps)
    5. Underhook Control (25% Resistance): As above, but Partner B offers light, passive resistance, trying to disengage their arm. (15 reps)
    6. Underhook Control (50% Resistance): Partner A attempts to secure an underhook. Partner B actively tries to break the underhook or create space. Partner A focuses solely on maintaining the underhook for 15 seconds. (10 reps)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is attempting a takedown, and you need to establish a strong defensive frame.
    • When you are in your opponent's guard and want to break their posture and control their hips.
    • When attempting to transition from open guard to a more dominant position like side control or mount.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an underhook?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are reaching with a straight arm and trying to force the position, which strains the rotator cuff. Instead, bend your elbow, drive your bicep into their chest, and use your hips to create leverage. This keeps the joint in a stronger, more protected position.

    Q: How do I get an underhook against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on timing and leverage rather than strength. Use a slight Hip Escape to create a momentary angle, then drive your hips forward and down as you reach for the underhook, getting your bicep deep on their chest. Keep your base wide and your knees bent to absorb their weight.

    Q: You should prioritize an underhook when your opponent is pressuring forward, attempting a takedown, or when you are trying to control their hips to prevent them from passing your guard. It provides superior control over their torso and is a critical component for establishing dominant positions.
