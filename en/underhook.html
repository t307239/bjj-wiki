The underhook is a fundamental control position, most commonly established from a defensive stance when your opponent attempts a Guard Pass or from within various guards. It allows you to control your opponent's posture and create leverage for sweeps or transitions.

White belts often struggle with the underhook because they rely on brute strength instead of precise body mechanics, leading to poor positioning and wasted energy. They might also overextend, exposing themselves to submissions.

The key mechanical insight is to drive your shoulder into your opponent's armpit while simultaneously connecting your forearm to their upper back or shoulder blade, creating a powerful "frame" that breaks their posture and allows you to dictate the engagement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a stable base with knees slightly bent, feet hip-width apart. Your hips should be low, close to the mat.
    2. Initiate the movement by stepping your lead leg slightly forward, creating a staggered stance.
    3. Drive your lead shoulder forward and down, aiming your acromion process (the bony point of your shoulder) into your opponent's armpit.
    4. Simultaneously, extend your lead arm, palm facing inward, and wrap your forearm across your opponent's upper back, just below their scapula.
    5. Secure a "collar grip" with your free hand on your opponent's opposite collar, or a "sleeve grip" on their same-side sleeve.
    6. Drive your hips forward and slightly upward, creating a wedge with your shoulder and arm, breaking their posture by collapsing their upper body towards you.
    7. Maintain a strong frame by keeping your elbow bent at approximately 90 degrees, preventing them from posturing up or creating space.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Error: Reaching with a straight arm to secure the underhook. Failure: This leaves your elbow vulnerable to hyperextension, potentially tearing the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) of the elbow. Correct: Keep a slight bend in your elbow as you drive it into the armpit, ensuring your forearm connects to their back.
    • Error: Arching your back excessively to create the underhook. Failure: This strains the lumbar spine and can lead to lower back pain or disc injury. It also compromises your base. Correct: Drive your hips forward and maintain a neutral spine, engaging your core.
    • Error: Letting your head fall to the side or forward while establishing the underhook. Failure: This exposes your neck to chokes like the guillotine or arm-triangle. Correct: Keep your head up, looking forward, and maintain a strong, stable neck position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Shadow Drilling (0% Resistance): Practice the underhook entry and frame extension 20 times per side. Focus on the hip and shoulder drive.
    2. Partner Static Entry (25% Resistance): With a partner kneeling or in a stable base, practice entering the underhook and establishing the frame. Partner offers minimal resistance. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partner Posture Control Drill (50% Resistance): Partner tries to posture up slightly. You focus on maintaining the underhook frame and preventing them from straightening their back. 15 reps per side.
    4. Guard Retention with Underhook (75% Resistance): From closed guard, practice creating space and achieving the underhook as partner attempts to break your guard. Focus on maintaining the frame. 10 reps per side.
    5. Guard Pass Defense with Underhook (90% Resistance): As partner attempts a common guard pass (e.g., knee-slice), focus on establishing the underhook to disrupt their pass. 5 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (100% Resistance): Integrate the underhook into your rolling rounds, focusing on achieving and maintaining it for control and sweeps. Aim for 5 minutes of focused underhook attempts.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass from standing or on the ground, creating an opening.
    • When you are in your opponent's guard and they are posturing up, allowing you to "pull them down" into your frame.
    • When you are in a scrambled position and need to establish immediate control and prevent them from advancing past you.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Head and Arm" Defense: If your opponent secures an underhook, immediately drive your head to the same side as their underhook, connecting your forehead to their shoulder. Simultaneously, place your opposite arm across their chest, creating a strong "frame" to prevent them from driving their shoulder into your armpit and breaking your posture.
    • The "Elbow Escape" (from bottom half-guard): If your opponent has an underhook and you are in half-guard, drive your hips away from their underhook arm. Bring your bottom leg up and plant your foot on their hip. This creates space to shrimp your hips out and regain a more neutral position, allowing you to fight for your own underhook or escape.
    • The "Switch" (from bottom guard): If your opponent secures an underhook from your guard, use your legs to create slight distance and immediately switch your hips to the opposite side. Use your arms to "frame" against their chest and shoulder, preventing them from advancing their underhook. This can set up sweeps like the hip bump sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an underhook?

    Shoulder pain often occurs from reaching with a straight arm and "jamming" your shoulder directly into your opponent's armpit without proper hip and body alignment. Instead of just reaching, think about driving your entire shoulder girdle forward and down, using your hips to generate power. Ensure your forearm connects to their back, creating a strong frame and distributing the force, rather than solely relying on your shoulder joint.

    Q: How can I get an underhook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, brute force won't work. Focus on timing and technique. Get low, use your legs to create angles, and drive your shoulder into their armpit as they attempt to advance. Think about "climbing" your arm up their back. Use your hips to create leverage, allowing you to break their posture and control their weight distribution, rather than trying to muscle them.

    Q: The underhook is best used defensively when your opponent is trying to pass your guard or offensively to break their posture and set up attacks from dominant positions like side control or mount. You should avoid actively seeking an underhook if you are already in a compromised position (e.g., being stacked) or if your opponent has a strong grip on you and you can't create the necessary space or angle to establish a good frame. Overextending for an underhook in the wrong situation can lead to giving up your back or a submission.
