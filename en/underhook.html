The Underhook: A Fundamental BJJ Position for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Underhook is a dominant position where one grappler secures an arm around the opponent's torso, creating a powerful connection. It's crucial for controlling posture and initiating sweeps or passes.

White belts often struggle by overextending, losing their base, or using their arm strength inefficiently instead of leveraging their entire body.

The key mechanical insight is to drive your shoulder into their chest while simultaneously creating a tight frame with your arm, using your hips to anchor your base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a position with your opponent in front of you, such as from closed guard or half guard.
    2. Lower your hips slightly, bringing your pelvis closer to the mat.
    3. Extend your non-hooking arm to establish a frame against the opponent's hip or thigh to prevent them from posturing up.
    4. Drive your shoulder into the opponent's sternum or upper abdomen, initiating a slight forward pressure.
    5. Reach your hooking arm underneath the opponent's armpit, aiming for their back or far hip.
    6. Secure a grip on the opponent's belt, gi pants (near the hip), or establish a gable grip on your own wrist behind their back.
    7. Shift your weight slightly onto your hooking side, tucking your chin to your chest to avoid giving up your neck.
    8. Ensure your base is wide, with your knees bent and feet flat on the mat, creating a stable foundation.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect arm extension: Extending your hooking arm fully with a straight elbow while the opponent is postured up can hyper-extend your elbow joint, risking ligament tears (ulnar collateral ligament).
    • Losing base: Reaching for the underhook without a stable base and driving your shoulder in can lead to you being swept or falling forward, risking knee or ankle sprains as your weight shifts unpredictably.
    • Over-reliance on grip strength: Trying to muscle the underhook without proper body mechanics and hip drive can strain your shoulder joint, leading to rotator cuff impingement or tears.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the motion of lowering your hips, framing with one arm, and reaching for the underhook, focusing on shoulder drive and hip connection. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drilling - static (25% resistance): Opponent stands or kneels passively. Practice securing the underhook from a guard position, focusing on the exact grip and frame. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling - controlled movement (50% resistance): Opponent offers light resistance, trying to deny the underhook. Focus on timing your shoulder drive and hip movement to secure the position. (5 reps per side)
    4. Guard retention drill (75% resistance): Opponent attempts to pass your guard while you focus on establishing and maintaining the underhook to prevent the pass. (3 minutes)
    5. Positional sparring - guard (90% resistance): Start from closed guard. You aim to establish an underhook to break posture and threaten submissions or sweeps. Opponent defends. (5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (100% resistance): Integrate the underhook into your regular sparring rounds, actively seeking opportunities to establish and utilize it. (5-10 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard, creating an opportunity to establish control.
    • When you are in your opponent's guard and they are postured up, allowing you to underhook their arm.
    • When transitioning between guard positions, using the underhook to prevent them from establishing dominant control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Frame and Hip Escape: If your opponent attempts an underhook, immediately frame with your opposite arm against their shoulder or chest and hip escape away to create space and break their connection.
    • Head and Arm Control: If your opponent secures an underhook, control their head and arm by posting your hand on their head or shoulder and using your free arm to control their elbow or wrist, preventing them from driving in.
    • Stance Adjustment and Weight Shift: If your opponent tries to underhook you, widen your base, lower your hips, and shift your weight to the side of the underhook to make it difficult for them to drive their shoulder into you.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an underhook in BJJ?

    Shoulder pain often stems from trying to muscle the underhook with your arm alone, rather than using your entire body. This puts excessive strain on the joint. Instead, focus on lowering your hips and driving your shoulder into your opponent's torso while keeping your elbow slightly bent. This leverages your body's power, not just arm strength, and protects your shoulder.

    Q: How can I get an underhook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Don't try to match their strength. Instead, focus on timing and angles. Lower your hips significantly, almost to the mat, and use your legs to create space. Drive your shoulder into their chest at a slight upward angle. Secure a grip on their belt or pants to anchor yourself, then use your hips to generate forward momentum to secure the underhook.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an underhook when defending a guard pass?

    The optimal time to attempt an underhook while defending a Guard Pass is as the opponent begins to commit to their weight transfer. As they drive forward and attempt to flatten you out, this creates a momentary opening. Drive your shoulder into their chest and reach for the underhook simultaneously with their forward momentum to disrupt their pass and regain control.
