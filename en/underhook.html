The BJJ Underhook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The underhook is a fundamental control position, typically achieved from guard or during transitions, aimed at controlling your opponent's torso. It allows you to establish superior leverage for sweeps, passes, or submissions, preventing them from establishing their own dominant positions.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength rather than understanding the body mechanics, leading to wasted energy and potential injury. They fail to create a stable base or use their hips effectively, making the underhook easily countered.

The key mechanical insight is using your shoulder and elbow to create a wedge, driving your opponent's weight onto their own base, while simultaneously using your hips to create off-balancing angles. This positional advantage is crucial.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated guard, establish a collar grip with your non-underhooking arm, keeping your elbow tucked to your side.
    2. Extend your underhooking arm, reaching around your opponent's torso.
    3. Lock your bicep to your opponent's latissimus dorsi muscle, creating a strong, stable connection.
    4. Drive your shoulder into your opponent's sternum or clavicle, creating a wedge.
    5. Simultaneously, pivot your hips, turning your pelvis towards your opponent's hips.
    6. Lower your base by bending your knees and planting your feet firmly on the mat, creating a wide, stable platform.
    7. Use your underhooking arm to slightly pull their weight forward and onto their base, compromising their balance.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Elbow Flare: Extending the elbow of the underhooking arm outwards, creating a large gap between your arm and their body. This damages the glenohumeral joint (shoulder) by allowing excessive external rotation and potential labral tears. The correct alternative is to keep the elbow tucked, creating a tight connection.
    • Hunching the Back: Rounding the upper back and neck to try and reach the underhook. This puts excessive strain on the cervical spine (neck) and thoracic spine (upper back), leading to muscle strains and disc compression. The correct alternative is to maintain a neutral spine and use hip and shoulder pressure.
    • Stiff Arming: Trying to force the underhook with a straight, rigid arm. This can lead to tendonitis in the elbow or wrist due to repetitive, non-yielding force. The correct alternative is to use a slight bend in the elbow and drive with the shoulder and hip.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation: Practice reaching the underhook position and holding it for 10 seconds, focusing on shoulder and hip placement. (20 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partnered Static Hold: With a partner standing, practice securing the underhook and holding their weight without moving. Focus on creating the wedge and hip angle. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    3. Controlled Transition Drill: From closed guard, practice opening the guard, establishing the underhook, and returning to closed guard. Partner offers minimal resistance. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    4. Guard Retention Drill: Opponent attempts to pass guard. You focus on securing the underhook to prevent the pass and maintain guard. (10 rounds, 60 seconds each, 60% resistance).
    5. Sweep Attempt Drill: From guard, secure the underhook and attempt a basic sweep (e.g., scissor sweep). Partner defends passively. (10 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling (Controlled): Implement the underhook into your rolling rounds, focusing on securing it and using it to create opportunities. Communicate with your partner about intensity. (3 rounds, 5 minutes each, 80% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up from your guard, creating space to insert the arm.
    • During transitions, such as when they are trying to pass your guard and you need to re-establish control.
    • When you are defending a pass and need to create a strong frame to prevent them from flattening you out.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The Elbow Escape: If your opponent has a strong underhook, keep your hips down and use your free hand to grip their hip. Drive your hips away and bring your knee towards your chest, creating space to shrimp out and reset.
    • The Frame and Shuck: If your opponent tries to drive their shoulder into your sternum, use your forearm to create a frame against their head or shoulder. Simultaneously, step your leg over their head and use your underhooking arm to pull their head down, shucking them to the other side.
    • The Overhook Counter: If they have the underhook, you can attempt to secure an overhook on the opposite side. This creates a deadlock situation, forcing them to either commit to the underhook or release it, giving you an opportunity to escape or attack.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an underhook?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are overextending your arm and relying on a stiff, straight limb to create the underhook. This puts immense stress on the shoulder joint. Instead, focus on driving your shoulder into your opponent's torso and using your hips to create the leverage, keeping a slight bend in your elbow for stability and power.

    Q: How do I get an underhook against a bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, brute strength is your enemy. Focus on timing and angles. Look for moments when they are slightly off-balance or reaching. Use your hips to drive into their center of gravity, creating a wedge with your shoulder. Think of it as fitting your body into a small space, rather than overpowering them.

    Q: You should prioritize the underhook when your opponent is posturing up and creating space, or when they are actively trying to pass your guard. A closed guard is excellent for control and submissions, but an underhook provides superior leverage for sweeps and transitions, especially when your opponent is trying to flatten you out or establish a dominant passing position.
