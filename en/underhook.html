The Underhook: A Foundational BJJ Position for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The underhook is a dominant position gained from various guards and scrambles, aiming to control your opponent's torso. It allows for superior leverage and is crucial for sweeps and positional advancement.

White belts often fail by reaching with their arm and compromising their base, leading to loss of control or submission. They also forget to connect their shoulder to their opponent's hip.

The key insight is creating a strong frame with your arm, driving your shoulder into their hip, and using your core to connect your body, making you a unified structure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a kneeling or staggered stance, position your opponent in front of you.
    2. Reach your non-dominant arm *under* your opponent's armpit, aiming for their far hip or rib cage.
    3. Grip their gi pants at the hip or their gi fabric on their side, ensuring your forearm is tight against their torso.
    4. Drive your shoulder into their hip, creating a strong frame.
    5. Keep your elbow tucked and your forearm connected to their body.
    6. Simultaneously, drive your hips forward and slightly to the side, perpendicular to your opponent.
    7. Maintain a narrow base with your knees and feet, keeping your weight distributed forward.
    8. Your head should be up, facing your opponent, and your posture should be upright but engaged.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Reaching with the elbow: A white belt might extend their elbow fully to get the underhook, leaving their shoulder exposed. This can lead to a shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strain as the opponent drives into the extended limb.
    • Lack of hip connection: Failing to drive the shoulder into the opponent's hip creates a gap. The opponent can then use this space to spin out or pass your guard, potentially leading to a knee hyperextension if you try to recover incorrectly.
    • Wide base and upright posture: Standing too tall with a wide base makes you an easy target for takedowns and prevents effective weight transfer. This uncontrolled movement can result in ankle sprains or knee ligament damage when the opponent exploits the instability.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation (0% resistance): Practice reaching for the underhook and driving your shoulder into a dummy or a stable object for 5 minutes, focusing on the hip connection and frame. (50 reps)
    2. Partner Mirroring (0% resistance): With a partner standing, mirror their movements, practicing the reach and hip drive for underhook acquisition. (30 reps per side)
    3. Passive Underhook (25% resistance): Your partner stands passively, allowing you to secure the underhook and hold the position. Focus on maintaining the frame and hip pressure. (1 minute hold, 10 reps)
    4. Controlled Underhook Entry (50% resistance): Your partner offers light resistance as you enter the underhook from a neutral position (e.g., collar-and-sleeve grip). (30 seconds to secure, 10 reps)
    5. Underhook Control with Partner Movement (75% resistance): Your partner attempts to break your underhook or move slightly, and you focus on maintaining control by adjusting your hip and frame. (1 minute, 5 reps)
    6. Live Rolling Integration (90-100% resistance): In rolling, actively look for opportunities to secure the underhook from various positions, practicing control and transition. (5 minute rounds, multiple rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating an opening to reach under their arm.
    • During guard recovery when your opponent has passed part of your guard, allowing you to establish a defensive underhook.
    • In scrambles or transitions where you can quickly secure the underhook before your opponent stabilizes.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opposite Side Underhook: If your opponent secures an underhook, immediately reach for the underhook on the opposite side of their body. Drive your shoulder into their hip to negate their leverage and create your own frame.
    • Breaking the Grip and Framing: If they have a strong underhook grip, use your free hand to peel their gi grip off your hip or waist. Simultaneously, extend your arm to create a frame and push their body away.
    • Spinning Under: If your opponent has a dominant underhook and is pressuring forward, use your frame to create space and then spin your hips underneath their body to face them or transition to a sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an underhook in BJJ?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are reaching with an extended elbow and not driving your shoulder into your opponent's hip. This creates a leverage point for them to push into, straining your shoulder joint. Ensure your elbow stays slightly bent and your shoulder makes firm contact with their hip, creating a solid frame and connecting your body.

    Q: How do I get an underhook against a bigger and stronger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, timing and precision are key. Instead of trying to muscle it, wait for them to create a slight opening, perhaps when they are adjusting their weight or reaching. Use your hips to drive forward and into their hip as you secure the underhook grip, using their own weight against them. Focus on creating a tight frame with your forearm against their torso.

    Q: When should I prioritize getting an underhook over other grips in BJJ?

    Prioritize the underhook when you want to control your opponent's posture and prevent them from effectively passing your guard or advancing position. It's also essential for setting up sweeps from guard (like Butterfly Guard) and for establishing dominant control in scrambles. It provides a strong base from which to initiate offense or defend.
