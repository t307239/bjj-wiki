The Underhook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Foundation

The underhook is a fundamental control position, typically established when you are inside your opponent's guard or during scrambles. It allows you to disrupt their base and create opportunities for sweeps or passes.

White belts often fail by attempting to muscle the underhook instead of using leverage, leading to energy expenditure and poor positioning.

The key mechanical insight is to use your shoulder and hip to create a wedge, driving your opponent off-balance while securing your arm's depth.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a kneeling or staggered stance, approach your opponent.
    2. Drive your lead shoulder into their hip socket, creating a slight forward pressure.
    3. As your shoulder makes contact, extend your lead arm underneath their armpit.
    4. Grip their far latissimus dorsi muscle or the fabric of their gi on their back, just below the armpit.
    5. Simultaneously, drive your own hips slightly forward and to the side of your opponent's hips.
    6. Your weight should be distributed primarily onto your lead leg and the ball of your rear foot, with your base slightly wider than shoulder-width.
    7. Rotate your torso slightly, ensuring your shoulder is snug against their torso and your elbow is tucked towards your own body for leverage.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Reaching for the underhook with a straight arm and attempting to pull their arm across: This exposes your shoulder joint to a potential kimura or americana, as the straightened arm lacks the structural integrity to resist torque.
    • Trying to muscle the underhook with your neck and head forward: This can lead to neck strain or hyperextension, especially if your opponent pushes back forcefully.
    • Sacrificing your base and letting your hips fall back when attempting the underhook: This removes your base of support, making you vulnerable to sweeps and allowing your opponent to easily disengage or improve their position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the body mechanics of driving your shoulder and inserting your arm without a partner (50 reps).
    2. Partner drill (static): With a compliant partner, practice establishing the underhook from a neutral kneeling position, focusing on grip and hip angle (25 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (light pressure): Partner applies minimal resistance. Focus on driving through and securing the underhook with correct weight distribution (50 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (medium pressure): Partner actively resists. Work on driving and maintaining the underhook through their attempts to break it (50 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Situational sparring: Start from positions where an underhook is likely (e.g., inside guard, during scrambles) and focus solely on securing and holding the underhook (3 x 2-minute rounds, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the underhook as a primary control tool, looking for opportunities to use it for sweeps or transitions (5 x 5-minute rounds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass such as a knee-slice or toreando, creating an opening to enter.
    • When you are inside your opponent's guard and they are posturing up, creating space to drive your shoulder.
    • During scrambles and transitions, when both parties are momentarily off-balance and seeking dominant grips.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Whizzer" (Overhook): As your opponent attempts the underhook, quickly secure an overhook on the same side. Drive your shoulder down and slightly back, creating a superior lever to break their underhook and create space.
    • Hip Escape and Angle Change: If you feel the underhook being established, immediately hip escape away and pivot your hips to face your opponent's attacking shoulder. This disrupts their leverage and allows you to disengage or counter-attack.
    • "Shrimping" and Framing: Use your legs to create frames between your body and your opponent's. "Shrimp" your hips away to create distance, then re-establish your base or transition to a different guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to get an underhook against a bigger opponent?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are trying to use your arm and shoulder strength alone, rather than your body's structure. Against a bigger opponent, you must use your hips to drive into them and your core to create a stable wedge. Ensure your elbow is tucked and you are driving your shoulder into their torso, not just reaching with your arm.

    Q: How can I get an underhook when my opponent is turtled up?

    When your opponent is Turtled, the underhook is typically achieved by attacking their hips or base. Drive your chest into their back and use your arms to hook around their hips, then drive your shoulder underneath their armpit. Focus on breaking their posture by pulling them forward slightly while driving your underhook deep.

    Q: When is the best time to let go of an underhook if I can't secure a dominant position?

    You should immediately disengage from an underhook if you feel your base compromised or your arm trapped in a vulnerable position. If your opponent is successfully countering your underhook attempt by creating a strong overhook or bridging, it's better to reset and create space rather than forcing a losing battle.
