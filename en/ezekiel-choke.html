Ezekiel Choke: The Essential BJJ Technique for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Ezekiel Choke is typically applied from Closed Guard or mount, aiming to constrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries. It's a powerful choke often initiated with a sleeve and collar grip.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength rather than precise leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Understanding the subtle mechanics is crucial.

The core principle is creating a constricting loop using your arm and the opponent's own gi, tightening it through body weight and precise hip movement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a cross-collar grip with your right hand (thumb inside the collar). Your left hand grips the cuff of your opponent's right sleeve, creating a fist with your thumb inside.
    2. Pull your opponent's head down towards your chest, breaking their posture and creating a tight connection.
    3. Rotate your right forearm to be perpendicular to your opponent's neck, with the forearm pressing against one side of their trachea.
    4. Insert your left fist (the one gripping the sleeve) directly under your right forearm, against the opposite side of their trachea, forming a "fist-grip" choke.
    5. Tuck your chin down and slightly to the right, creating a stable base for the choke.
    6. Shift your hips slightly to the left, creating a slight angle and increasing the pressure.
    7. Squeeze your elbows inwards towards your body while simultaneously driving your hips forward, tightening the choke.
    8. Maintain constant pressure by keeping your head tucked and your elbows close to your body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke by simply pushing your right arm forward: This can hyperextend the elbow of the arm controlling the sleeve, potentially damaging the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL).

    Correct: Rotate your right forearm and drive your hips forward, not just push with the arm.

    • Gripping the sleeve too loosely or with fingers exposed: This can lead to a wrist sprain or fracture if the opponent defends aggressively by pulling their arm.

    Correct: Form a tight fist with your thumb inside the sleeve cuff.

    • Arching your back excessively while attempting the choke: This can put strain on your lumbar spine and offer less effective pressure transfer.

    Correct: Tuck your chin and drive with your hips, keeping your core engaged.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip setup and arm rotation without a partner. Focus on the feel of the forearm and fist placement. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a willing partner, practice the full sequence of grips, posture break, and choke application. Focus on correct body mechanics. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to posture break and choke. Focus on maintaining tightness and correct hip movement. (30 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Partner actively defends the choke. Focus on maintaining your grips and adjusting your body to overcome their defense. (40 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Situational sparring: Attempt the Ezekiel Choke from closed guard during light sparring rounds. Focus on timing and application. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Incorporate the Ezekiel Choke into your regular sparring. Aim to set it up and execute it against an actively resisting opponent. (10 minutes, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard and you can secure a strong cross-collar grip.
    • When your opponent tries to pass your guard and you can isolate an arm and secure the sleeve grip.
    • From mount, when you can establish a strong cross-collar grip and control their head.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Grip Break (Opponent's Defense): The opponent can break your right hand's collar grip by prying your fingers open with their own forearm or by creating space with their elbow.

    Correct Application: Maintain tightness and continue to drive hips forward, or transition to another attack if the grip is broken.

    • Arm Escape (Opponent's Defense): The opponent can pull their sleeve arm out of your left fist before you can establish the choke, or they can bring their elbow to their hip to create space.

    Correct Application: Be quick with your grip and squeeze your elbows in immediately to prevent escape.

    • Posture Up (Opponent's Defense): If the opponent maintains good posture, it's harder to get the choke in. They can try to stand up or push your hips away.

    Correct Application: Focus on breaking their posture first by pulling their head down and using your legs to control their hips.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Ezekiel Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs if you're gripping the sleeve with your fingers too far out, creating a lever arm that stresses your wrist joint. Also, if you're trying to 'muscle' the choke by pulling your sleeve arm outwards, you're putting excessive torque on your wrist. Ensure your fist is tight and your thumb is inside the cuff, and focus on squeezing your elbows inward and driving your hips forward for the choke.

    Q: How can I use the Ezekiel Choke against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on breaking their posture completely by pulling their head down to your chest and using your legs to control their hips. Do not try to overpower them. The Ezekiel Choke relies on constriction, not brute force. Ensure your forearm is deep and your fist is snug against their neck. Shift your hips to create a slight angle to maximize the choking pressure.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an Ezekiel Choke, and how do I avoid giving up my back?

    The Ezekiel Choke is best attempted when your opponent is slightly postured up, or when you've managed to isolate an arm during a Guard Pass attempt. To avoid giving up your back, always maintain a tight connection with your legs (closed guard) or your body (mount). If you feel your opponent is about to escape and potentially take your back, don't commit fully to the choke; be ready to transition to a defensive posture or another submission.
