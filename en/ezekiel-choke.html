Ezekiel Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Ezekiel Choke is typically applied from Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries. It's a powerful submission when executed correctly.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of body control and understanding of leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. They tend to rely on raw strength rather than technique.

The key mechanical insight is using your forearm as a fulcrum against the opponent's neck, driven by hip pressure and a precise grip. This maximizes choke efficiency with minimal strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a closed guard position. Secure your opponent's posture by pulling them down with your legs.
    2. Insert your right forearm deep into the opponent's left collar. Your palm should face upwards.
    3. Grip your own right bicep with your left hand. This creates the 'sleeve' grip for the choke.
    4. Adjust your body: Lower your hips slightly and angle your torso approximately 30 degrees to your left.
    5. Extend your right forearm across the opponent's trachea. Your elbow should be slightly higher than your wrist.
    6. Arch your back and drive your hips forward and slightly up. This applies pressure from your forearm into their neck.
    7. Maintain head control by keeping your head tight to their shoulder, preventing them from posturing up.
    8. Squeeze your biceps together and slightly retract your right elbow to complete the choke.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke with your forearm across the *windpipe* instead of the *carotid artery*: This can cause severe damage to the trachea and esophagus. Always aim for the side of the neck.
    • Gripping your own bicep too loosely: This results in a loss of structural integrity and prevents effective pressure transfer, potentially leading to a failed submission and wasted energy.
    • Forcing the choke with shoulder and arm strength alone: This overloads the shoulder joint, increasing the risk of rotator cuff tears and impingement. Focus on hip drive and body mechanics instead.
    • Allowing the opponent to create space by posturing up: This compromises the choke's effectiveness and exposes your own neck to counter-attacks. Maintain tight leg control.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip sequence and forearm insertion without a partner. (10 reps per side)
    2. Static partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner in closed guard, practice inserting the forearm and gripping your bicep. Focus on body positioning and hip drive. (10 reps per side)
    3. Light resistance drill (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to posture up. Focus on maintaining leg control and executing the choke mechanics. (5 reps per side)
    4. Controlled resistance drill (50% resistance): Partner attempts to defend by posturing slightly. Focus on hip drive and maintaining the choke's structure. (5 reps per side)
    5. Scenario drill (75% resistance): Start from closed guard with a partner actively defending. Focus on setting up the Ezekiel and completing the choke. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Attempt the Ezekiel Choke from closed guard during positional sparring. Prioritize correct mechanics over submission. (3-5 attempts per round)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is postured down in your closed guard, creating an opening for collar grips.
    • When the opponent is trying to break your closed guard by posturing up, allowing you to secure the grip as they rise.
    • Immediately after transitioning from another guard, if you can secure the collar grip before they establish strong posture control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posture up and create space: The opponent can counter by driving their hips into you and creating space to relieve pressure. This is achieved by widening their base and pushing their knees towards your hips.
    • Grip Release (Hand Fighting): The opponent can attempt to break your grip by striking your hands or arms with their free hand, specifically targeting your grip on your own bicep or the collar grip.
    • Shoulder Roll Defense: If the choke is applied incompletely, the opponent can roll their shoulders away from the choking arm, creating a 'dead zone' and relieving pressure. This requires significant hip and shoulder mobility.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Ezekiel Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you're over-extending your wrist in an attempt to create leverage. Instead of relying on wrist flexion, ensure your forearm is positioned correctly across the carotid artery and drive your hips forward. Keep your wrist neutral and use your entire body's momentum.

    Q: How can I apply the Ezekiel Choke against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on breaking their posture down aggressively by pulling their head towards your chest with your legs. Once their posture is broken, insert your forearm deep and use your hips to drive into them, creating the necessary pressure. Don't try to overpower them; use their size against them by creating a tight, structurally sound choke.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Ezekiel Choke, and when should I avoid it?

    The Ezekiel Choke is best attempted when the opponent is relatively flat on their back or postured down, allowing for deep collar grips. Avoid it when the opponent has strong posture and is controlling your hips effectively, as this makes it difficult to establish the necessary leverage and can expose you to Guard Passes. Also, be cautious if the opponent has very thick necks, as the choke may be less effective.
