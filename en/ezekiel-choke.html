Ezekiel Choke: The Ultimate BJJ Guide for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Ezekiel Choke is a powerful submission typically initiated from Closed Guard or side control, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain. It uses the gi sleeve to create a constricting grip around the opponent's neck.

White belts often struggle with the Ezekiel by trying to muscle it, leading to poor positioning and potential injury. They miss the subtle leverage points that make the choke effective without brute strength.

The key mechanical insight is utilizing your forearm as a fulcrum against your opponent's carotid artery, using your body weight and hip drive for leverage, not just arm strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from closed guard: Secure your opponent's collar with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel, creating a standard cross-collar grip. Your left hand grips your own right sleeve, thumb inside your right wrist, forming a pistol grip.
    2. Maintain a strong closed guard: Your knees should be tight around your opponent's hips, preventing them from posturing up. Your hips should be elevated slightly off the mat, allowing for hip movement.
    3. Begin to flatten your opponent: Use your legs to break their posture by pulling their head down towards your chest, while simultaneously driving your hips forward and up.
    4. Initiate the choke: As their posture breaks, slide your right forearm deeper into their neck, ensuring the lapel is snug against their carotid artery.
    5. Torque the grip: Rotate your right wrist inward and upward, simultaneously pulling your left elbow down and back. This motion tightens the lapel around the neck.
    6. Apply hip pressure: Drive your hips into your opponent's torso, creating a downward and forward pressure. This compresses their chest and amplifies the choke.
    7. Maintain head control: Keep your opponent's head pinned to your chest throughout the execution to prevent them from escaping the angle.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive force with only arm strength: This can lead to biceps tendon tears or shoulder impingement as the joint is overloaded without proper leverage.
    • Over-extending the neck or spine when trying to finish: This risks cervical spine strain or disc injury by forcing unnatural hyperextension.
    • Releasing the sleeve grip prematurely or incorrectly: This compromises the choke's effectiveness and can lead to the opponent posturing up, risking shoulder dislocation if the arm is trapped incorrectly.
    • Gripping the gi sleeve too loosely: This allows the sleeve to slip, reducing the choking pressure and potentially causing friction burns on the wrist.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the grip setup and the motion of tightening the choke on a dummy or a folded mat. Focus on the wrist rotation and forearm pressure. (50 reps)
    2. Partnered drilling, no resistance (0% resistance): With a willing partner, practice the entire sequence from closed guard. Focus on achieving the correct grips and body positioning without any pressure. (20 reps)
    3. Light resistance drilling (25% resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice breaking posture and applying the choke. Focus on the hip drive and shoulder rotation. (15 reps)
    4. Controlled resistance drilling (50% resistance): Your partner actively defends but allows you to work the mechanics. Focus on maintaining control and adjusting your body position. (10 reps)
    5. Moderate resistance drilling (75% resistance): Your partner defends more actively, requiring you to be more precise with your grips and timing. Focus on finishing the choke. (5 reps)
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Attempt the Ezekiel Choke during sparring, focusing on recognizing the opportunity and executing the technique with proper mechanics. (Until successful or opponent escapes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating an opening for you to break their posture.
    • When you have secured a deep cross-collar grip and your opponent is trying to stack you or create space.
    • When you have transitioned to side control and can secure a collar grip, then reach for your sleeve.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Posture Up and Grip Release: The opponent can immediately posture up, creating space and breaking the collar grip. They should then immediately remove the sleeve hand from their own wrist.
    • Shoulder Rotation and Hip Escape: The opponent can rotate their shoulders away from the choking arm and simultaneously hip escape to create distance and relieve pressure.
    • Framing with the Elbow: The opponent can use their free arm to frame against your bicep or shoulder, preventing you from driving your forearm deep into the neck and creating space to escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Ezekiel Choke from closed guard?

    Your wrist likely hurts because you are trying to generate the choking force solely by bending your wrist. The Ezekiel Choke's power comes from rotating your entire forearm and driving your hips. Ensure your thumb is inside your own sleeve grip and focus on the inward rotation of your forearm and the downward pull of your opposite elbow, using your hips for leverage.

    Q: How can I finish the Ezekiel Choke against a bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on breaking their posture down completely by driving your hips up and pulling their head towards your chest. Once their posture is broken, slide your forearm deep into their neck and use your body weight to drive down. The key is to create a tight seal with the gi and use your hips to amplify the pressure, rather than relying solely on arm strength.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an Ezekiel Choke against an opponent who is actively defending?

    The ideal time to attempt the Ezekiel Choke is when your opponent has committed to a specific movement that exposes their neck, such as trying to pass your guard or when they are postured high. Look for moments where their head is slightly lowered or their neck is accessible. A common mistake is attempting it when they are already in a strong defensive posture; wait for an opening created by their own actions.
