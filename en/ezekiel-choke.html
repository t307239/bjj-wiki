Ezekiel Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Ezekiel choke is typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries. It's a powerful submission that can be applied quickly if set up correctly.

White belts often struggle with the Ezekiel choke due to a lack of understanding of precise grip mechanics and body positioning, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is creating a tight "figure-four" grip and using your forearm to drive into the opponent's carotid artery, while your other arm provides stabilizing pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip Setup: From closed guard, establish a "figure-four" grip. Your right hand slides inside your opponent's gi collar, palm facing upwards. Your left hand grips the outside of your right forearm, palm facing downwards, fingers pointing towards your right elbow.
    2. Arm Placement: Ensure your right forearm is snug against your opponent's right carotid artery (on their neck). Your left hand grips your right wrist, creating a stable lever.
    3. Hip Angle: Slightly angle your hips to your left, bringing your right hip closer to your opponent's left shoulder. This creates a fulcrum.
    4. Weight Distribution: Shift your weight forward, driving your chest slightly into your opponent's chest. This prevents them from posturing up.
    5. Choking Action: Keeping the figure-four grip tight, extend your right arm slightly while simultaneously pulling your left elbow down and back. This action tightens the choke.
    6. Head Control: Use your head to push into your opponent's head, preventing them from turning away and relieving pressure.
    7. Core Engagement: Engage your core muscles to maintain tightness in the grip and body position throughout the submission attempt.
    8. Finishing Pressure: The choke is applied by the pressure of your right forearm against the carotid artery, amplified by the stabilizing force of your left hand and the body mechanics of the "figure-four."

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Grip Placement: A common error is placing the choking forearm too high on the neck, missing the carotid artery. This can strain the bicep or cause a "windpipe choke," which is less effective and potentially harmful to the trachea.
    • Excessive Neck Rotation: Attempting to force the choke by violently twisting the opponent's head can lead to cervical spine strain or ligament damage. Always focus on controlled pressure and body mechanics, not brute force.
    • Arm Entanglement: Allowing the opponent to "frame" with their arms inside your choking arm can compromise the grip and lead to your own arm being trapped, potentially causing shoulder impingement or a joint lock attempt against you.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip Practice: Practice forming the figure-four grip with your own arms. Focus on the feel of the forearm against the imaginary carotid. (50 reps)
    2. Partnered Grip Placement (0% Resistance): With a partner, practice establishing the figure-four grip and placing the choking forearm correctly on their neck. Partner holds still. (20 reps)
    3. Controlled Choke Movement (25% Resistance): From closed guard, with partner providing minimal resistance, practice the slight hip angle and body drive. Apply just enough pressure to feel the setup. (15 reps)
    4. Full Setup with Light Resistance (50% Resistance): Execute the entire Ezekiel choke setup from closed guard. Partner offers light resistance to posture and framing. (10 reps)
    5. Submission Attempt with Moderate Resistance (75% Resistance): Attempt the Ezekiel choke with a partner who actively tries to defend by posturing and framing. Focus on maintaining your grip and body position. (5 reps)
    6. Live Rolling Application (90-100% Resistance): Integrate the Ezekiel choke into live rolling scenarios, looking for opportunities to apply it from closed guard when the opportunity arises. Focus on correct mechanics over immediate taps.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating space between their neck and your chest.
    • When your opponent has a weak grip on your collar or is trying to break your guard.
    • When your opponent is trying to "stack" you, bringing their weight forward.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Defend the Grip: As the opponent attempts to form the figure-four, actively use your hands to "frame" their choking arm, preventing the forearm from reaching your carotid. Simultaneously, try to break their grip on your collar.
    • Break Posture and Create Space: If they are posturing up, use your legs to push their hips away, creating space to escape or switch to a different guard. This disrupts their leverage for the choke.
    • Turn Your Head: If the choke is being applied, turn your head sharply towards the choking arm. This creates a small amount of space and can relieve pressure, allowing you to defend or escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Ezekiel choke?

    Wrist pain often stems from an incorrect "figure-four" grip. Ensure your choking forearm (right arm) is directly on the carotid artery, and your gripping hand (left hand) is securely on your own right wrist, not just the gi. This distributes the pressure across your forearm and wrist, rather than isolating the wrist joint itself.

    Q: How can I successfully apply the Ezekiel choke against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on maximizing your leverage. Ensure your hip angle is sharp and your weight is driving forward. Use your legs to control their hips and prevent them from posturing up and using their size advantage. The "figure-four" grip must be exceptionally tight to overcome their size.

    Q: I can't seem to finish the Ezekiel choke; my opponent just taps their leg. What am I doing wrong?

    Tapping the leg is a sign that your choke setup is likely too loose, or you're not applying consistent pressure. Ensure your choking forearm is deep and pressing firmly into the carotid. The finishing movement should be a controlled extension of your choking arm and a pull down of your other elbow, creating a consistent, crushing pressure rather than a jerky motion.
