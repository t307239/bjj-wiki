Ezekiel Choke: Your Complete BJJ Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Ezekiel Choke is a powerful submission often initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries. It's a fantastic finisher once mastered.

White belts often struggle with the Ezekiel Choke due to a lack of understanding of grip independence and insufficient pressure control, leading to wasted energy and ineffective attempts.

The key mechanical insight is utilizing your opponent's own body as leverage by creating a tight frame and driving your forearm into their neck, while your other arm acts as a stabilizing anchor.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a closed guard position, ensuring your hips are mobile. Secure a 'cross-collar' grip with your right hand, thumb inside the opponent's gi collar, fingers gripping the fabric firmly.
    2. With your left hand, reach *through* the opening created by your right arm and grab your own right forearm, creating a 'sleeve grip' on your own gi.
    3. Maintain your closed guard, keeping your knees tight to your opponent's sides to prevent them from posturing up.
    4. Begin to angle your hips slightly to your right, creating a subtle torque.
    5. Drive your right forearm (the one in the collar) horizontally across your opponent's trachea, applying pressure.
    6. Simultaneously, use your left hand (gripping your own forearm) to pull your right forearm deeper into the choke, acting as a winch.
    7. Maintain a stable base by keeping your feet hooked behind your opponent's hips, preventing them from bridging or escaping.
    8. Apply constant, controlled pressure by squeezing your elbows together and driving your chest forward, creating a constricting force on the carotid arteries.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke with your forearm angled upwards towards the chin: This can hyperextend the cervical spine, risking a neck injury. The correct movement is to keep the forearm parallel to the ground, pressing directly into the trachea.
    • Gripping too loosely with the 'sleeve grip' on your own forearm: This allows the choke to slip and reduces power. The correct grip is firm and secure, ensuring your forearm doesn't slide out.
    • Overextending your back while attempting the choke: This can strain your lumbar spine. Maintain an upright posture and focus on driving your hips forward and squeezing your elbows.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip setup and forearm drive motion without an opponent. Focus on the feeling of the forearm pressure. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice establishing the closed guard and executing the choke mechanics. Focus on grip placement and body positioning. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance to your guard retention and choke attempts. Focus on maintaining your frames and applying the choke smoothly. (20 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Your partner actively defends, but allows the choke setup to be completed. Practice finishing the choke against light defense. (15 reps per side)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Ezekiel Choke from closed guard during controlled rolling sessions. Focus on timing and recognizing opportunities. (5 minutes, 25% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full): Integrate the Ezekiel Choke into your regular rolling. Aim to set it up and finish it naturally as the opportunity arises. (10 minutes, 75-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up in your closed guard, creating space between their head and chest.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard and you can secure the collar grip as they drive forward.
    • When you have successfully established a strong closed guard and your opponent is settled, making them a stationary target.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posture Up and Grip Release: As the choke is being applied, immediately break your opponent's grip on their own forearm by using your elbow to pry it away, and simultaneously posture up by extending your hips and straightening your back to create space. This disrupts the choking mechanism.
    • Shoulder Frame and Hip Escape: If the choke is not fully locked, use your shoulder to create a frame against your opponent's choking forearm. Simultaneously, hip escape to your side to relieve pressure and create an angle to peel their arm off.
    • Head and Neck Protection: If the choke is still developing, tuck your chin to your chest to protect your trachea and use your hands to push away the attacking forearm. This reduces the effectiveness of the choke and allows you to begin posturing.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Ezekiel Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs from an improper 'sleeve grip' on your own forearm. Instead of gripping your gi with all your fingers, ensure your thumb is also secured around your forearm. This creates a more stable fulcrum and prevents your wrist from bending unnaturally. Also, ensure you are driving with your forearm into their neck, not trying to bend your wrist to create the choke.

    Q: How can I use the Ezekiel Choke effectively against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is crucial. Focus on keeping your closed guard tight to prevent them from creating space to posture. When you initiate the choke, angle your hips significantly to your side to get under their shoulder. Drive your choking forearm deep into their neck and use your 'sleeve grip' to pull your own forearm in, effectively using their weight against them.

    Q: Is the Ezekiel Choke a good technique for white belts to start with in BJJ?

    Yes, the Ezekiel Choke is an excellent foundational choke for white belts because it teaches fundamental concepts like grip independence and using your own body as leverage. Mastering the precise grip and forearm placement will build a strong understanding of choke mechanics, which translates to other techniques.
