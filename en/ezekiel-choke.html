The Ezekiel choke is a powerful strangulation technique typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to cut off blood flow to the brain.

White belts often struggle by muscling the choke rather than using leverage and proper grip sequencing.

Its effectiveness relies on creating a tight seal around the carotid arteries with your forearm and the opponent's own collar.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a "figure-four" grip: your right hand grips your own right wrist, palm facing up.
    2. Your left hand then slides its fingers deep into the opponent's right collar, palm facing down.
    3. Your left forearm (the one in the collar) should be tight against the side of the opponent's neck, creating a "blade."
    4. Your right elbow should be tucked in, creating a "shelf" for your left wrist to press against.
    5. Initiate the choke by driving your left elbow down and slightly forward, while simultaneously pulling your right wrist towards your own chest.
    6. Your hips should remain relatively flat, not bridging excessively, to maintain control and prevent the opponent from posturing up.
    7. The pressure comes from the combination of your left forearm pressing into the carotid and your right wrist acting as a fulcrum, driving your left forearm deeper.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly reaching for the opponent's collar with an extended arm: This can expose your shoulder to an armbar and strain your shoulder joint capsule.
    • Applying excessive forward pressure with your head: This can hyperextend your cervical spine, risking disc injury.
    • Trying to "muscle" the choke by pulling with both arms simultaneously: This often results in a weak choke and can strain your biceps and shoulders, while failing to create the necessary pressure seal.
    • Gripping your own wrist too loosely: This allows the "figure-four" to loosen, negating the leverage needed to drive the forearm into the neck effectively.
    • Allowing the opponent to create space between your forearm and their neck: This breaks the seal and prevents the choke from being effective, while potentially allowing them to escape.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the "figure-four" grip and the inward pressure motion without a partner. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice establishing the grips and applying the choke motion slowly, focusing on the feeling of the forearm sealing the neck. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner actively resists by trying to pull their head out slightly. Focus on maintaining grip integrity and the sealing action. (10 reps per side)
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner begins to defend more actively, attempting to create space. Focus on re-establishing the seal and the correct pressure application. (10 reps per side)
    5. Live rolling simulation (75% resistance): Attempt the Ezekiel choke during rolling scenarios where the opponent is not actively trying to escape immediately after the choke is set. (5 attempts)
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Integrate the Ezekiel choke into your rolling, looking for opportunities to set it up from closed guard when the opponent is within range. (Focus on completion)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is posturing up in your closed guard, creating a slight gap.
    • When the opponent is defending your other submissions, leaving their collar accessible.
    • After a failed sweep or submission attempt, when the opponent is momentarily unsettled.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Head and Arm Defense": The opponent tucks their chin tightly to their chest and turns their head away, breaking the seal of your forearm against their carotid.
    • "Grip Break and Posture Up": The opponent uses their free hand to grab your choking arm's wrist and pulls it away while bridging to create space and stand up.
    • "Elbow Escape": The opponent uses their elbow to create space under your choking arm, allowing them to turn into you and relieve pressure.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Ezekiel choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping your own wrist too tightly or at an awkward angle. Ensure your "figure-four" grip is comfortable, with your right hand relaxed around your right wrist, palm facing up. The pressure should be generated by driving your left forearm into the opponent's neck, using your right wrist as a stable pivot point, not by acutely bending your right wrist.

    Q: How can I use the Ezekiel choke against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on keeping your hips low and tight to prevent them from posturing up. Ensure your left forearm is deeply inserted into their collar and creates a complete seal against their carotid artery. The power comes from the precise application of pressure, not brute force, so prioritize grip and angle over strength.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Ezekiel choke, and should I always use the figure-four grip?

    The Ezekiel choke is most effective when the opponent is slightly off-balance or has their posture compromised, creating an opening. While the figure-four grip is the standard and most biomechanically sound method for white belts, variations exist. However, for beginners, mastering the figure-four grip ensures proper leverage and reduces the risk of injury, making it the recommended approach.
