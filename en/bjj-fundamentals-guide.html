BJJ Fundamentals Guide

BJJ Wiki · Updated 2026-03-16

Fundamentals are not beginner concepts — they're the foundation that advanced practitioners return to repeatedly because they underpin everything else. Understanding why techniques work is more valuable than knowing hundreds of individual moves.

Contents

    Base and Posture

    Base is your structural stability — resistance to being moved or toppled. Wide stance, low center of gravity, and weight distributed evenly builds base. Posture is your spinal alignment relative to your opponent. Good posture prevents choking, creates power, and allows effective movement. These two concepts solve more problems than any individual technique.

    Frames and Levers

    Frames are structural braces using your skeletal system to create and maintain distance or redirect force. A properly placed knee frame against a closing guard can hold a 250-pound opponent at bay. Understanding frames is understanding that BJJ is physics — bone structure and alignment, not muscle strength, determines effectiveness.

    💡 Fundamental Law: Leverage defeats strength. A bent joint can be straightened by your entire body weight; a straight joint can be bent by two fingers. Understanding joint mechanics is understanding submission defense and offense simultaneously.

    Grips and Connection

    The quality of your connection to your opponent determines the quality of everything else. Sleeve grips, collar grips, wrist grips — each has optimal positioning that maximizes control while minimizing energy expenditure. Grip fighting is often where matches are won before any technique begins.

    The Positional Hierarchy

    BJJ has a clear hierarchy of positions from worst to best: guard → half guard → side control → knee on belly → mount → back control. Understanding this hierarchy guides decision-making at every moment: always work toward a higher position, never give up a higher position unnecessarily. Submission attempts from lower positions are riskier; from higher positions, safer and more numerous.

    Transitions and Connection

    The space between positions is where most mistakes happen. Sharp transitions — moving quickly and maintaining connection through the transition — prevent opponents from recovering. Slow, disconnected transitions give space and time to re-guard, create frames, and escape.
