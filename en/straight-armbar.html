The Straight Armbar in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Straight Armbar is a fundamental joint lock applied to the opponent's elbow, typically from closed guard or mount. It achieves a submission by hyperextending the elbow joint.

White belts often struggle by "muscling" the submission or failing to isolate the arm correctly, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is controlling the opponent's shoulder and hip while extending your own hips, creating a fulcrum for the elbow hyperextension.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an "S" grip on the opponent's gi sleeve with your right hand, palm facing up, thumb inside the sleeve. Your left hand secures the opponent's wrist.
    2. Shift your hips to your left, creating an angle. Your right knee should drive towards the opponent's left armpit.
    3. Bring your right leg over the opponent's head and chest, securing it around their neck. Your left leg hooks behind their right hip to prevent them from stacking.
    4. Untangle your right arm from between their arms, maintaining the S-grip on their sleeve. Your left hand now secures your own right bicep, creating a "figure-four" grip.
    5. Extend your hips upwards towards the ceiling, simultaneously pulling your opponent's wrist towards your chest with your left hand. Your right elbow remains anchored near your own hip.
    6. Ensure your torso is perpendicular to the opponent's body. Your chest should be pressed against their shoulder blade.
    7. Maintain a strong connection with your legs, squeezing your knees together to control their posture and prevent escape. Your hips remain elevated.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "pulling" with the S-grip arm: This can lead to the opponent's shoulder joint being wrenched, causing a rotator cuff tear or dislocation. The correct movement is to drive your hips up while maintaining the grip.
    • Allowing the opponent to stack by not securing the hip with the trailing leg: This can result in a "stack pass" where the opponent's weight crushes your chest and can injure your ribs or spine. Always hook the hip to maintain control.
    • Not fully extending the hips or pulling the wrist: This often results in a "dead arm" where no pressure is applied. The elbow joint requires sustained extension and wrist control to be effectively attacked.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and leg positioning without a partner. Focus on the transitions and body awareness (10 reps each side).
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Walk through the entire sequence with a compliant partner, focusing on correct grip placement and body angles (10 reps each side).
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Execute the armbar as the partner attempts to defend passively. Focus on maintaining control and applying pressure gradually (5 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drilling (moderate resistance): The partner actively tries to escape, but without full power. Focus on maintaining your structure and finishing the submission (5 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the armbar during live rolling but stop immediately if the submission is deep or if the partner taps. Focus on recognizing opportunities (5 attempts, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (full): Execute the armbar as a natural part of the roll. Focus on finishing the submission without undue force or speed (ongoing, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's arm is extended and they are postured down in your closed guard.
    • When you have successfully swept or passed to mount and the opponent is bridging or trying to relieve pressure by extending their arms.
    • When transitioning from other guard positions like butterfly guard or half guard, and you can isolate an arm.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "stack pass" counter: If the attacker's hips aren't properly controlled, the defender can drive their hips forward and stack the attacker, often breaking the grip and escaping. The attacker must maintain hip control.
    • The "roll through" counter: The defender can attempt to roll their body in the direction of the attacked arm, relieving pressure and potentially escaping the hold. The attacker must secure the wrist and prevent this rolling motion.
    • The "arm pull" escape: The defender can try to pull their arm out by extending it further and pulling it towards their body. The attacker must maintain a strong grip and use their hips for leverage.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Straight Armbar from closed guard?

    Your wrist is likely hurting because you are using your wrist to create the "pulling" force. The Straight Armbar submission comes from extending your hips and pulling the opponent's elbow, not from twisting their wrist. Ensure your S-grip is secure and your left hand is on your own bicep, then drive your hips upwards.

    Q: How can I apply a Straight Armbar against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on creating a tight angle with your hips and using your legs to control their posture and prevent them from posturing up. Ensure your S-grip is deep and your figure-four grip is locked before extending your hips. Do not try to out-muscle them; use their size against them by controlling their limb.

    Q: When is the best time to try for a Straight Armbar from closed guard, and how do I know if it's a good opportunity?

    The best time is when the opponent is postured down, their arm is extended, and they are not actively defending by pulling their arm in. Look for a moment when they are trying to improve their position or are caught off balance. If they are keeping their elbow bent and close to their body, or if they can easily bring their other arm to defend, it's usually not a good opportunity.
