Side control is a dominant positional category in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, achieved when you establish control over your opponent from a position beside their torso.

Many white belts struggle because they focus on holding instead of controlling, allowing their opponent to bridge or shrimp away due to poor weight distribution.

The key mechanical insight is creating a strong, stable base while anchoring your hips low and tight to your opponent's center of gravity.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure a cross-collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel, and a sleeve grip (same-side sleeve) with your left hand, palm facing up.
    2. Body Alignment: Position your chest over your opponent's chest, facing their head.
    3. Hip Angle: Drive your right hip down towards your opponent's left hip, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and theirs.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your opponent's sternum and diaphragm, compressing them downwards.
    5. Leg Placement: Your right knee should be tucked towards their armpit, while your left foot is planted firmly on the mat, providing a stable base.
    6. Head Position: Keep your head on your opponent's right side, driving your forehead into their shoulder to prevent them from turning in.
    7. Arm Control: Your left arm (sleeve grip) prevents their right arm from posturing up or creating space.
    8. Hip Pressure: Continuously drive your right hip into their hip, limiting their ability to shrimp or bridge effectively.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grip: Grabbing only the collar and leaving the sleeve loose allows the opponent to create space with their free arm, leading to potential escapes.
    • Joint Damage: The opponent can use their free arm to frame and push, potentially hyperextending your elbow if you are not careful with your base.
    • Correct: Maintain a strong sleeve grip on the opponent's same-side arm.
    • Weight Distribution: Posting up on your knees with your hips high allows the opponent to bridge and escape easily.
    • Joint Damage: A bridge can cause axial loading on the spine or hyperextension of the knees if the opponent is significantly larger or stronger.
    • Correct: Keep your hips low, driving your weight onto their chest and diaphragm.
    • Framing: Allowing your opponent to create a strong frame with their arms between your chest and theirs enables them to push you away.
    • Joint Damage: A sudden push can cause shoulder impingement or elbow hyperextension if you are not braced.
    • Correct: Use your chest and shoulder to maintain constant pressure, minimizing space for frames.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Drive: From a side control position on a mat dummy, practice driving your hip low and tight, focusing on the 45-degree angle. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Grip and Base Drill: Partner up. From side control, practice establishing the cross-collar and sleeve grip, then driving your hip low and planting your base. Hold for 10 seconds, then reset. (10 sets, 0% resistance)
    3. Controlled Hip Pressure: With a partner, start in side control. The top person focuses solely on maintaining hip pressure and the 45-degree angle, while the bottom person tries to create minimal space. (10 sets, 25% resistance)
    4. Basic Side Control Hold: From side control, the top person establishes the position and holds for 30 seconds. The bottom person applies light pressure to try and escape. (5 sets, 50% resistance)
    5. Simulated Escape Defense: Start in side control. The bottom person attempts a basic bridge or shrimp escape. The top person focuses on maintaining their hip pressure and base to prevent the escape for 15 seconds. (5 sets, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling (Side Control Focus): Begin live rolling with the explicit goal of achieving and maintaining side control for as long as possible, or until a submission is achieved. (3 rounds, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • After successfully executing a guard pass, like a knee-slice pass or headquarters pass.
    • When your opponent attempts a sweep from guard and you manage to stabilize their hips and hips.
    • Following a successful scramble where you end up in a dominant position beside them.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Shrimping (Hip Escape): When the opponent's hips are too high or they are not anchoring their weight properly, create space by driving your hips away from them, planting your feet, and shrimping your hips back to reclaim guard.
    • Bridging: If the opponent's weight is too far forward, use your legs to create a lever. Drive your feet into the mat, arch your back, and explosively lift your hips to create space and potentially flip them over.
    • Re-guarding (Tuck and Turn): If the opponent overcommits to one side, use your free arm to frame, tuck your knees to your chest, and turn your body towards their legs to re-establish guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to hold side control?

    This often happens because you're using your shoulder as a primary point of contact and pressure, rather than your hips and base. If you're allowing your opponent to create a frame with their arm, your shoulder takes the brunt of the pushing force, leading to impingement or strain. Focus on driving your chest and hip into their body, keeping your elbows tucked, and maintaining a low base.

    Q: How can I maintain side control against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, static holding is ineffective. You must focus on dynamic control. Drive your hip into their hip crease and keep your weight centered over their sternum. Use your cross-collar grip to pull their head down and their sleeve grip to control their arm, preventing them from creating space. Continuously adjust your angle to stay ahead of their hips.

    Q: Is it better to have my knee on the mat or my foot when in side control?

    For fundamental side control, your foot should be planted firmly on the mat, providing a stable base. Your knee that's closest to their head should be tucked in towards their armpit, but not necessarily on the mat. Driving your knee onto the mat can sometimes create a pivot point for your opponent to roll you. The key is to use your hip and chest for pressure and your foot for stability.
