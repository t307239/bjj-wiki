Bow and Arrow Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Breakdown

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

This choke is typically applied from the back-Mount position, aiming to secure a submission by constricting the carotid arteries.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage and body mechanics, leading to inefficient attempts and potential injury.

The core principle is creating a fulcrum with your body to isolate and compress the opponent's neck using their own collar and lapel.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From back-mount, secure a deep collar grip with your right hand, fingers inside the gi collar, thumb pointing down.
    2. With your left hand, secure a pant grip on the opponent's right leg, specifically on the upper thigh area, just above the knee.
    3. Drive your hips forward and slightly to your left, creating a strong base.
    4. Begin to extend your right leg, pushing your heel towards the opponent's hip.
    5. Simultaneously, elevate your left leg, hooking your foot behind the opponent's right knee.
    6. Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, pulling the collar grip towards your left shoulder.
    7. Use your left leg as a lever, driving your heel into the opponent's thigh to create separation and tension.
    8. Apply upward pressure with your right forearm against the opponent's neck and downward pressure with the collar grip to complete the choke.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke by simply pulling the collar with your arms: This can strain your shoulder joint (rotator cuff) and elbow ligaments due to excessive internal rotation and valgus stress.

    Correct: Focus on rotating your torso and using your hips and legs to generate power, keeping your arms controlled.

    • Over-extending your neck while trying to finish: This can lead to cervical spine strain or whiplash-like injuries.

    Correct: Keep your neck neutral and let the leverage of your body and the grips do the work.

    • Using your legs improperly to drive into the opponent: This can cause knee or hip joint hyperextension if not controlled.

    Correct: Maintain a slight bend in your knees and hips, using controlled extension to create leverage.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo positional drilling: Practice the body positioning and leg hooks without a partner. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner assisted grip and body movement drill: Partner remains passive, you practice the grip transitions and hip movement. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Controlled choke application drill: Partner allows you to secure the grips and make the initial body movements, but resists the final choke. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    4. Dynamic positional drilling: Partner actively defends slightly, forcing you to adjust your body mechanics to maintain position. (10 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    5. Live rolling with focus on submission attempts: Attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke during rolling, focusing on correct mechanics, not just finishing. (5 rounds, 90% resistance)
    6. Full live rolling: Integrate the Bow and Arrow Choke as a natural submission option. (10 rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established a dominant back-mount position with control of the opponent's hips.
    • When the opponent attempts to roll or escape, creating an opportunity to transition to the Bow and Arrow grip.
    • When the opponent exposes their neck by turning into your back-mount control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Shoulder Roll Escape: If the opponent initiates the choke, they can roll their shoulders forward and away from the pressure, creating space and relieving the compression.
    • Bridging and Turning In: The opponent can bridge their hips into the attacker and turn their head towards the side of the collar grip, breaking the angle and relieving pressure.
    • Arm Protection: The defender can attempt to bring their own arm across their neck to block the collar grip, creating a barrier and preventing the choke from fully engaging.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Bow and Arrow Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you grip the collar too shallowly and rely solely on your forearm strength. The correct grip involves sinking your hand deep into the collar, allowing your forearm to press into the side of the neck and your wrist to remain relatively straight. Ensure your fingers are inside the collar and your thumb is pointed downwards, creating a stable, powerful grip that minimizes wrist flexion.

    Q: How can I do the Bow and Arrow Choke effectively against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paramount. Focus on establishing a deep collar grip with your dominant hand and a strong pant grip with your other hand. Drive your hips forward aggressively and use your legs as levers by extending your choking leg and hooking their opposite knee. The key is to create a strong fulcrum with your body, allowing you to use their size against them by isolating their neck and generating choking pressure through torso rotation.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to the Bow and Arrow Choke from the back?

    The optimal time to transition is when the opponent attempts to escape or turn into you. As they try to create space or roll, their body alignment often exposes their neck. Look for moments when their hips are angled away from you or when they expose their collar. A common trigger is when they try to peel your hooks off; this is your cue to secure the Bow and Arrow grips and initiate the body mechanics.
