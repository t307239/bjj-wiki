Bow and Arrow Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Breakdown

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

This choke is typically applied from the back control position, aiming to constrict the carotid arteries and secure a submission.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength rather than leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key insight is understanding how to use your leg as a lever against their hip to create the necessary tension for the choke.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From back control, secure your opponent's far arm with your near-side arm's bicep. This is the 'Lapel Grip'.
    2. Reach your far-side arm across their body and secure their lapel with a deep grip, thumb inside. This is the 'Collar Grip'.
    3. Place your leg on the same side as your collar grip over their hip, creating a perpendicular angle to their spine. This is the 'Hip Hook'.
    4. Drive your chest into their back, slightly forward, to prevent them from turning into you.
    5. Extend your legs, pushing your hips away from them while simultaneously pulling your collar grip towards their neck.
    6. Your near-side arm (bicep grip) should remain tight, preventing them from posturing up or creating space.
    7. Rotate your hips to your side, using your leg hook to create a fulcrum and increase the choking pressure.
    8. The combination of the lapel pull, hip pressure, and leg extension compresses their airway and carotid arteries.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke by simply pulling the lapel with your arms while your hips are parallel to your opponent's. This can strain your shoulders and biceps without generating effective pressure, and risks hyperextension of the elbow if they resist strongly.
    • Instead, ensure your leg is hooked over their hip and your hips are angled, creating a fulcrum for leverage.
    • Trying to crank the choke with your arms while your opponent is facing you. This puts immense strain on your own neck and shoulders, and can lead to a herniated disc or severe muscle tear if they counter forcefully.
    • Maintain a strong chest-to-back connection and use your legs and hips for the primary driving and pulling mechanics.
    • Squeezing with your bicep grip on their arm too tightly before establishing the choke mechanics. This can lead to nerve impingement in their shoulder or arm, causing temporary numbness or pain.
    • Keep the bicep grip firm but not constrictive until the choking mechanics are fully engaged.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip sequence and leg placement on a dummy or mat, focusing on hip angle and leg hook. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the full motion from back control, focusing on the sequence of grips, hip angle, and leg extension. (20 reps, 25% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner resists slightly by trying to turn their head or create space. Focus on maintaining grips and hip position. (20 reps, 50% resistance).
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Partner actively defends by posturing up or trying to peel grips. Work on maintaining control and adjusting your mechanics. (15 reps, 75% resistance).
    5. Positional sparring (back control focus): Start from back control and attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke when the opportunity arises, allowing for more dynamic defense. (10 rounds, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Bow and Arrow Choke into your rolling, looking for the proper setup and timing. (Full rolling session, 100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is turtled or has their back exposed, allowing easy access to their lapel and neck.
    • When you have established a strong back control with hooks in, and they are attempting to escape by turning away.
    • When your opponent tries to bridge or shrimp away, creating an opening for you to secure the collar and hip hook.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • The "Turn-In" Defense: When the choke is being applied, the defender turns their chin towards the choking arm and drives their shoulder into the attacker's chest. This relieves pressure on the carotid artery and allows for a potential escape or counter-grapple.
    • The "Grip Break" Defense: The defender uses their free hand to reach under the attacker's collar grip and peel it off, or uses their other arm to create space by pushing against the attacker's bicep. This requires precise timing and mobility.
    • The "Bridge and Roll" Defense: The defender bridges their hips upwards powerfully while simultaneously rolling towards the side of the choking arm. This disrupts the attacker's base and can lead to a reversal or a scramble.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Bow and Arrow Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs if you are gripping the lapel too loosely or with a bent wrist. Ensure your grip is deep and your thumb is inside the lapel, creating a strong fist. Additionally, avoid 'death-gripping' the lapel; the choke's power comes from leverage and hip extension, not just raw grip strength. Relax your grip slightly once the mechanics are set to prevent unnecessary strain.

    Q: How can I apply the Bow and Arrow Choke against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Focus on securing a very deep collar grip and making sure your leg is hooked securely over their hip to create a solid fulcrum. Instead of trying to pull them down, imagine driving your hips into them while extending your legs, using your body weight and their momentum against them. A tight connection to their back is paraMount.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke from guard?

    The Bow and Arrow Choke is primarily a back-control submission and is rarely attempted directly from open or Closed Guard without a transition. A common scenario is transitioning from a failed sweep or guard pass attempt where you end up on your opponent's back momentarily. You might also see it attempted from a scramble after losing top position, where you manage to secure back control.
