The Bow and Arrow Choke is a powerful lapel choke initiated from the Side Control or mount position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the opponent's carotid arteries.

White belts often struggle by focusing on brute strength rather than leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key is to use your body as a fulcrum, creating a long lever with your arm and opponent's lapel to generate immense choking pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Entry Grip: From side control, secure a deep grip on the opponent's far side lapel with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel, fingers gripping the fabric.
    2. Secondary Grip: With your left hand, establish a grip on the opponent's pants near the knee on the same side as the lapel grip (e.g., right lapel, right pant leg).
    3. Hip Angle: Turn your hips towards the opponent's head, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and theirs. Your right hip should be lower than your left.
    4. Arm Extension: Extend your right arm fully, pulling the lapel taut across the opponent's neck. This creates the "bowstring" of the choke.
    5. Leg Drive: Drive your left knee forward, tucking it behind the opponent's far armpit. This prevents them from shrimping away and creates leverage.
    6. Body Rotation: Rotate your torso counter-clockwise, bringing your chest closer to the opponent's head. Your right shoulder should press into their neck.
    7. Choking Action: Simultaneously, push your hips forward and pull the lapel grip towards your own chest. This compresses the carotid artery on the side of their neck.
    8. Finishing Posture: Maintain a tight frame, keeping your hips heavy and your body connected to the opponent. Your left knee should remain anchored.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Arm Extension: Grabbing the lapel too loosely and not extending the right arm fully allows the opponent to escape. This can lead to a hyperextended elbow or shoulder injury if they resist incorrectly.
    • Jerking Motion: Attempting to finish with a sudden, jerky pull on the lapel instead of a controlled, sustained pressure. This can strain the rotator cuff muscles in the shoulder or cause a biceps tear.
    • Lifting Hips Prematurely: Raising your hips too high off the mat before establishing the leg drive and hip angle. This compromises your base, making you vulnerable to sweeps and potentially straining your lower back.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Lapel and Pant Grip Practice: Practice securing the grips on a dummy or a willing partner without any movement. 20 reps each side.
    2. Solo Hip Angle and Arm Extension: From a controlled position (e.g., knee-on-belly), practice the hip angle and arm extension, feeling the tension in the lapel. 15 reps each side.
    3. Partnered Static Position: Partner lies passively. You practice all the grip and body mechanics without any resistance. 10 reps each side.
    4. Light Resistance Drill (25%): Partner offers minimal resistance by trying to slightly turn into you. Focus on maintaining your frame and hip angle. 15 reps each side.
    5. Controlled Flow Drill (50%): Partner attempts to defend by turning their head or shrimping slightly. You focus on applying the choke mechanics smoothly. 10 reps each side.
    6. Live Rolling Application (75%): In live rolling, actively look for opportunities to set up the Bow and Arrow Choke, applying the mechanics with intent. 3-5 attempts per roll.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From Side Control: When you have established a strong cross-face and your opponent is turtled or trying to bridge.
    • From Mount: When the opponent attempts to escape by turning onto their side, exposing their lapel.
    • When Opponent Tries to Shrimp Away: If the opponent tries to create space by shrimping, you can transition to the Bow and Arrow setup.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Defend the Lapel Grip: The opponent should immediately try to peel your hand off the lapel grip or prevent you from getting a deep grip in the first place.
    • Turn into the Choke: The opponent can turn their head into the choke, making it harder to apply pressure to the carotid artery. This requires a strong neck and core.
    • Shrimp and Create Space: If the choke is not fully locked in, the opponent can try to shrimp their hips away, creating space to relieve pressure and potentially escape the hold.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right wrist hurt when I try to do the Bow and Arrow Choke?

    Your wrist pain likely stems from gripping the lapel too tightly with your fingers only, or from an unnatural wrist angle. Instead, aim for a strong, neutral grip by wrapping your fingers around the lapel fabric and letting the pressure come from your forearm and shoulder rotation, not just finger strength. Ensure your wrist stays straight and aligned with your forearm.

    Q: How can I use the Bow and Arrow Choke effectively against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paraMount. Focus on driving your hips forward and creating a strong base with your left knee. Extend your right arm fully to maximize the lever arm created by the lapel. Your body rotation and hip pressure are more important than trying to overpower them; think of it as a sophisticated lever system.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to a Bow and Arrow Choke from side control?

    The optimal time to transition is when your opponent exposes their lapel and is either Turtled or attempting to bridge/turn away. If they are flat on their back and you have a strong cross-face, they are less likely to give you the necessary space and angle. Look for moments of defensive movement from them to initiate the setup.
