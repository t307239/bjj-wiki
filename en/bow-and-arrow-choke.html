Bow and Arrow Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

This choke is typically initiated from the guard, aiming to isolate an opponent's neck and arm for a powerful submission. White belts often struggle with the coordination and leverage required, leading to inefficient attempts. The key to the Bow and Arrow Choke is understanding how to create a "bow" with your legs and a "string" with your arm grip to generate immense pressure. Mastering this technique requires precise body positioning and understanding the rotational forces involved.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a deep cross-collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel, palm facing upwards.
    2. With your left hand, establish a sleeve grip on the opponent's gi sleeve on the same side as your collar grip (e.g., right collar, right sleeve).
    3. Untuck your right leg from your opponent's guard and swing it over their shoulder, placing your foot on the mat for base.
    4. Simultaneously, extend your left leg, hooking your opponent's far hip with your foot, creating a lever.
    5. Rotate your hips to your right, driving your right knee towards your opponent's chest while keeping your left foot anchored on their hip.
    6. Pull down on the collar grip with your right arm and pull up on the sleeve grip with your left arm, creating a sawing motion.
    7. Arch your back and drive your hips forward, tightening the choke by compressing the carotid artery and jugular vein.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive shoulder pressure to the opponent's head: This can strain your own shoulder joint (rotator cuff ligaments) or cause hyperextension in the opponent's neck if not controlled. The correct movement involves isolating the neck with the arm and using hip drive for pressure.
    • Gripping too high on the collar or too low on the sleeve: This compromises the choke's effectiveness and can lead to your grip slipping, resulting in a loss of position or an arm injury (biceps tendon strain) for you. Always aim for the deep lapel grip and a firm sleeve grip.
    • Swinging your legs wildly without control: This can lead to your opponent easily posturing up or escaping, and can strain your hip flexors and lower back. Ensure your legs are placed with intention and create a stable base and lever.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo grip and body positioning drill: Practice the collar and sleeve grips, and the leg placement without a partner. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Static partner drill (no movement): Your partner lies flat, you practice the entire sequence of grips and body movements without them resisting. (5 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Light resistance drill (slow movements): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the mechanics of the choke setup and execution. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled resistance drill (focus on setup): Partner actively defends the setup, but not the choke itself. Focus on executing the correct grips and body transitions. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live drill with escape focus: Practice the choke setup against a resisting opponent who is actively trying to escape. Stop if the choke is not secured within 15 seconds. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    6. Full rolling simulation: Attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke in live rolling scenarios, focusing on proper setup and timing. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • From closed guard when the opponent postures up and creates space between your bodies.
    • When the opponent attempts to stack you from your guard, creating an opportunity to isolate their arm and neck.
    • When the opponent is on their hands and knees in front of you, and you have established a strong grip on their collar and sleeve.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Posture Up and Grip Release: If the opponent attempts the choke, immediately posture up by driving your hips down and forward, creating space to break their collar and sleeve grips. Simultaneously, bring your knees to your chest to defend.
    • Shoulder Walk and Escape: As the choke is being applied, walk your shoulders towards the opponent's hips and attempt to drive your hips away. This elongates their body and relieves pressure, allowing for an escape.
    • Hip Escape and Reguard: If you feel the choke tightening, perform a strong hip escape to your side and bring your legs back to a defensive guard position (e.g., closed guard or half guard).

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Bow and Arrow Choke, even if I don't tap my opponent?

    Your neck might hurt because you are applying pressure incorrectly. Instead of compressing the carotid artery and jugular vein on one side of the neck, you might be twisting your own neck or applying pressure unevenly with your forearm against their trachea. Ensure your collar grip is deep and your body is perpendicular to their neck, using hip drive to tighten.

    Q: How can I use the Bow and Arrow Choke effectively against a much larger and stronger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on getting a very deep collar grip and a secure sleeve grip. Use your legs to create a strong lever by hooking their far hip and driving your hips forward. Their size means they have more mass to resist, so a clean, tight setup is crucial to overcome their strength.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to a Bow and Arrow Choke from closed guard, and what if they defend my initial setup?

    The best time is when the opponent postures up, creating space. If they defend your initial setup by pulling their head down or posturing up too much, don't force it. Instead, use that moment to transition to another sweep or submission, like a triangle choke or Armbar, by re-establishing your guard.
