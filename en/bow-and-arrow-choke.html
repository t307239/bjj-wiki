This choke is typically applied from the Back Mount position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via carotid artery compression. It's a powerful submission that can be learned by beginners.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength instead of precise leverage, leading to inefficient application and potential injury. Understanding the body mechanics is paraMount for success.

The key mechanical insight is creating a fulcrum with your body and using your opponent's own structure against them, generating immense pressure with minimal effort.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the opponent's back with both hooks in place. Your chest should be firmly against their upper back.
    2. Establish a strong grip on the opponent's opposite side collar with your non-driving arm (e.g., if your right arm is driving, use your left hand for the collar).
    3. With your driving arm, reach *under* the opponent's armpit on the same side as your collar grip, and secure a deep grip on their pants (ideally the hip area or belt).
    4. Rotate your hips so they are perpendicular to your opponent's spine. Your hips should be lower than theirs.
    5. Begin to drive your hips forward and slightly upward, creating a pulling tension between your collar grip and your pants grip.
    6. Extend your legs and bridge your hips, creating a spinal extension and pulling your collar grip towards your opponent's head.
    7. Simultaneously, use your driving arm (which is gripping the pants) to pull their hips down and away, creating a significant shearing force that compresses the carotid artery.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to crank the neck by twisting your body: This can hyperextend the cervical spine, leading to neck strain or disc injury. The correct movement involves driving your hips and pulling the collar, not twisting.
    • Gripping the opponent's gi too loosely with the pants grip: This can cause the grip to slip, resulting in a loss of control and potentially an uncontrolled fall. Always ensure a deep, secure grip on the fabric.
    • Overextending your own back in an attempt to generate power: This can strain your lumbar spine. The power comes from hip drive and leverage, not from arching your back excessively.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip acquisition and body positioning without a partner. Focus on hip rotation and weight transfer. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence, focusing on precise grip placement and hip movement. (10 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Light resistance drilling: Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the mechanics of the choke. Focus on maintaining your structure. (5 reps, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled resistance drilling: Partner actively tries to defend slightly, forcing you to adjust your grips and angles. (5 reps, 50% resistance)
    5. Moderate resistance drilling: Partner actively defends, requiring you to apply the technique with proper leverage and timing. (5 reps, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke in a live rolling situation, focusing on recognizing opportunities and executing with proper mechanics. (Ongoing, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have secured both hooks and have control of your opponent's back.
    • When your opponent attempts to turn into you or is posturing up from bottom back mount.
    • When you have established a strong collar grip and your opponent is not actively defending their neck.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Shrimping" away and creating space: If the opponent attempts the choke, the defender can immediately shrimp their hips away, breaking the tight connection and making the choke ineffective. The defender should then try to establish a better defensive posture.
    • "Tucking the chin" and turning into the attacker: The defender can tuck their chin to their chest and attempt to turn their head into the side of the choke. This makes it harder for the attacker to isolate the carotid artery and can allow the defender to escape the position.
    • "Posting" the elbow and "bridging": The defender can try to post their elbow on the mat on the side of the choke. Then, they can bridge their hips into the attacker, disrupting their base and potentially creating an escape opportunity.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Bow and Arrow Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you grip the opponent's collar too deeply with your fingers extended straight, creating an acute angle at the wrist. To fix this, ensure you are gripping the gi fabric with your knuckles, not your fingertips, and allow a slight bend in your wrist. Focus on driving your hips and pulling with your forearm, not just your wrist.

    Q: How can I apply the Bow and Arrow Choke against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is even more critical. Focus on getting your hips lower than theirs, creating a strong fulcrum. Ensure your pants grip is deep and secure, and use your entire body's momentum to drive forward. Do not try to muscle through; instead, use their weight against them by controlling their hips and pulling their collar towards their head.

    Q: When is the best time to set up the Bow and Arrow Choke from the back?

    The Bow and Arrow Choke is most effective when your opponent is posturing up or trying to turn into you. If they are flat on their back, it's harder to generate the necessary leverage. Look for moments when they are actively trying to create space or improve their position, as this often opens up the angles needed for the choke.
