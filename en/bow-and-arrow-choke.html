This choke is typically applied from the Back Mount position, aiming to secure a submission by constricting the carotid arteries.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of precise control and understanding of leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The core principle is creating a powerful rotational force by anchoring one limb and pulling with the other, cinching the lapel against the neck.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From back mount, secure your opponent's far shoulder with your near arm (e.g., right arm secures opponent's left shoulder).
    2. Secure the opponent's same-side lapel (e.g., right lapel) with your far hand (e.g., left hand), gripping deep inside the collar.
    3. Place your feet on the opponent's hips, knees bent, creating a stable base with your hips slightly elevated.
    4. Drive your hips forward and to the side, creating a perpendicular angle relative to your opponent's torso.
    5. Extend your near leg (e.g., right leg) straight, pushing off the opponent's hip to create separation and leverage.
    6. Simultaneously, pull the lapel grip downwards and slightly across your opponent's body, while your near arm acts as a fulcrum.
    7. Rotate your torso away from the opponent, applying consistent pressure with the lapel into their carotid artery.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying the lapel grip too shallowly: This fails to create sufficient pressure on the carotid artery, offering no submission threat and potentially causing skin abrasion on the neck.
    • The correct grip is deep within the collar, allowing the fabric to press directly onto the artery.
    • Overextending your hips directly backward instead of to the side: This compromises your base and creates a straight-line pull, reducing the rotational leverage needed for the choke.
    • Your hips should drive forward and *then* to the side, establishing a strong perpendicular angle.
    • Using your arms alone to pull the lapel without hip and body rotation: This is inefficient and places undue strain on your shoulders and biceps, often resulting in a weak choke.
    • Integrate your entire body's weight and rotational power, using your legs to create the primary drive.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of grip placement and hip movement without a partner (10 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drilling: Secure the lapel and shoulder grips, practice hip drive and leg extension without applying choke pressure (10 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    3. Partner drilling: Apply the choke with minimal pressure, focusing on the body rotation and lapel cinching (10 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drilling: Increase pressure gradually, focusing on maintaining the correct body angle and leverage (10 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    5. Live rolling: Attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke in controlled sparring situations when the opportunity arises (5 attempts, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Bow and Arrow Choke into your regular sparring, aiming to finish the submission (10 attempts, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent has their back exposed and is turtled or attempting to escape forward.
    • When you have established a strong seatbelt grip or body triangle and can transition to back mount.
    • When your opponent attempts to defend a rear-naked choke by turning their head away.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Defensive Turn-in: If the lapel grip is not fully secured, the opponent can turn their head towards the choking arm, relieving pressure on the carotid artery.
    • Hand Fight and Grip Break: The opponent can actively fight the lapel grip with their free hand, attempting to peel the choking hand off the collar before the choke is fully applied.
    • Hip Escape and Base Recovery: If the attacker's hip drive is weak, the defender can attempt to shrimp their hips away and re-establish a base or turn into the attacker to relieve pressure.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Bow and Arrow Choke?

    Wrist pain often stems from an improper lapel grip or excessive reliance on wrist strength. If you are gripping the lapel too shallowly or with your fingers pointing straight out, you are putting the joint in a vulnerable position. Ensure your grip is deep within the collar and your forearm is aligned with the direction of pull, using your body's rotation rather than just your wrist strength.

    Q: How can I do the Bow and Arrow Choke against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paraMount. Focus on creating a significant perpendicular angle with your hips and driving your own weight into them. Use your legs to create separation and a strong fulcrum, ensuring your lapel grip is deep and the choke is cinched tight before initiating the rotational pull. Do not try to overpower them; out-leverage them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke from the back?

    The Bow and Arrow Choke is most effective when your opponent is turtled or actively trying to escape forward, exposing their back. It's also a great follow-up if your opponent defends a rear-naked choke by turning their head away. Avoid attempting it if your opponent is facing you or has strong Side Control established.
