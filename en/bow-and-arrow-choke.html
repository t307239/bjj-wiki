Bow and Arrow Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Bow and Arrow Choke is a powerful lapel choke typically initiated from the Closed Guard or sometimes from side control when transitioning to mount.

It aims to restrict blood flow to the carotid arteries by creating a fulcrum with the opponent's shoulder and an opposing pressure with the lapel.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage, opting for brute force instead of precise body mechanics.

The key insight is using your hips and back to generate the choking pressure, not just your arms, creating a fulcrum effect.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a high cross-collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside the lapel, fingers digging into the opponent's clavicular region.
    2. Secure a sleeve grip with your left hand on the opponent's same-side sleeve (right sleeve for right-handed choke), gripping the fabric firmly near the wrist.
    3. Shift your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's torso, ensuring your left hip is closer to their hip.
    4. Drive your left knee (same side as sleeve grip) across the opponent's body, aiming to pin their left shoulder blade to the mat with your knee pit.
    5. Extend your right leg, placing your right foot on the opponent's hip on the same side as your collar grip (their right hip).
    6. Arch your back, lifting your hips off the mat while simultaneously pulling the opponent's sleeve towards your chest with your left hand.
    7. Apply downward pressure with your right forearm across the opponent's collarbone, using your extended right leg as a brace against their hip to amplify the choking action.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Collar Grip: Grabbing too low on the collar or with fingers outside can lead to ineffective pressure and potential shoulder strain for the attacker.
    • Injury: Rotator cuff impingement or strain due to improper force vector.
    • Correct: Thumb inside the lapel, fingers deep and high on the clavicular region.
    • Over-Extending the Back: Arching the back excessively without proper hip elevation can hyperextend the lumbar spine.
    • Injury: Lumbar disc strain or muscle spasms.
    • Correct: Engage core, elevate hips, and use leg brace for leverage.
    • Neglecting the Sleeve Grip: Not securing a firm sleeve grip or letting it slip allows the opponent to create space and relieve pressure.
    • Injury: Wrist strain or sprain for the attacker if they rely solely on arm strength.
    • Correct: Firm grip near the opponent's wrist, pulling towards your chest.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip and Body Positioning: Practice establishing grips and hip angles without a partner. (10 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    2. Assisted Setup: Partner lies on their back, you practice the grip, hip angle, and knee placement. Partner offers no resistance. (5 reps per side, 0% resistance).
    3. Controlled Execution: Partner in closed guard. You execute the setup and choke mechanics, partner offers minimal, controlled resistance to the movement. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Flow Drilling: Practice transitioning into the Bow and Arrow Choke from closed guard, with partner offering light, predictable defense. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Situational Sparring: Start from a position where the Bow and Arrow Choke is viable (e.g., opponent in closed guard, you have a good cross-collar grip). Partner actively defends. (3 rounds, 75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling Application: Attempt the Bow and Arrow Choke during live rolling when the opportunity arises, focusing on technique over outcome. (5 rounds, 90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is in your closed guard and you have established a strong cross-collar grip.
    • When transitioning from side control to mount and you can secure the collar and sleeve grip.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you from closed guard, creating an opening for the choke.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Grip Break: If the collar grip is shallow, the defender can actively peel the attacker's hand off their collar. The attacker must secure the grip immediately or abandon the attempt.
    • Shrimping/Creating Space: The defender can shrimp their hips away to break the angle and relieve pressure on the neck. The attacker must maintain hip control and prevent this space creation.
    • Tucking Chin and Turning: The defender can tuck their chin tightly to their chest and attempt to turn their head towards the choking arm. The attacker must maintain control of the lapel and head position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Bow and Arrow Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you're relying too heavily on your forearm and wrist to generate the choking force, particularly with the sleeve grip. If your opponent is resisting by pulling their arm back, your wrist can get jammed. Ensure your main choking pressure comes from arching your back and driving your hips, using your leg as a brace, while the sleeve grip's primary function is to control their posture and prevent them from creating space, not to pull with maximum force.

    Q: How can I set up the Bow and Arrow Choke against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paraMount. Focus on securing the collar grip as high as possible on their neck and dig your fingers deep. Your sleeve grip needs to be very secure, controlling their arm's movement. The key is to use your hips to create the angle and the arching of your back to generate the leverage. Don't try to muscle them; instead, use their size against them by creating a tight fulcrum with your body and their shoulder.

    Q: When should I stop trying the Bow and Arrow Choke and switch to another technique?

    You should consider switching if your opponent successfully breaks your collar grip, creates significant space by shrimping their hips away, or if they are able to tuck their chin effectively and turn their head. If you are unable to establish the correct body angle (45 degrees) or if your leg brace on their hip is ineffective, it's also a sign to reassess. Continuing to force a poorly set up choke can lead to wasted energy and defensive openings.
