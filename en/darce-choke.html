The D'Arce Choke is a powerful submission typically initiated from the side-control or head-and-arm control positions, aiming to constrict blood flow to the brain.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of positional control and an over-reliance on raw strength rather than precise leverage.

The key insight is to use your opponent's own body weight and momentum against them by creating a tight, constricting grip and driving your shoulder into their carotid artery.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, secure a cross-face with your left arm, pinning their head to the mat. Your right arm reaches under their head, palm facing up.
    2. Interlace your fingers or gable grip your right hand around your left bicep, creating a "figure-four" grip.
    3. Drive your left shoulder into their neck, aiming for the right side of their carotid artery. Your head should be tight against their left shoulder.
    4. Angle your hips to your right, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's torso. This shifts your weight onto them.
    5. "Walk" your right elbow down towards their hip while simultaneously driving your left shoulder forward and slightly down.
    6. Maintain a tight grip by pulling your interlaced hands towards your chest, creating a "squeezing" motion.
    7. As you feel resistance, continue to drive your shoulder into their neck and slightly lift your hips off the mat, further tightening the choke.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "pulling hard" with your arms: This can strain your own biceps and shoulders, and often fails to generate sufficient choke pressure. The correct alternative is to use your body weight and shoulder pressure to constrict the artery.
    • Allowing your opponent to create space by "bridging" without proper hip control: This can hyperextend the opponent's neck or put excessive pressure on your own spine if you try to resist with brute force. The correct alternative is to maintain hip-to-hip connection and use a strong cross-face to prevent the bridge.
    • Gripping too loosely or incorrectly: A loose grip allows the opponent to escape the choke easily and can lead to your hand slipping, potentially causing wrist strain. The correct alternative is a tight figure-four grip with your forearm pressing directly into the carotid artery.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the shoulder drive and hip angle. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drilling with no resistance: Your partner lies flat on their back, allowing you to practice the entire sequence with no counter-pressure. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling with light resistance (25%): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the mechanics of the choke. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drilling with moderate resistance (50%): Your partner actively tries to defend, but not with full intensity. Focus on maintaining your position and grip. (5 reps per side)
    5. Partner drilling with high resistance (75%): Your partner defends with significant effort, simulating a more realistic scenario. Focus on finishing the choke despite their defense. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Attempt the D'Arce Choke during rolling, focusing on situational awareness and correct execution under pressure. Tap immediately if you are caught.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established solid side-control and the opponent is facing away from you.
    • When you achieve head-and-arm control (a "body triangle" from your guard can also lead to it).
    • When your opponent attempts to "bridge" or "shuck" your head-control, creating an opening for the arm-in position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Stacking": The opponent drives their weight directly onto your shoulders, flattening you out and relieving pressure. To counter, maintain hip-to-hip connection and drive your hips back slightly.
    • "Turning into the choke": The opponent turns their body towards the choking arm, creating space and weakening the constriction. To counter, maintain your shoulder pressure and adjust your hip angle to follow their turn.
    • "Postural Escape" (opponent stands up): The opponent uses their legs to stand up, attempting to break the choke. To counter, immediately transition to a "guard pass" or "take down" to maintain control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the D'Arce Choke?

    Wrist pain in the D'Arce Choke often stems from gripping too tightly with the palm and fingers, or from twisting your wrist unnaturally. Ensure your grip is a "figure-four" where your forearm is the primary driver, not just your wrist. Keep your wrist in a neutral position and focus on driving your shoulder into the opponent's neck while pulling your hands towards your chest, not twisting.

    Q: How can I D'Arce Choke a bigger opponent when I'm smaller?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on getting your shoulder deep into their carotid artery and securing a very tight grip. Utilize your hip angle aggressively to drive your weight onto them, making them feel your pressure. Do not try to "muscle" the submission; instead, use their size against them by creating a constricting "vice" with your arms and shoulder.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a D'Arce Choke during a BJJ match?

    The D'Arce Choke is most effective when the opponent is slightly Turtled or facing away from you, making it difficult for them to defend with their hands. It's also a great opportunistic submission when you have established head-and-arm control from side-control or mount, or when they attempt a "shuck" to escape your control. Avoid attempting it when they are facing you directly and can easily "stack" you.
