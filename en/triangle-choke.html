The Ultimate Triangle Choke BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Triangle Choke is a submission from the Closed Guard, designed to constrict blood flow to the brain by using your legs to create a "triangle" around the opponent's neck and one arm.

White belts often struggle because they focus on squeezing with their legs rather than creating the correct angles and pressure points, leading to a weak submission and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is to use your hips to drive *into* the opponent's shoulder, creating a fulcrum that cinches the choke, not just leg strength alone.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an over-under grip on the opponent's head and opposite arm (right hand grips opponent's left collar, left hand grips opponent's right arm).
    2. Shift your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to the opponent's torso. Your left hip should be elevated.
    3. Bring your right knee across your opponent's face, aiming to trap their head and left shoulder between your shin and hamstring.
    4. Release the collar grip, and immediately step your right foot over your opponent's left shoulder, bringing your right shin to the outside of their head.
    5. Secure your right foot with your left hand (forming the "lock"), ensuring your right shin is perpendicular to the mat.
    6. Drive your hips forward and slightly upward, pressing your right hamstring into the side of the opponent's neck and your left calf into the back of their shoulder.
    7. Tighten the choke by squeezing your knees together and slightly lifting your hips, creating pressure with your right shin against the carotid artery and your right thigh against the trapezius muscle.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly lifting your hips too high while your legs are not properly locked can hyperextend the opponent's neck, risking cervical spine injury.
    • Squeezing your knees together with excessive force before the triangle is properly formed can strain your own knee ligaments (MCL/LCL).
    • Trying to "pull down" with your arms to finish the choke, instead of driving hips forward, can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears in your own shoulder.
    • Failing to get your shin across the opponent's face and instead having it on their chest can lead to the opponent posturing up and escaping, potentially causing knee or ankle sprains if you are not anchored.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg movements from guard on the ground without a partner, focusing on hip angle and shin placement. (50 reps, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drilling (static): With a compliant partner lying on their back, practice entering the triangle from closed guard, focusing on the hip shift and leg placement. (25 reps per side, 10% resistance).
    3. Partner drilling (moving hips): Partner gently tries to posture up. You practice driving your hips forward to maintain the lock and leg pressure. (20 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    4. Partner drilling (basic control): Partner resists slightly by trying to prevent you from locking the triangle. Focus on securing grips and hip drive. (15 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    5. Controlled rolling: Roll with a partner who understands the technique and allows you to attempt it, providing minimal but realistic resistance to the entry and finish. (5 rounds, 75% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Triangle Choke in a live rolling situation against a resisting opponent. (10 minutes, 90-100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured up in your closed guard and you can isolate an arm.
    • When the opponent tries to pass your guard and you can catch them in transition with an arm extended.
    • When you have achieved a strong closed guard and the opponent is pressing their weight down, making it difficult for them to posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stacking": The opponent drives their weight forward, bringing their hips over your shoulders and attempting to crush your legs. To counter, drive your hips up and away, creating space to escape or transition.
    • "Cutting the Corner": The opponent attempts to step their leg out and drive their shoulder into your hip to break the triangle. To counter, maintain tightness, drive your hips forward, and use your free leg to push their head away.
    • "Arm Escape": The opponent pulls their trapped arm free by rotating their shoulder. To counter, secure a strong over-hook on their arm before initiating the triangle and maintain a tight grip on their head.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do a Triangle Choke from closed guard?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are trying to squeeze with your legs alone and not using your hips correctly. Instead of just squeezing, drive your hips forward and slightly up into the opponent's shoulder. This creates a fulcrum that cinches the choke by pressing your shin into their carotid artery and your thigh into their trapezius muscle, minimizing strain on your own neck.

    Q: How can I Triangle Choke a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, the mechanics are even more crucial. Focus on isolating one of their arms and creating a significant hip angle (around 45 degrees) before initiating the leg over the head. Drive your hips forward aggressively as you lock the triangle. The key is leverage and creating a tight 'V' shape with your legs, using your shin to press on their carotid and your thigh to control their shoulder, rather than relying on raw strength.

    Q: I can't seem to finish the Triangle Choke, my opponent just taps their knee. What am I doing wrong?

    Tapping their knee usually indicates they are feeling pressure, but not the choke itself. This means your triangle is likely not tight enough. Ensure your shin is perpendicular to the mat when locked, your hips are driving forward, and your knees are squeezed together. Also, check that you've trapped their shoulder with your calf; if their shoulder can escape, the choke won't be effective. Adjust your hip angle and leg positioning to create a more complete seal.
