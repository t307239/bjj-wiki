Triangle Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide to BJJ

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Triangle Choke is a submission from the guard, aiming to cut off blood flow to the brain by compressing the carotid arteries. It requires precise body positioning to be effective and safe.

White belts often struggle due to imprecise limb placement and a lack of understanding of the core mechanics, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key insight is using your own leg to pinch the opponent's arm and head together, creating a fulcrum with your hip and driving your shin across their neck.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an overhook on one of the opponent's arms with your arm, ensuring your bicep is pressed against their triceps. Maintain a tight grip on their opposite collar with your free hand.
    2. Begin to swing your hips to your side, bringing your legs up. Your hip on the side of the overhook should move away from the opponent's body.
    3. Swing the leg on the side of the overhook over the opponent's head, aiming to place your ankle on the outside of their shoulder blade on that same side.
    4. Bring your other leg across the opponent's back, trapping their trapped arm between your thighs. Your shin should cross your own ankle, forming a tight triangle shape.
    5. Lock your ankles together by gripping your own shin with your free hand (if possible) or by flexing your feet strongly inwards. This creates a rigid frame.
    6. Squeeze your knees together forcefully, while simultaneously driving your hips forward and slightly upwards. This compresses their neck.
    7. To finish, lean back slightly and drive your hips into their body. Keep your back straight and your core engaged, ensuring your opponent's trapped shoulder is pushed into their own neck.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'muscle' the choke by pulling with your arms and squeezing your knees without proper hip and leg positioning can strain your shoulder and elbow joints.
    • If your leg slides off the opponent's head prematurely, and you continue to drive your hips, you risk hyperextending your knee joint or tearing your ACL.
    • Forcing your opponent's head down into the choke while your hips are not aligned correctly can cause cervical spine strain or impingement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg positioning and hip movement without a partner. Focus on locking the triangle shape securely. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice entering the triangle from closed guard, focusing on the exact grip and hip movement. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Your partner offers light resistance to your attempts to swing your leg over and lock the triangle. Focus on maintaining control. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Your partner attempts to posture up or step out. You focus on adjusting your hips and leg pressure to maintain the triangle. (5 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Your partner actively defends by posturing or trying to pass your legs. You work on finishing the choke or transitioning if the opportunity is lost. (3 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Apply the triangle choke in a sparring situation, focusing on timing and execution against an opponent actively defending. (3 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured down in your closed guard, offering their head and arm within reach.
    • When the opponent attempts a guard pass and their weight is distributed forward, allowing you to trap an arm.
    • When you have secured a strong overhook and are able to isolate one of their arms.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Defense 1 (Head and Arm): If the opponent tries to straighten their posture and escape, immediately drive your hips forward and squeeze your legs. If they manage to pull their head out, transition to an armbar by extending your legs.
    • Defense 2 (Hip Escape): If the opponent stacks you, hip escape to your side to relieve pressure and adjust your leg position. If you can't reset, be ready to release and re-establish guard.
    • Defense 3 (Counter-Pass): If the opponent attempts to pass by stepping over your legs, maintain the triangle grip and use your free hand to pummel your leg through their legs, creating space to re-guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do a Triangle Choke?

    Neck pain often occurs when you are trying to force the choke by squeezing your legs without properly engaging your hips. The compression should come from your thigh pinching their carotid artery against their own shoulder, with your hips driving forward to complete the seal. Ensure your shin is across their neck, not just their jaw, and that their trapped shoulder is wedged into their neck.

    Q: How can I Triangle Choke a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, precise hip and leg positioning is paraMount. Focus on getting your leg high over their shoulder and trapping their arm tightly. Use your hips to create an angle, driving them forward to maximize the pinch. Don't try to muscle it; leverage is key. Ensure your ankles are locked tightly to prevent them from posturing up and creating space.

    Q: Why can't I finish my Triangle Choke even when it feels tight?

    A common reason for an incomplete choke is insufficient hip pressure and an open triangle. Ensure your hips are driving forward into your opponent's body, not just squeezing your knees. Your shin should be pressing across their carotid artery, and their own shoulder should be acting as the second point of pressure. Lock your ankles firmly to maintain the structural integrity of the triangle.
