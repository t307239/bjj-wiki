Triangle Choke Technique for BJJ White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Triangle Choke is a submission that uses your legs to cut off blood flow to the opponent's brain, typically initiated from Closed Guard. It's a powerful tool for submitting from a dominant position.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute strength rather than precise body mechanics. This leads to wasted energy and missed opportunities.

The key insight is creating a tight "figure four" with your legs, using your shin to press on the carotid artery and your ankle to lock off the other side.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure one of the opponent's arms by gripping their wrist with your same-side hand (e.g., left hand grips opponent's right wrist).
    2. Simultaneously, use your other hand to grab the opponent's collar on the same side as the trapped arm, pulling it towards you.
    3. Swing your leg (same side as trapped arm) up and over the opponent's shoulder, aiming to get your shin across their neck.
    4. Your hip on the side of the trapped arm should angle away from the opponent, creating space to bring your other leg across.
    5. Bring your second leg (the one not on the neck) across the opponent's back, connecting your ankle to the back of your knee on the first leg, forming a "figure four" lock.
    6. Ensure your shin of the first leg is directly across the opponent's carotid artery, not their windpipe. Your heel should be tucked towards your own hip.
    7. With your legs locked, sit up slightly and drive your hips forward. Use your free arm to push the opponent's trapped arm across their body, deepening the choke.
    8. To finish, contract your leg muscles and squeeze your knees together while continuing to drive your hips forward. The pressure on the carotid artery will cause the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke with your shin across the windpipe instead of the carotid artery can cause choking on air, leading to an ineffective submission and potential neck injury from pressure on the trachea.
    • Trying to "squeeze" the choke with only leg strength without proper hip engagement can overextend your knee and ankle joints, risking ligamentous tears in your knee or ankle.
    • Allowing the opponent to stack you heavily while setting up the triangle, without maintaining a stable base and hip angle, can lead to lower back compression or disc injury.
    • Grabbing the opponent's gi with your free hand instead of controlling their trapped arm can allow them to escape the choke by posturing up.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg movement and "figure four" lock on your own without a partner. Focus on the hip angle and leg placement. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from closed guard to locked triangle, focusing on precise grip and body positioning. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drilling (25% resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to arm control and posture. Focus on maintaining your angles and securing the lock. (15 reps)
    4. Partner drilling (50% resistance): Partner attempts to defend by posturing up or shrugging their shoulders. Practice maintaining the setup and adjusting your position. (10 reps)
    5. Partner drilling (75% resistance): Partner actively defends and attempts to escape. Focus on keeping the choke tight and finishing. (5 reps)
    6. Live rolling (90% intensity): Attempt the triangle choke in a rolling scenario. Prioritize correct technique over immediate submission. (2 rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured down in your closed guard, creating a good angle.
    • When the opponent tries to stack you from closed guard, and you can use their weight to help you swing your leg over.
    • When the opponent tries to pass your guard and leaves an arm exposed in a position where you can isolate it.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stacking defense: The opponent straightens their posture and drives their weight forward, creating space and relieving pressure on their neck. They then look to pass your guard or reset.
    • "Hanging" defense: The opponent places their weight on your hips, preventing you from sitting up and driving your hips forward. They might then try to walk their legs out of the choke.
    • "Knee-slide" defense: The opponent uses their free arm to push your thigh away and slides their knee across your hips, breaking the lock and opening your guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt and not my opponent when I try the Triangle Choke?

    This usually happens when you are placing your shin across the opponent's windpipe instead of their carotid artery. The carotid arteries are located on the sides of the neck. Ensure your shin is directly on the side of their neck and your ankle is locked behind their knee, with your hips driving forward to create the squeeze.

    Q: How can I Triangle Choke a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a tight "figure four" lock and using your hips to drive forward. Don't let them stack you; instead, use their weight to your advantage by angling your hips away. Securing a strong grip on their collar and pulling it across their body is crucial for leverage.

    Q: When should I abandon a Triangle Choke attempt and what is the safest way to do so?

    You should abandon the attempt if the opponent has successfully defended by stacking you heavily, or if they have managed to free their trapped arm and are posturing up significantly. To disengage safely, break your leg lock by extending your leg, then bring your knee back towards your chest to regain guard or transition to another submission.
