Triangle Choke: The Essential BJJ Submission for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Triangle Choke is a submission initiated from guard positions, aiming to constrict blood flow to the brain by pressing the opponent's own shoulder into their carotid artery.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or improper angles, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Understanding the mechanics is key.

The core principle is using your legs to create a "figure-four" lock around the opponent's neck and arm, while your hips drive forward to cinch the choke.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure your opponent's posture by controlling their sleeves with your hands, palms facing up, gripping the fabric just above the elbow.
    2. Lift your hips slightly and swing one leg up and over your opponent's shoulder, aiming to place the back of your knee on their neck.
    3. As your leg crosses, immediately bring your other leg across your shin, creating a figure-four grip with your ankles locked or your heel cupping your knee.
    4. Adjust your hips so they are perpendicular to your opponent's torso, creating a strong base and preventing them from posturing up.
    5. Drive your hips forward and slightly upward, simultaneously pulling your opponent's arm across their own neck, ensuring it contacts their carotid artery.
    6. Squeeze your knees together, driving your shin into the back of your opponent's neck and your hamstring into their trapped arm's bicep.
    7. Maintain a tight head and arm control by cupping the back of their head with your free hand and keeping their trapped arm pinned to their body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect hip angle: Trying to finish the choke with hips parallel to the opponent's torso allows them to escape by posturing up, potentially leading to shoulder impingement if you over-rotate.
    • Over-extending the leg: Pushing the leg too far past the shoulder can strain the hamstring and hip flexor, and compromise the choke's effectiveness.
    • Forcing the lock with arms: Relying on arm strength to pull the opponent's head down instead of driving hips forward can lead to shoulder joint strain and ineffective choke.
    • Ignoring opponent's posture: Not actively preventing the opponent from posturing up by driving hips and controlling their head allows them to relieve pressure, potentially causing neck strain for you.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg swing, figure-four lock, and hip drive motion without a partner. 10 repetitions per side.
    2. Partner assisted drilling (0% resistance): Opponent remains completely still. Focus on executing each biomechanical step precisely. 5 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner assisted drilling (25% resistance): Opponent offers minimal resistance, allowing you to focus on hip movement and leg positioning. 5 repetitions per side.
    4. Partner assisted drilling (50% resistance): Opponent begins to slightly resist, simulating a real scenario without full pressure. Focus on maintaining control. 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Controlled sparring (75% resistance): Engage in light sparring, specifically looking for opportunities to initiate the Triangle Choke. Focus on correct setup and finish. 3 rounds, 3 minutes each.
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Apply the Triangle Choke in a full sparring match, understanding that opponents will actively defend. Focus on adapting and finishing.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up and you have them in closed guard.
    • When you successfully isolate one of your opponent's arms while they are attempting to pass your guard.
    • When you have transitioned to a position like side control and can attack from a modified guard or by bringing your legs over.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stacking Pass Defense: Opponent stacks their weight onto your shoulders, attempting to break your guard and flatten you out. You must maintain hip pressure and keep your hips perpendicular to their torso.
    • Arm Release: Opponent pulls their trapped arm out of the choke by lifting their elbow and stepping their arm out. You counter by driving your hips forward and cinching the choke tighter before they escape.
    • Posture Up: Opponent regains upright posture, breaking your leg control. You counter by driving your hips forward aggressively and securing their head with your hands to prevent them from straightening up.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do a Triangle Choke from closed guard?

    Your neck pain likely stems from trying to force the choke using your arms instead of your legs and hips. When you don't drive your hips forward and create the correct "figure-four" lock, you end up pulling your opponent's head down with your hands, putting direct pressure on your own neck or their upper spine. Focus on the hip drive and squeezing your knees together to finish the choke, which will compress their carotid artery, not your neck.

    Q: How can I Triangle Choke a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, precision is paramount. Ensure you secure their posture tightly in Closed Guard before initiating the Triangle. When you swing your leg over, prioritize getting the back of your knee high on their neck, and immediately lock your ankles or heel. Drive your hips forward forcefully and use their own arm to complete the choke; their size will actually help cinch the choke tighter once the lock is secure.

    Q: Why does my Triangle Choke feel loose and my opponent can easily escape?

    A loose Triangle Choke often means your hips are not positioned correctly. You need to angle your hips so they are perpendicular to your opponent's torso, creating a strong base. If your hips are parallel, they can simply posture up and break the lock. Also, ensure you are actively driving your hips forward and squeezing your knees together, using your hamstrings and shins to apply pressure, rather than just holding the position.
