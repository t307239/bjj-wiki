Triangle Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Triangle Choke is typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the opponent's brain. White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage and body positioning.

The key insight is to use your legs to create a "figure four" around the opponent's neck and arm, then use your hips to drive into the choke.

This technique relies on precise angles and pressure, not brute strength, making it accessible even for smaller practitioners.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure an over-under-hook on one of the opponent's arms with your same-side arm. Your other arm should grip the opponent's far collar.
    2. Swing your leg that is on the same side as the over-hook over the opponent's shoulder, aiming to bring your shin across their neck.
    3. Simultaneously, bring your other leg up and lock it over your ankle of the first leg, creating a "figure four" grip with your legs around the opponent's head and the trapped arm.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly to the side of the trapped arm, ensuring your shin presses into the opponent's carotid artery.
    5. Extend your hips upwards and slightly arch your back, while simultaneously pulling down on the opponent's head with your collar grip.
    6. Ensure the trapped arm's bicep is pressed against the opponent's own neck, further tightening the choke.
    7. Keep your back flat on the mat and your hips elevated, creating a stable base for the choke to be effective.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly swinging your leg over the opponent's head can lead to your knee hyperextending, potentially damaging the ACL or PCL.
    • The correct movement involves a controlled swing, keeping your knee bent and aligning your shin across their neck.
    • Failing to properly lock your legs can result in your opponent escaping or, worse, you losing balance and falling awkwardly, risking a shoulder or neck injury.
    • Always ensure your legs are securely locked in the "figure four" before applying pressure, and maintain a stable base.
    • Trying to "crank" the choke with your arms instead of using hip pressure can strain your shoulders and elbows.
    • Focus on driving your hips forward and upward, using your legs to constrict, rather than relying on arm strength.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the leg "figure four" lock and hip drive motion without a partner, focusing on smooth transitions. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice setting the triangle from closed guard, focusing on correct grip and lock. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner attempts to posture up slightly. Practice maintaining the lock and driving hips. (10 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend by posturing. Focus on maintaining tightness and hip pressure. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner attempts to stack or pass. Practice holding the triangle and adjusting angles. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the triangle choke when the opportunity arises, focusing on correct execution under light pressure. (3-5 attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up inside your closed guard, providing an opportunity to isolate an arm.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and you can trap an arm in the process.
    • When you have good control of your opponent's upper body from closed guard, limiting their ability to escape.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stack Pass Defense" (Opponent's Response): The opponent drives their weight forward, attempting to stack you and break the lock. To counter, you must keep your hips elevated and continue driving them away, or switch to an armbar.
    • "Head and Arm Release" (Opponent's Response): The opponent pulls their head and arm out of the choke. To counter, immediately re-establish the lock, or transition to a sweep or other submission if the opportunity presents itself.
    • "Knee Slide Pass Defense" (Opponent's Response): The opponent attempts to slide their knee past your hips. To counter, maintain a tight guard, bring your knees to your chest, and use your legs to prevent their knee from getting past your hips.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to do the Triangle Choke?

    Neck pain often arises from trying to force the choke with your arms or by not positioning your shin correctly. Ensure your shin is directly across the carotid artery, not the windpipe, and that your hips are driving forward to create the compression. Avoid pulling down on their head with excessive force; let your leg pressure do the work.

    Q: How can I Triangle Choke a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on getting the "figure four" lock extremely tight and ensuring their trapped arm's bicep is pressing into their own neck. Drive your hips forward with maximum power and keep your back flat; your legs are your primary tools, not your upper body strength.

    Q: Why can't I finish the Triangle Choke, my opponent just escapes?

    A common reason for failure is not having the "figure four" lock tight enough or not driving your hips forward. If the opponent's arm is not properly secured, they can often pull it out. Also, ensure you are not leaving too much space between your knees and their body; maintain a compact, controlled structure.
