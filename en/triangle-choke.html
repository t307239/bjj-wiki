Triangle Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Triangle Choke is typically initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the opponent's brain by compressing the carotid arteries with your leg.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of the leverage points, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is using your shin to drive into the opponent's neck while your opposite hamstring squeezes their shoulder, creating a vise-like effect.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish control: grip opponent's left sleeve with your right hand and their right collar with your left hand.
    2. Untuck your right leg and swing it over the opponent's head, aiming to place your right shin across their neck.
    3. At the same time, pull the opponent's head down and to your left with your collar grip, while their left arm is trapped across their own chest by your right leg's initial placement.
    4. Bring your right foot to meet your left knee, creating a closed triangle shape with your legs, with your right shin firmly against the opponent's neck.
    5. Rotate your hips to your left, angling your body so your left hip is off the mat, creating a 45-degree angle relative to the opponent's torso.
    6. Squeeze your right hamstring (behind the opponent's head) against their left shoulder, and push your left knee forward with your left hand to enhance the compression.
    7. Ensure your right shin is flat across the carotid artery, not digging into the windpipe, and your left foot is behind your right knee to lock the triangle securely.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly placing the shin across the throat instead of the carotid artery: This can damage the trachea, leading to a collapsed airway and breathing difficulties.
    • Applying pressure with the knee joint directly to the opponent's head: This can cause knee hyperextension or ligament damage to your own knee, and also puts undue pressure on the opponent's neck structure.
    • Trying to "crank" the choke with arm strength alone without hip rotation: This ineffective method can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears in the attacker, and doesn't effectively cut off blood flow.
    • Failing to secure the opponent's arm across their own chest: This allows the opponent to posture up and escape the choke, potentially leading to a loss of position or a counter-submission.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice swinging your leg over the head and forming the triangle shape with your legs, focusing on hip rotation and shin placement. (50 reps per side)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Partner lies on their back. You practice setting up the triangle from closed guard, focusing on grip acquisition and leg placement. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner allows you to set up the triangle and applies minimal pressure to resist. Focus on completing the lock and squeezing. (30 reps)
    4. Partner drill (controlled): Partner attempts to defend slightly. You work on maintaining the triangle and applying pressure, focusing on the correct hip angle and hamstring squeeze. (40 reps)
    5. Live rolling (limited): Focus exclusively on attacking the triangle choke from closed guard. Your partner can defend normally but should not actively try to pass or submit you. (3 rounds @ 50% intensity)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the triangle choke whenever the opportunity arises from closed guard. (5 rounds @ 75% intensity)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent is postured down in your closed guard, creating an opening for your leg to swing over their head.
    • When the opponent is attempting to pass your guard and their head is within reach of your legs.
    • When you have successfully isolated one of the opponent's arms and they are attempting to posture up, creating a "trap" scenario.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stacking": The opponent straightens their body and drives their weight forward, attempting to relieve pressure on their neck by compressing their own chest. To counter, maintain hip angle and drive your hips towards their shoulder.
    • "Smashing" the triangle: The opponent attempts to pass your guard by driving their head down and forward, breaking the triangle. To counter, maintain tight control of the opponent's arm and head, and continue to squeeze.
    • "Stepping out" of the triangle: The opponent attempts to free their head by stepping their head out of the triangle. To counter, fall to your side and bring your heel to their hip, re-establishing the angle.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt and not my opponent when I try a Triangle Choke?

    This often happens when you're gripping the opponent's collar too high or placing your shin across their windpipe instead of their carotid artery. Ensure your shin is flat against the side of their neck and your opposite hamstring is pressing into their shoulder. The pressure should come from the squeeze of your hamstrings and the angle of your hips, not direct neck pressure from your shin.

    Q: How can I Triangle Choke a much larger and stronger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, precise mechanics are crucial. Focus on trapping their arm *across their chest* before swinging your leg over. Use your hips to create a sharp angle (45 degrees) away from their posture. Squeeze your hamstring into their shoulder while driving your knee forward with your free hand; this leverage is more important than brute strength.

    Q: I keep losing the Triangle Choke setup when my opponent tries to stand up. What am I doing wrong?

    You're likely not establishing a strong enough grip on their collar and sleeve, or you're not committing to the hip angle. As soon as you swing your leg over, pull their head down with your collar grip and immediately rotate your hips to your side. This hip rotation is key to preventing them from posturing up and breaking the triangle.
