Your First Steps in Closed Guard BJJ

WHITE★☆☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Closed guard is a fundamental BJJ position where you control your opponent between your legs, typically from your back. It allows for offensive opportunities like sweeps and submissions while offering a degree of defensive security.

Many white belts struggle because they treat it like a static hug, lacking the necessary hip and leg engagement. This leads to them being easily passed or losing control.

The key is to create a strong, flexible frame with your legs, using your hips to generate angles and pressure, not just holding tight. This allows you to dictate distance and create openings.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start from your back, knees bent, feet flat on the mat or slightly elevated.
    2. Bring your opponent's hips close to yours, ensuring your knees are inside their armpits.
    3. Secure a "double-sleeve grip" by gripping both of your opponent's sleeves at the forearm, just above the wrist bone.
    4. Interlock your ankles behind your opponent's back, crossing them firmly, to create the "closed guard" structure.
    5. Arch your lower back slightly, lifting your hips off the mat to create space for leg movement and to drive your knees towards their chest.
    6. Use your feet on their hips or biceps (depending on the situation) to push and pull, controlling distance and creating angles.
    7. When attempting a sweep or submission, shift your weight to one side by driving that hip into the mat, creating an off-balance moment for your opponent.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too tightly on the sleeves with a straight wrist: This can lead to wrist hyperextension, damaging the carpal ligaments. Always maintain a slight bend in your wrist, allowing your forearm muscles to absorb pressure.
    • Allowing opponent to stack you by not maintaining hip elevation: This compresses your spine and can cause lumbar strain. Actively drive your hips upwards, keeping your glutes engaged to maintain spinal neutral.
    • Letting your knees drift too wide apart when an opponent is postured up: This compromises your guard's integrity and opens up your hips for passing. Keep your knees relatively close together, using your inner thighs to maintain pressure and control.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drill: Practice closing your guard from a supine position, focusing on the hip lift and ankle lock. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo Drill: Practice maintaining closed guard while an imaginary opponent attempts to posture up, focusing on hip control and maintaining leg tightness. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner Drill: With a compliant partner, practice securing closed guard from a neutral position, focusing on the double-sleeve grip and ankle lock. (10 rounds of 30 seconds, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner Drill: Practice maintaining closed guard as the partner attempts to posture up, focusing on hip elevation and preventing them from breaking your guard. (10 rounds of 30 seconds, 50% resistance)
    5. Partner Drill: Introduce simple sweeps like the scissor sweep, with the partner offering minimal resistance to your sweep attempt. (10 rounds of 30 seconds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling: Apply closed guard in a controlled rolling session, focusing on maintaining control and looking for sweep or submission opportunities. (3 rounds of 5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is on their knees or in a low stance, attempting to pass.
    • When you have successfully prevented an opponent from establishing a dominant passing position.
    • When an opponent is trying to stand up from a position where you can control their legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Posture Control: Maintain your hips elevated and knees tight to your opponent's chest, preventing them from posturing up to create passing angles. Use your forearms on their biceps and your legs to create a strong frame.
    • Hip Escape (Shrimping): If your opponent is too close and pressuring your guard, shrimp your hips out to the side to create space and re-establish your leg control, often leading to sweep opportunities.
    • Guard Retention Frame: When an opponent tries to break your guard by pushing your knees apart, immediately bring your forearms to their hips or biceps and use your legs to create a strong, inward-pulling frame to maintain the guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my opponent always break my closed guard so easily?

    Your opponent likely breaks your guard because you are not actively using your hips and legs to create a dynamic frame. Instead of just holding their arms, focus on actively pushing your knees towards their chest and arching your lower back to lift your hips. This creates a powerful lever system that makes it much harder for them to posture up or move their hips away.

    Q: How can I use closed guard effectively against a much larger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your goal is to minimize their ability to use their weight advantage. Focus on keeping your knees tightly hugged to their hips, preventing them from establishing a strong base or posturing up. Use your legs to control the distance and angles, and look for opportunities to off-balance them with precise hip movements for sweeps rather than relying on strength for submissions.

    Q: When should I transition from closed guard to another position?

    You should consider transitioning when your opponent has successfully neutralized your primary offensive threats from closed guard, such as by significantly posturing up or establishing a strong base that resists your sweeps. If they are consistently preventing you from creating angles or are defending your submission attempts effectively, it's time to explore options like opening your guard for an open guard position or attempting a sweep to achieve a dominant top position.
