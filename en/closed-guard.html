Closed Guard: The Foundation of BJJ Control

WHITE★☆☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Closed Guard is a fundamental BJJ position where you secure your opponent between your legs, creating a strong frame. It allows for control, submission opportunities, and sweeping from your back.

White belts often struggle because they view it passively, failing to actively engage their hips and core. This leads to being easily postured up or passed.

The key is to use your legs and hips to create constant pressure and angles, denying your opponent a stable base and posture.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Initial Setup: From your back, bring your knees towards your chest, ankles crossed behind your opponent's hips. Your shins should be pressed against their outer thighs.
    2. Secure the Lock: Once your ankles are crossed, squeeze your knees together and pull your heels towards your glutes. This creates a strong, tight "lock" around their hips.
    3. Grip Control: Establish grips on your opponent's collar (lapel grip with both hands, or one lapel and one sleeve grip) or their biceps. These grips are crucial for breaking their posture.
    4. Hip Escape (Shrimping): To create space and angles, perform a hip escape by pushing off the mat with your free foot and driving your hips to one side, creating a slight angle.
    5. Frame Creation: Use your forearms and elbows to maintain a frame against your opponent's hips and biceps. This prevents them from posturing up and stacking you.
    6. Weight Distribution: Keep your weight centered on your hips, not your shoulders. Your lower back should be on the mat; avoid lifting your hips too high unless setting up a sweep.
    7. Leg Pressure: Continuously apply inward pressure with your knees and squeeze your glutes. This pressure makes it uncomfortable for your opponent to maintain their base and posture.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Hyperextension: Trying to "lock" the guard too wide with straight legs, especially when your opponent is heavy and pushing down, can cause hyperextension of the knee joint, leading to ligament tears (ACL, MCL).
    • Correction: Always maintain a slight bend in your knees and focus on squeezing your glutes to achieve the lock, not just leg extension.
    • Low Back Strain: Arching your back excessively to try and lift your opponent, or having your hips too far away from your opponent's base, puts immense strain on the lumbar spine.
    • Correction: Keep your hips connected to your opponent's hips as much as possible and use your core to generate power for sweeps, not just your back.
    • Wrist and Elbow Injury: Gripping too tightly for extended periods with straight wrists, or allowing your opponent to apply pressure to your bent elbow in an unstable position, can lead to carpal tunnel syndrome or elbow tendonitis.
    • Correction: Maintain a neutral wrist and use your shoulder and core to generate pulling power, not just your grip strength.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Guard Closing: From a seated position on the mat, practice bringing your knees to your chest, crossing your ankles, and squeezing your legs shut. Focus on the feeling of the lock. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Static Guard Retention: Have a partner sit passively in front of you while you're in Closed Guard. Practice maintaining the lock, adjusting grips, and performing small hip escapes. (2 minutes, 25% resistance)
    3. Posture Break Drill: With your partner sitting, practice breaking their posture using your grips and pulling them forward. Focus on the timing of the pull and hip movement. (30 reps, 50% resistance)
    4. Basic Sweep Setup (Scissors Sweep): From Closed Guard, practice setting up and executing a scissor sweep against a passively resisting partner. Focus on the leg and hip mechanics. (20 reps per side, 60% resistance)
    5. Guard Retention Against Light Pressure: Your partner now applies light pressure to try and open your guard. Focus on maintaining your lock and hip position. (3 minutes, 70% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling - Closed Guard Focus: Engage in light rolling with a partner, specifically focusing on maintaining and attacking from Closed Guard. Your partner will actively try to pass. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard by stepping their legs wide.
    • When your opponent tries to establish heavy pressure and "stack" you.
    • When your opponent is in your guard and you have established control of their upper body.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Pass (e.g., Torreando Pass): If your opponent is too far away and doesn't have strong control, they may attempt to step around your legs. You can counter by framing with your arms and hips to create space and either re-guard or transition to another position.
    • Stack Pass: If your opponent tries to stack you by driving their weight forward, use your grips to pull them off-balance to the side and shrimp your hips out to create space or attempt a sweep.
    • Knee-to-Elbow Guard Pass: If your opponent tries to bring their knee inside your guard, focus on keeping your legs tight and using your shin to block their knee. You can then use your grips to pull them off-balance and set up a sweep or submission.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do Closed Guard in BJJ?

    Wrist pain in Closed Guard often stems from gripping too tightly with a hyperextended or flexed wrist. When you grip the lapel or sleeve, ensure your wrist is neutral, not bent at an extreme angle. Also, focus on using your shoulder and core to pull your opponent forward, rather than relying solely on grip strength. This reduces the strain on your wrists.

    Q: How can I effectively use Closed Guard against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, your Closed Guard becomes about control and leverage, not brute strength. Focus on keeping your legs tight around their hips, always maintaining that "lock." Use your grips to break their posture and create angles by shrimping your hips. Your goal is to make them uncomfortable and off-balance, setting up sweeps like the scissor sweep or transitioning to submissions that exploit their size, like a Triangle Choke.

    Q: Is it okay to let my opponent posture up a little in my Closed Guard?

    No, it is never ideal to let your opponent posture up significantly in your Closed Guard. When they gain good posture, they gain leverage and control, making it much harder for you to attack or sweep. You must actively work to break their posture by pulling them forward with your grips and using your hips to create angles. The tighter your guard and the more you control their posture, the more effective your attacks will be.
