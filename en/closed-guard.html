Closed Guard: Your First BJJ Foundation

WHITE★☆☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Closed Guard starts from a seated position with your opponent between your legs, aiming to control their posture and create submission opportunities.

Many white belts struggle by being too rigid or relying on brute strength, neglecting the subtle body mechanics needed for effective control.

The key is using your legs as a vise and your hips as a base to break your opponent's posture and dictate the engagement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Sit up with your knees bent at approximately 90 degrees, feet flat on the mat or hooked behind your opponent's hips.
    2. Secure a double-sleeve grip, placing your forearms inside your opponent's arms and gripping their sleeves near the elbow.
    3. Alternatively, secure a collar grip with your right hand on their left collar and your left hand on their right collar, fingers inside.
    4. Pull your opponent's arms towards your chest, using your elbows to frame against their biceps, creating a tight connection.
    5. Scoot your hips back slightly, tilting your pelvis forward to create a stable base and break their forward posture.
    6. Maintain constant pressure with your knees squeezing inward and your heels driving towards your glutes.
    7. Keep your spine relatively straight, avoiding excessive arching or rounding, to maintain a strong, compact frame.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too high on the sleeve (near the wrist): This allows your opponent to easily straighten their arms, breaking your frame and potentially injuring your elbow or shoulder ligaments due to hyperextension.
    • Pushing with your hands instead of pulling with your arms: This is biomechanically inefficient and can lead to your opponent easily shucking your arms off, opening you up to passes.
    • Allowing your opponent to flatten your back to the mat: This removes your hip base and makes it impossible to control their posture or create angles, increasing the risk of spinal compression injuries.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice closing and opening your guard, focusing on the hip movement and leg squeeze. 10 reps each side.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Your partner stands, you close guard. Practice securing double-sleeve grips and pulling them down. 10 reps.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to your grips and posture control. Focus on maintaining the connection. 5 reps.
    4. Guard retention drill: Partner attempts to open your guard with controlled pressure. You focus on re-closing and maintaining control. 5 reps.
    5. Sweep attempt drill: From closed guard, practice setting up and executing a simple scissor sweep against a resisting partner. 5 reps.
    6. Live rolling (50% resistance): Apply closed guard techniques in a controlled sparring situation, focusing on maintaining control and posture.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • When you are on your back and want to establish a dominant control position.
    • When your opponent has a forward-leaning posture, making it difficult for them to defend.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Knee-slice pass: Opponent steps one knee across your body, placing it on the mat, and drives their weight forward to pass your legs.
    • Torreano pass: Opponent steps to the side of your legs, aiming to get their hips past your hips and drive forward.
    • Stack pass: Opponent drives their weight directly down onto your chest, attempting to flatten you out and bypass your guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to hold closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs from gripping too tightly with the fingers only, or by allowing your wrists to bend backward excessively. Instead, ensure you grip with your entire hand and maintain a neutral or slightly flexed wrist position. Use your forearm and bicep strength to pull your opponent's arms in, rather than just squeezing with your fingers.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, focus on using your legs as a vise and your hips to create angles and break their posture. Avoid letting them establish heavy pressure on top. Actively pull their arms in and use your head to push their face away, creating space to work for submissions like the triangle choke or armbar.
    Q: When should I switch from closed guard to another guard?

    You should consider transitioning from closed guard when your opponent consistently defends your posture control and prevents you from creating submission opportunities. If they are successfully posturing up and keeping their base wide, it’s a good indicator to transition to an open guard like spider guard or De La Riva Guard to create new angles and attacks.
