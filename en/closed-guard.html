Closed Guard is a fundamental BJJ position where you control your opponent from your back, legs locked around their waist. It allows for submission opportunities and sweep setups, providing a safe base.

White belts often struggle with Closed Guard by becoming too rigid or relying on strength. This leads to poor posture for the opponent and ineffective attacks.

The core principle is creating a strong, tight frame with your legs and hips, using your core to generate leverage rather than brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From your back, bring your knees towards your chest, then extend your legs to hook behind your opponent's hips.
    2. Lock your ankles by crossing them, ensuring your heels are close to your glutes for maximum leverage. This creates a tight "box" around their hips.
    3. Maintain a slight pelvic tilt forward (anterior tilt) to keep your hips close to your opponent's hips, preventing them from posturing up.
    4. Grip both of your opponent's sleeves at the wrist joint with an "over-under" grip or a "double-sleeve" grip.
    5. Apply slight inward pressure with your knees and squeeze your legs together, creating tension and controlling their base.
    6. Use your grips to pull your opponent's upper body towards your chest, breaking their posture and bringing them into range for attacks.
    7. Continuously adjust your hip angle, driving your hips forward and slightly up to maintain pressure and control their center of gravity.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping the opponent's arms too high on the bicep: This allows them to easily posture up by straightening their arms, creating a "lever" that can hyperextend your shoulders or elbows if you resist with equal force.
    • Locking ankles too loosely: This creates "slack" in your guard, allowing the opponent to easily "step out" or create space to posture, compromising your control and leading to potential knee or ankle twists if they apply pressure.
    • Arching your back excessively to create space: This puts immense strain on your lumbar spine, risking disc injury. Instead, focus on tilting your pelvis forward and driving your hips up.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice locking and unlocking your Closed Guard, focusing on the ankle lock and pelvic tilt. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill: With a compliant partner, practice establishing Closed Guard from their side. Focus on the initial leg hook and lock. (20 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Grip fighting drill: Practice establishing double sleeve grips from Closed Guard and pulling the opponent's posture down. (30 reps, 50% resistance)
    4. Basic sweep drill (Scissor Sweep): Practice the mechanics of the Scissor Sweep from a stable Closed Guard. (20 reps per side, 60% resistance)
    5. Submission drill (Triangle Choke setup): Practice the initial steps of setting up a Triangle Choke from Closed Guard. (15 reps per side, 70% resistance)
    6. Light positional sparring: Practice maintaining Closed Guard against a resisting opponent, focusing on control and not letting them pass. (3 rounds, 80% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard from a standing or kneeling position.
    • When your opponent is postured up and you have an opportunity to establish control.
    • When you are on your back and your opponent is between your legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Posture Control: Maintain constant pressure with your hips, driving them forward to prevent your opponent from straightening their back. This is achieved by keeping your knees tight to your chest and tilting your pelvis anteriorly.
    • Grip Fighting: Actively fight for and maintain control of your opponent's sleeves. This prevents them from framing effectively and allows you to pull them into your attacks or sweeps.
    • Hip Movement: Be prepared to shift your hips to the side to create angles for sweeps or submissions. A slight hip escape can open up opportunities for a Triangle Choke or a Hip Bump Sweep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do my knees hurt when I try to keep my Closed Guard tight?

    Your knee pain likely stems from locking your ankles too far back, creating excessive torque. Instead, ensure your heels are close to your glutes and the primary pressure comes from squeezing your thighs and calves together, not just the Ankle Lock. This distributes the force more evenly and protects your knee joints.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, focus on creating a tight "box" with your legs, preventing them from creating space to posture. Use your grips to pull their upper body down and break their posture. Prioritize hip pressure and core engagement over muscular strength. Small, consistent adjustments are more effective than large, forceful movements.

    Q: You should consider transitioning if your opponent consistently "posts" their feet on your hips and maintains a strong, upright posture that you cannot break. This indicates they are neutralizing your leg control. It's also time to transition if they are successfully creating significant space and threatening to pass your guard without you having a clear submission or sweep opportunity.
