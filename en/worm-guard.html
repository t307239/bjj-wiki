Worm Guard is an open guard position initiated from your back, aiming to control your opponent's legs and hips to create off-balancing and sweep opportunities.

White belts often struggle by focusing on 'pulling' rather than creating leverage through precise body mechanics and frame creation.

The core principle is establishing a deep, controlling leg entanglement that allows you to manipulate your opponent's base and posture by controlling their hips.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From your back, establish your feet on your opponent's hips. Your knees should be bent at approximately 90 degrees.
    2. Initiate a "worm" grip: insert your right arm under your opponent's right thigh, cupping their hamstring with your hand. Your forearm should be flush against their leg.
    3. Simultaneously, insert your left arm under your opponent's left thigh, cupping their hamstring with your hand. Your forearm should be flush against their leg.
    4. Your opponent's legs are now trapped between your arms, creating a "worm" entanglement.
    5. Drive your hips slightly upwards, creating a slight anterior tilt of your pelvis. This elevates your hips off the mat.
    6. Use your legs to maintain a frame on your opponent's hips, preventing them from easily posturing up or collapsing your guard.
    7. To initiate a sweep, shift your weight to your left hip, bringing your right leg forward and over your opponent's left hip, while simultaneously driving your left shin across their body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grasping ankles or shins instead of hamstrings: This compromises your ability to control hip rotation and leverage, leading to a loss of guard and potential knee hyperextension if the opponent drives forward forcefully.
    • Allowing knees to collapse inward: This compresses your own knee joints and makes it impossible to create a stable frame, inviting guard passes and risking meniscus tears from lateral force.
    • Overextending your back when trying to create space: This places undue stress on your lumbar spine and can lead to muscle strains or disc injuries; maintain a neutral spine by using your hips and core to generate movement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice establishing the worm grip and hip elevation with no resistance (0% resistance). Perform 10 repetitions.
    2. Partner drilling: With a compliant partner, practice establishing the grip and hip movement (25% resistance). Focus on control, not speed. Perform 5 repetitions.
    3. Guard retention drill: Partner attempts to pass your guard from a static position. Focus on maintaining the worm grip and hip frame (50% resistance). Perform 3 minutes.
    4. Sweep initiation drill: Practice the initial movements of a sweep from the worm guard, with partner offering minimal resistance (75% resistance). Perform 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Flow rolling: Engage in light sparring, focusing on transitioning into and maintaining worm guard, and attempting sweeps (90% resistance). Perform 5 minutes.
    6. Live rolling: Apply worm guard in a full sparring scenario, emphasizing control and proper biomechanics over immediate submission (100% resistance). Perform 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up and creating distance, allowing you to intercept their legs.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard from a standing or kneeling position, and you can secure the leg entanglement.
    • When you are being pressured from a closed guard or half guard and need to open up to a more dynamic position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass: Opponent drives their weight directly forward, attempting to collapse your guard by driving their chest into your hips. Counter by widening your knee base and using your arms to push their hips away, maintaining a strong frame.
    • Toreando Pass: Opponent attempts to circle their hips around your legs and pass to the side. Counter by tightening your worm grip, rotating your hips to follow their movement, and using your free leg to block their hips.
    • Knee Slice Pass: Opponent drives their knee across your body to cut through your guard. Counter by adjusting your hip angle to face them, using your free leg to create a barrier, and potentially transitioning to a butterfly guard or x-guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do Worm Guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs when your wrists are bent backwards (extension) while you are gripping. This happens if you are gripping too loosely or if your opponent is actively pushing into your grip. Ensure you are gripping the hamstrings firmly with your palm and fingers, keeping your wrists in a neutral or slightly flexed position. Imagine you are trying to 'cup' the hamstring rather than just 'holding' it.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, your Worm Guard becomes a tool to prevent them from using their weight advantage to crush you. Focus on creating a strong frame with your hips and legs to keep their weight slightly elevated. Use the worm grips to control their leg base and dictate their movement, preventing them from settling their weight onto you. Prioritize sweeps that unbalance them rather than trying to lift them.

    Q: If your opponent effectively counters your sweep attempts or manages to pass your leg entanglement, it's time to transition. For example, if they stack you effectively and you cannot create space, you might transition to a butterfly guard by tucking your hips under them and bringing your knees to your chest. If they circle their hips for a toreando, and you can't follow, you might release one grip and attempt a leg drag.
