Sickle Sweep: A Biomechanical Guide for White Belts

The Sickle Sweep is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to unbalance your opponent and transition to a superior position. White belts often struggle with the timing and leverage required for this sweep.

The key mechanical insight is using your hip's rotation to create a fulcrum, driving your opponent's base off-center with a specific leg motion.

This sweep effectively disrupts your opponent's posture and forward pressure, creating an opening to advance your position.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong closed guard: secure both of your opponent's legs with your ankles crossed behind their back.
    2. Grip control: Obtain a high cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left sleeve) with your left hand.
    3. Posture break: Pull your opponent forward and down towards your chest by extending your arms and engaging your core.
    4. Hip elevation: As your opponent's weight shifts forward, lift your hips off the mat, creating space between your pelvis and theirs.
    5. Leg motion initiation: While maintaining the grips and hip elevation, begin to rotate your hips to your left, bringing your right knee towards your chest.
    6. Sickle action: Extend your right leg out and across your opponent's body, aiming to sweep their left leg from under them. The motion should resemble a sickle cutting through grass.
    7. Weight transfer: Simultaneously, as your right leg extends, drive your hips into your opponent's left hip while pulling their upper body further towards you with your grips.
    8. Transition: As your opponent falls, immediately drive your hips up and forward, transitioning to a top position like side control or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to sweep by just pushing with your arms: This can cause hyperextension of your elbows and shoulders, damaging ligaments.
    • The correct movement is to use hip rotation and leg extension as the primary force.
    • Tensing your neck and shoulders excessively during the pull: This can lead to cervical strain or shoulder impingement.
    • Focus on using your lats and core for the pull, keeping your neck relaxed.
    • Extending your leg too early or too straight: This can expose your knee to a knee-bar or cause a hamstring strain.
    • Ensure your knee is bent and the sweep is initiated with hip rotation, not just leg power.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0% resistance): Practice the hip elevation, rotation, and leg extension motion without an opponent, focusing on the feeling of the sickle action. 10 reps.
    2. Partner drilling, no resistance (25% resistance): With a compliant partner lying on their back, practice the full sequence of grips, posture break, hip lift, and sweep motion. 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drilling, light resistance (50% resistance): Your partner offers slight resistance to your posture break and hip lift. Focus on maintaining your grips and hip angle. 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drilling, moderate resistance (75% resistance): Your partner actively tries to prevent the sweep by posting their feet or shrugging their shoulders. Adapt your timing and pressure. 5 reps per side.
    5. Live drilling, controlled (90% resistance): Execute the Sickle Sweep from closed guard in a controlled sparring situation. Focus on timing and proper execution. 3 reps.
    6. Live sparring (100% resistance): Attempt the Sickle Sweep during regular sparring. Prioritize safe execution and learning from attempts. 5 attempts.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up slightly, creating a small gap at their hips.
    • When your opponent is leaning heavily forward, attempting a pressure pass.
    • When you have achieved a strong collar and sleeve grip, allowing you to control their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape to Guard Recovery: If your opponent attempts the sweep, immediately hip escape to your right, bringing your right knee to your chest to re-establish guard.
    • Grip Break and Posture Up: As your opponent initiates the sweep, focus on breaking their collar grip and using your hands to post on their hips or shoulders to regain your base.
    • Leg Pummeling: If your opponent's leg is coming across, actively pummel your leg to the outside of theirs, creating space and preventing the sweep from connecting.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right leg feel weak and unstable when I try to sickle sweep?

    This often occurs when you're not properly utilizing your hips as the primary lever. Instead of extending your leg directly, focus on rotating your hips to your left first, creating a scooping motion with your right leg. Ensure your left leg remains tight around your opponent's hips to maintain your base.

    Q: I keep falling over when I try the sickle sweep, what am I doing wrong?

    You're likely not transferring your weight effectively after the sweep. As your opponent begins to fall, drive your hips *into* their falling hip and forward. This upward and forward drive is what allows you to land in a top position, rather than falling with them.
