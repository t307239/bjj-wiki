The Sickle Sweep is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to disrupt your opponent's base and create an opportunity to advance your position.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength or incorrect body angles, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The core principle is using your hips and a precise leg motion to 'hook' and 'pull' your opponent's leg, creating a lever action.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (e.g., opponent's left sleeve) with your left hand.
    2. Maintain closed guard, ensuring your knees are tight around your opponent's torso.
    3. Shift your weight slightly to your left hip, creating a 45-degree angle between your torso and your opponent's spine.
    4. With your left leg, hook your opponent's right ankle from the inside, ensuring your shin is perpendicular to the floor.
    5. Simultaneously, pull down on the opponent's collar with your right hand and push their right shoulder forward with your left forearm.
    6. Drive your right hip upwards and slightly towards your opponent's right hip, creating a lifting motion.
    7. As your hips lift, extend your left leg forcefully in a sweeping motion, like a sickle, driving their right foot upwards and across their body.
    8. As their weight shifts, immediately bring your right knee to the mat, pivoting your hips to face your opponent, and bring your left leg to secure a top position.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'pull' the opponent directly over with arm strength alone: This strains the shoulder joint (rotator cuff tears) and elbow joint (ligament damage) due to improper leverage. Instead, use your hips to drive and your legs to hook and sweep.
    • Extending your right leg too early or with insufficient hip drive: This can hyperextend the knee of your sweeping leg, risking ACL or MCL tears. Ensure your hips are elevated and driving before the leg extension.
    • Not maintaining closed guard tightness: If your knees are loose, the opponent can easily posture up or pass, and attempting the sweep can lead to your own hips being exposed to pressure, risking hip flexor strains.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip lift and leg hook/sweep motion without a partner, focusing on the body mechanics. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the full sequence of grips, hip movement, and sweep. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Partner maintains a stable base but offers minimal resistance to the sweep. Focus on timing. (15 reps)
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance - 50%): Partner attempts to resist by shrimping or adjusting their base slightly. Focus on overcoming slight pressure. (15 reps)
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Partner actively tries to prevent the sweep by posturing up or creating frames. Focus on dynamic adjustments. (10 reps)
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Attempt the Sickle Sweep during sparring. Focus on successful execution and recovery if the sweep fails. (5 attempts per roll)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent establishes a tight closed guard and is posturing slightly.
    • When your opponent attempts to break your guard by posting their feet on your hips.
    • When you have established control of both collar and sleeve, and the opponent's weight is not heavily shifted onto their base.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Pass (e.g., Toreando Pass): If the sweep is slow and the opponent recognizes it, they can often step their legs out and drive forward to pass. Maintain grip control and transition to a defensive posture.
    • Hip Escape and Re-guard: If the sweep is initiated but fails, immediately hip escape to create space and re-establish a guard position, preventing them from passing.
    • Stacking and Pressure: A strong opponent might attempt to 'stack' you by driving their weight directly onto your hips and chest. Disengage the sweep and focus on framing with your forearms to create space.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right knee hurt when I try to do the Sickle Sweep?

    Your right knee likely hurts because you are not properly engaging your hips to create the sweeping motion. Instead, you are relying on a forceful extension of your leg, which puts undue stress on the knee joint. Ensure your hips are elevated and driving upwards before you extend your leg outwards, creating a lever action rather than a direct push.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, focus on maximizing your leverage and minimizing their ability to use their weight. Ensure your closed guard is extremely tight, and use a strong cross-collar grip to control their posture. The key is to 'hook' their ankle with precision and use your hip elevation to off-balance them before the leg sweep, rather than trying to overpower their weight.

    Q: The optimal time to attempt the Sickle Sweep is when your opponent is slightly postured up, creating a gap in their base. If they are completely flattened out or excessively postured, the sweep becomes much harder. Look for moments when they are shifting their weight or trying to create space themselves, as this is when their base is most vulnerable to disruption.
