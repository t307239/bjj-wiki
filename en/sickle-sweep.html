The Sickle Sweep is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to unbalance your opponent and reverse the position into your guard or mount.

White belts often struggle by trying to muscle the sweep, neglecting the crucial hip and leg mechanics required.

The key is creating an off-balancing angle with your hips while simultaneously using your legs to drive and lift.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong double-sleeve grip on your opponent's gi sleeves, approximately 2 inches from the elbow joint.
    2. Place your left foot on your opponent's right hip, creating a frame and angling your body 45 degrees to your left.
    3. Simultaneously, drive your right foot's heel towards your opponent's left hip crease, keeping your knee slightly bent.
    4. Initiate a controlled hip lift, driving your lower back into the mat and elevating your hips off the ground.
    5. As your hips lift, use your right leg (the one driving the heel towards the hip crease) to hook behind your opponent's left knee.
    6. Simultaneously, extend your left leg off their hip, creating a lever.
    7. Drive your opponent's left leg forward and across their body using your hooked right leg, while pulling their right sleeve across their body with your right hand, and their left sleeve towards your left hip with your left hand.
    8. Continue the hip lift and the leg drive to complete the sweep, transitioning to your knees or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to lift your opponent solely with your arms and upper body, leading to potential shoulder impingement or rotator cuff tears from excessive strain.
    • Instead, focus on the hip lift and leg drive; keep your arms engaged but not as the primary force generators.
    • Kicking your legs outwards in a wide arc, which can hyperextend your knees or ankles, causing ligamentous sprains.
    • Maintain controlled movements and a tight connection with your opponent's legs; avoid uncontrolled flailing.
    • Letting your hips drop to the mat after the initial lift, which removes the leverage needed for the sweep and can lead to your guard being passed.
    • Maintain active hip elevation throughout the sweep motion, even as you transition to the next position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip lift and leg hook motion without a partner, focusing on the feeling of creating the angle and leverage. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice establishing grips and executing the leg hook and hip lift. (25 reps per side)
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner provides minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the timing of the sweep. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drilling (controlled resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the sweep but allows you to complete it with proper technique. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (positional sparring): Start from closed guard and attempt the Sickle Sweep with a partner who is actively defending. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full rolling): Integrate the Sickle Sweep into your regular rolling sessions. (Focus on opportunities, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing up heavily within your closed guard, creating space.
    • When your opponent is attempting to break your closed guard by stacking or pushing down on your knees.
    • When your opponent's weight is distributed unevenly, leaving one leg more accessible for the hook.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If the sweep is initiated, immediately hip escape away from the direction of the sweep and re-establish your guard.
    • Knee Shield and Grip Break: If your opponent hooks your leg, use your free leg to create a knee shield and simultaneously break their sleeve grip.
    • Stance Adjustment and Base Widening: If the opponent attempts to sweep by lifting, widen your base and adjust your stance to remain stable, preventing the off-balancing effect.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try the Sickle Sweep?

    Shoulder pain during the Sickle Sweep often occurs when you rely too heavily on pulling with your arms to generate the sweep. This puts excessive strain on your shoulder joint. Instead, focus on the powerful hip lift and leg drive to create the off-balancing motion. Your arms should be used to control your opponent's posture and guide their movement, not to muscle the sweep.

    Q: How can I perform the Sickle Sweep against a much larger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paraMount. Focus on timing your hip lift precisely when they are posturing up or shifting their weight. Use your left foot on their hip to create a strong frame and angle your body significantly. Drive your right heel deep into their hip crease and hook behind their knee with control, then use your hips and legs to drive their weight away from you, rather than trying to lift them directly.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Sickle Sweep from closed guard?

    The optimal time to attempt the Sickle Sweep is when your opponent is posturing up and leaning forward, creating an imbalance. If they are too flat or their weight is directly over your hips, the sweep will be less effective. Look for moments when they are actively trying to break your guard by pushing down or stacking, as this often creates the necessary opening for the sweep's mechanics.
