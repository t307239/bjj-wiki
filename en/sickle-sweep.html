The Sickle Sweep originates from Closed Guard and aims to invert your opponent's base, allowing you to sweep them and secure a dominant position.

White belts often struggle by trying to force the sweep with raw strength, neglecting the crucial hip mechanics and timing needed for efficient execution.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to create an off-balancing angle, allowing your legs to do the work of sweeping, not your arms.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (left arm) with your left hand. Your weight should be centered, knees tucked.
    2. Shift your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to your opponent's torso. Your left knee should be slightly forward.
    3. Extend your right leg, aiming to place your right foot on your opponent's left hip crease. Keep your right leg straight but not locked.
    4. Simultaneously, begin to pull down on the collar grip and slightly pull the sleeve grip towards your chest. This initiates the off-balancing.
    5. Drive your left knee forward and under your opponent's left hip, creating a lever point. Your left shin should be perpendicular to the mat.
    6. As your opponent attempts to post their weight forward, drive your right foot into their hip and sweep your right leg in a sickle-like motion, arcing it upwards and across their body.
    7. As your right leg sweeps, drive your hips upwards and forward, using the lever of your left knee to lift their hip. This motion should cause them to fall to their right.
    8. Immediately transition to a hip-to-hip connection, bringing your left knee to the mat and your right leg to their hip, ready to secure side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to lift the opponent solely with arm strength: This can cause shoulder impingement and rotator cuff tears due to excessive external rotation and strain.
    • Extending the right leg too forcefully without hip engagement: This risks hyperextending the knee and damaging the ACL or MCL from sudden, uncontrolled impact.
    • Not creating a sufficient hip angle or driving the left knee under: This leads to a "pushing" motion rather than a sweep, potentially causing lumbar spine strain from poor leverage and overextension.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and leg sweep motion without a partner, focusing on hip angle and foot placement. 20 reps.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sweep sequence, focusing on grip and hip mechanics. 10 reps per side.
    3. Light resistance drill (25%): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the sweep mechanics and hip drive. 10 reps per side.
    4. Moderate resistance drill (50%): Partner actively tries to maintain their base, requiring you to adjust your timing and hip pressure. 10 reps per side.
    5. High resistance drill (75%): Partner actively defends, simulating a live roll scenario where you must commit to the sweep. 5 reps per side.
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Apply the Sickle Sweep in a live rolling context, focusing on recognizing opportunities and adapting to resistance. 3-5 attempts per roll.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is posturing down, making it difficult for them to maintain a strong base.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you from closed guard, creating an opening for an inversion.
    • When your opponent's hips are close to yours, allowing for effective hip leverage.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Base Reinforcement: If the sweep is initiated, the defender can immediately hip escape to their right and drive their left knee to the mat, creating a strong base and preventing the sweep.
    • Grip Break and Posture Up: The defender can quickly break the collar grip and posture up, negating the pulling pressure and stabilizing their base.
    • Leg Overhook and Pressure: The defender can place their right leg over the attacker's right leg and apply downward pressure, preventing the sickle motion and potentially transitioning to a pass.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my right knee hurt when I try the Sickle Sweep?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are extending your right leg with excessive force and no hip engagement. This can cause hyperextension or a shearing force on the ACL/MCL. Focus on using your hips to drive the sweep and keep your knee slightly bent throughout the motion, rather than locking it out.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, leverage is paramount. Ensure you create a significant hip angle to their side, making them off-balance. Use your left shin as a solid fulcrum under their hip, and drive your hips upwards as your right leg sweeps. Do not try to lift them vertically; focus on rotating their base.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Sickle Sweep, and why does it fail if I do it too early?

    The Sickle Sweep is best attempted when your opponent is postured down or attempting to stack you. If you attempt it too early, while they are still upright and have a solid base, they can easily resist the sweep by maintaining their posture and weight distribution, rendering your efforts ineffective and potentially leaving you exposed.
