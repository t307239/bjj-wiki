The Sickle Sweep is executed from Closed Guard, aiming to unbalance your opponent and end up in a dominant top position. It relies on a specific leg mechanics to generate leverage.

White belts often fail because they rely on brute strength or incorrect body positioning, leading to ineffective sweeps and potential injury. Understanding the rotational mechanics is crucial.

The single most important insight is using your hips to drive your opponent's base offline, creating the opening for your leg to hook and sweep.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Establish a cross-collar grip with your right hand and a cross-sleeve grip on their left arm with your left hand. Your hips should be slightly elevated, creating space.
    2. Hip Angle: Rotate your hips 45 degrees to your left, so your right hip is closer to your opponent's left hip.
    3. Leg Placement: Bring your right leg up and over their left hip, aiming to place your right foot's sole on their left hip bone. Your left leg remains inside their legs, bent at the knee.
    4. Frame Creation: Use your right arm (collar grip) to pull their upper body towards you, while your left arm (sleeve grip) creates a frame by pushing their left elbow slightly away, forcing them to lean forward.
    5. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your left leg, allowing your right leg to become the primary lever.
    6. Hip Drive: Drive your right hip forward and upwards, using your right leg on their hip as a pivot point. Simultaneously, pull their collar grip to initiate a rotational force.
    7. Sweep Motion: As you drive your hip and pull their collar, extend your right leg with a sweeping motion, hooking their hip and driving their weight over their base. Your left leg acts as a secondary hook or frame to prevent them from re-establishing base.
    8. Transition: Immediately follow the sweep by posting your left hand on the mat and driving your hips forward to secure a top position, typically side control or mount.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Knee Extension: Extending your right leg too forcefully and straight when your foot is on their hip can hyperextend your knee. This can damage the ACL or MCL ligaments. Instead, maintain a slight bend in your right knee as you initiate the sweep, allowing for a more controlled rotation.
    • Jerking the Collar Grip: Yanking upwards on the collar grip without hip drive can strain your shoulder joint and rotator cuff. This is due to an uncontrolled anterior translation of the humerus. Instead, use the collar grip in conjunction with your hip drive for a pulling and rotating motion.
    • Allowing Opponent to Post: If your opponent's leg remains too active or they create a strong base, attempting to force the sweep can lead to your ankle being twisted. This can cause sprains or fractures to the talocrural joint. Instead, recognize when the sweep is not working and revert to a safe guard position or transition to another technique.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Drill: Practice the hip rotation and drive motion without a partner. Focus on rotating your hips 45 degrees and driving your right hip forward. 50 reps.
    2. Isolation with Partner (No Resistance): With a partner lying down, practice the leg placement, hip angle, and initial hip drive. They offer no resistance. 20 reps per side.
    3. Controlled Sweep (Light Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance (e.g., just trying to keep their base). Focus on the full execution of grips, hip drive, and sweep. 15 reps per side.
    4. Adding Collar/Sleeve Control: Partner now actively resists slightly. Focus on maintaining grips and using them with your hip drive to create the unbalancing. 15 reps per side.
    5. Introduction to Counter-Pressure: Partner begins to actively defend or attempt to recover base. You must adapt your sweep mechanics to overcome their light pressure. 10 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (50% Resistance): Apply the Sickle Sweep in rolling scenarios where your partner is actively trying to pass or sweep, but with controlled intensity. Focus on timing and proper execution. 5 minutes rolling, attempt 2-3 sweeps.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is posturing up and leaning forward, creating an overextended upper body.
    • When opponent is gripping your closed guard too tightly, making it difficult for them to defend leg movements.
    • When opponent attempts to stack you from closed guard, leaving their hips accessible for a sweep.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The Stand-up: If you feel the sweep is being initiated, quickly stand up on your feet while maintaining control of their legs. This negates the sweep leverage by raising your base. The opponent must then react to your new top position.
    • The Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If the sweep is weak, immediately hip escape your body away from the direction of the sweep. This creates space to bring your legs back into a more secure guard position (e.g., butterfly guard or open guard). This prevents you from being swept and allows for a new attack.
    • The Knee-Shield/Half Guard Transition: If the sweep attempt leaves your legs in an awkward position, use your free leg to establish a knee shield against their hip. This allows you to transition into half guard, where you can then work for sweeps or passes from that position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my hip hurt when I try the Sickle Sweep from closed guard?

    Your hip pain likely stems from attempting to force the sweep with your leg alone, rather than using your hips as the primary driver. When you extend your leg too hard against their hip bone without proper hip rotation, you can impinge the hip joint or strain the surrounding musculature. Focus on rotating your hips 45 degrees and driving your right hip *forward* and *up*, using the leg on their hip as a pivot point, not a ram.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, brute strength is your enemy. The Sickle Sweep's effectiveness comes from leverage, not power. Focus on creating a tight closed guard and generating maximum space with your hips. Use your grips to pull their upper body forward and create a significant lean. Then, initiate the hip rotation and drive your right hip forcefully. The goal is to off-balance their entire frame, not to lift them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Sickle Sweep in BJJ?

    The ideal time to attempt the Sickle Sweep is when your opponent is leaning forward, creating an overextended posture where their weight is forward. This typically happens when they are trying to break your guard, posture up, or pass your guard aggressively. If they are too upright or their base is too stable, the sweep will be significantly harder to execute and less effective, so wait for that forward lean.
