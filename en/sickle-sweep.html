The Sickle Sweep is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to disrupt the opponent's base and create an angle for you to come up to a dominant position.

White belts often fail by trying to muscle the sweep, neglecting the crucial hip movement and leverage.

The core principle is using your leg as a lever, driven by hip rotation and a precise weight transfer, not brute force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure a collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on the opponent's left sleeve with your left hand.
    2. Leg Placement: Bring your right foot to the opponent's left hip crease, with your knee pointing slightly upwards.
    3. Hip Angle: Simultaneously, turn your hips to your left, creating a 45-degree angle relative to the opponent.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your left hip, lowering your center of gravity.
    5. Leverage: Extend your right leg, using your foot on their hip as a fulcrum.
    6. Sweep Motion: Drive your hips forward and to your left, pulling the opponent's left arm across their body.
    7. Completion: As the opponent falls, use your grips to guide them and immediately transition to your left side, bringing your right knee to their hip to establish side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Knee Extension: Trying to extend the sweeping leg straight out without hip rotation can hyper-extend the knee joint, risking ACL or meniscus tears.
    • Jerking Grips: Pulling rigidly on the collar and sleeve without body movement can strain the rotator cuff and elbow ligaments.
    • Flat Footedness: Remaining flat on your back during the sweep attempt exposes your spine and tailbone to impact, increasing risk of lumbar strain or coccyx injury.
    • Over-Rotation: Spinning your hips too far without control can lead to hip flexor strains or labral tears.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Execution (0% resistance): Practice the hip angle, leg placement, and weight transfer without an opponent, focusing on smooth transitions. (20 reps)
    2. Partner Assisted (25% resistance): Have a partner lie on their back. You perform the sweep motion, and they gently resist to feel the leverage. (15 reps)
    3. Controlled Sweep (50% resistance): From closed guard, initiate the sweep. Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the sweep action. (10 reps)
    4. Dynamic Sweep (75% resistance): Your partner attempts to posture up slightly. Execute the sweep, focusing on timing the hip drive with their weight shift. (10 reps)
    5. Live Sweep Attempt (90% resistance): In a controlled rolling scenario, attempt the Sickle Sweep when the opportunity arises. Focus on correct execution over success. (5 attempts)
    6. Full Rolling Application (100% resistance): Incorporate the Sickle Sweep into your rolling, aiming for successful sweeps against a resisting opponent. (Continuous)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent has their weight distributed forward, creating an opening for a sweep.
    • When the opponent is attempting to posture up, creating a moment of instability.
    • When you have established strong collar and sleeve control, preventing them from easily breaking your guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Stack Pass: If the opponent realizes the sweep attempt and drives their weight forward, they can attempt a stack pass by driving their hips over yours. To counter, maintain your hip angle and use your free leg to push their hips away, or transition to a triangle choke.
    • Knee Slice Defense: If the opponent starts to pass by bringing their knee across your body, maintain tight guard and use your feet to push their knee back out, or transition to a butterfly guard to regain a better position.
    • Base Out: The opponent can attempt to base out by widening their stance and keeping their weight back. If this happens, you may need to switch to a different sweep or attempt a submission from guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to Sickle Sweep?

    Knee pain during the Sickle Sweep often occurs when you extend your sweeping leg without proper hip rotation. This places excessive shear force on the knee joint. Ensure your hips are turned at a 45-degree angle to your opponent and that your sweeping leg acts as a lever driven by hip extension, not a straight-leg kick.

    Q: How can I use the Sickle Sweep against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on establishing very tight collar and sleeve grips to control their upper body and prevent them from easily posturing. Crucially, exaggerate your hip rotation and weight transfer; the bigger they are, the more their own weight will work for you when you disrupt their base. Do not try to lift them; focus on the angle and the sweep.

    Q: When is the best time to initiate the Sickle Sweep from closed guard?

    The optimal time to initiate the Sickle Sweep is when the opponent is slightly off-balance, typically when they are trying to posture up or have their weight forward to apply pressure. Look for moments where their weight is not directly over their hips. Avoid attempting it when they are perfectly squared and have a strong, stable base.
