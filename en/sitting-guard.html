Mastering Sitting Guard in BJJ: A White Belt's Foundation

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Sitting Guard is an open guard position where you sit upright with your opponent in front, legs between you, controlling distance and creating attack angles. White belts often get stuck here because they lack the fundamental understanding of posture and base required to maintain it effectively. The key insight is using your feet on the hips to create a constant, dynamic frame, preventing your opponent from collapsing your structure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start from a supine position (on your back). 2. Drive your hips slightly forward and lift your torso to an upright sitting posture, knees bent at 90 degrees. 3. Place the soles of your feet firmly on the opponent's hip bones, creating a strong anterior pelvic tilt. 4. Grip the opponent's sleeves or collar with both hands, elbows tucked close to your body to maintain a tight frame. 5. Maintain a slight gap between your hips and the opponent's torso by actively pushing with your feet. 6. Keep your back straight and your core engaged to support your upright posture. 7. Your weight should be distributed evenly on your glutes, not on your tailbone, allowing for hip mobility.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly placing feet too low on the opponent's thighs: This allows the opponent to easily drive forward and collapse your guard, potentially leading to a knee hyperextension injury if their weight drives into your bent knee. Always aim for the hip bones.
    • Slouching your back and letting your head drop forward: This compromises your posture and makes you vulnerable to pressure passes, also straining your lumbar spine. Maintain a tall, proud chest and a neutral spine.
    • Gripping too wide or with extended elbows: This creates a weak frame, allowing the opponent to easily break your grips and advance. Keep your elbows tucked and your grips tight on the sleeves or collar.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice sitting up, placing feet on imaginary hips, and holding a tall posture for 30 seconds. Repeat 10 times.
    2. Solo drill: Practice sitting up, placing feet on a partner's hips (no resistance), and maintaining the frame for 30 seconds. Repeat 10 times.
    3. Partner drill (0% resistance): Opponent stands in front. You practice entering Sitting Guard, establishing grips, and maintaining the frame for 1 minute. Switch roles. 5 reps per person.
    4. Partner drill (25% resistance): Opponent attempts to hug your legs or step around. You focus on maintaining the frame and hip push. 1 minute rounds. 5 reps.
    5. Partner drill (50% resistance): Opponent attempts to advance posture and pass. You focus on blocking their hips with your feet and maintaining frames. 1 minute rounds. 5 reps.
    6. Live rolling (75% resistance): Focus on maintaining Sitting Guard and preventing immediate passes for 2 minutes. Gradually increase intensity.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is posturing up after you have been playing guard from your back.
    • When an opponent attempts to pass your guard but is held at a medium distance.
    • When you are transitioning from another guard and want to establish a stable, upright position.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Leg Sweep Defense: If the opponent attempts to sweep your legs, actively push their hips away with your feet while simultaneously pulling on their collar or sleeve to disrupt their balance.
    • Posture Control: If the opponent tries to lower their base and drive forward, use your feet on their hips to create space and your arm grips to pull them off-balance.
    • Grip Fighting: If the opponent tries to break your grips, maintain a tight frame with your forearms and elbows while continuing to push their hips back with your feet.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try to maintain Sitting Guard for too long?

    This typically occurs from a lack of core engagement and a rounded spine. Instead of relying on your lower back, focus on keeping your glutes firmly on the mat and actively engaging your abdominal muscles to support an upright posture. Imagine a string pulling the crown of your head towards the ceiling to create spinal extension.

    Q: How can I effectively use Sitting Guard against a much bigger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your primary goal is to use your feet on their hips as a powerful lever to create and maintain distance. Actively push their weight away to prevent them from collapsing your guard. Focus on controlling their posture with precise collar and sleeve grips, and be ready to transition to sweeps or submissions that exploit their weight advantage against them.

    Q: Is Sitting Guard considered an open guard or a closed guard in BJJ?

    Sitting Guard is classified as an open guard. Unlike Closed Guard, where your legs are locked around the opponent's waist, in Sitting Guard, your legs are extended and used to create a frame and control distance. This allows for more dynamic movement and a wider range of offensive and defensive options.
