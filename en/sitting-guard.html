Sitting Guard: The Essential BJJ Technique for White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Sitting Guard is a versatile guard position initiated from a seated posture, designed to control distance and create offensive opportunities from your back.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of body awareness, leading to uncontrolled movements and overextension, which invites passes and injuries.

The core principle is maintaining a strong, mobile base with your hips elevated and your legs actively framing to prevent forward pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Begin seated on your mat, spine neutral, knees bent to 90 degrees.
    2. Place your feet flat on the mat, hip-width apart, creating a stable base.
    3. Grip opponent's sleeves (collar and sleeve grip) or pant legs (pant grip) with both hands.
    4. Extend your arms to create a frame, keeping elbows inside your knees.
    5. Elevate your hips slightly off the mat by engaging your glutes and lower back.
    6. Rock your base forward and backward by flexing and extending your hips.
    7. Adjust your knee angle dynamically to control the distance and pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Slumping your back: This causes spinal flexion, risking lumbar disc herniation and losing all framing power. Always maintain a neutral or slightly arched spine, engaging your core.
    • Overextending arms: Pushing directly forward with straight arms strains your elbows and shoulders, inviting armbars. Keep a slight bend in your elbows and focus on pushing your opponent's shoulders away.
    • Letting hips drop: This removes your base and framing ability, allowing your opponent to easily advance their hips and pass. Actively lift your hips using your glutes and core muscles.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice sitting up, establishing grips on imaginary sleeves, and rocking your hips back and forth for 1 minute, 5 reps.
    2. Solo drill: Practice maintaining grips while shifting your weight side-to-side, 1 minute, 5 reps.
    3. Partner drill (0% resistance): Partner stands, you sit in Sitting Guard. Practice establishing grips and maintaining frames. Focus on hip movement. 5 reps each side.
    4. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner gently tries to advance. You focus on maintaining grips and framing. 5 reps each side.
    5. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner applies light pressure. Focus on hip escapes and framing to prevent them from clearing your legs. 5 reps each side.
    6. Live rolling (75% resistance): Integrate Sitting Guard into positional sparring. Focus on achieving and holding the position, not submissions. 3 x 2-minute rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When an opponent is postured up and attempting to pass your guard.
    • When you are on your back and need to create space to re-guard or attack.
    • When an opponent is driving forward with significant pressure.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Knee to Chest: When the opponent is too close, bring your knee sharply towards your chest to create space and reset your frame.
    • Hip Escape: If the opponent is pressuring a specific side, perform a hip escape to the opposite side to realign your hips and re-establish your base.
    • Guard Pull: If the opponent is attempting a takedown, transition to a deep grip and pull them into your guard, emphasizing a seated or half-guard position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to hold grips in Sitting Guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping too hard with your fingers only and not utilizing your forearm. Ensure your grip is firm but relaxed, with your forearms engaged to support the pressure. Try to maintain a neutral wrist position rather than bending it excessively backwards or forwards.

    Q: How can I use Sitting Guard effectively against a much bigger and heavier opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your focus must be on active framing and hip movement. Use your legs to create distance and prevent them from collapsing your guard. Grip their sleeves or pants and use your forearms to push their shoulders away, constantly shifting your hips to off-balance them.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from Sitting Guard to another guard like Closed Guard?

    The ideal time to transition is when the opponent is leaning in, attempting to break your grips or advance their hips. As they commit their weight forward, use your hip movement to bring your legs around their body and establish the Closed Guard, securing their posture.
