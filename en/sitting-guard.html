Sitting Guard: Your First Open Guard in BJJ

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Sitting Guard is an open guard position where you sit upright with legs extended, creating a frame to control distance and attack. It's a foundational open guard, allowing for sweeps and submissions from a stable, upright base.

White belts often struggle by collapsing their posture or overextending their limbs, losing the frame and inviting passes. They might also grip too tightly or too loosely, negating the control.

The key is maintaining a strong, upright posture with bent knees and actively using your shins and forearms to create a consistent barrier against your opponent's hips and chest.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start from a supine (lying on your back) position, knees bent, feet flat on the mat. Shift your hips back slightly.
    2. Sit up by driving your feet into the mat and lifting your hips, maintaining a neutral spine and slightly rounded upper back.
    3. Extend your legs forward, keeping a slight bend at the knees. Your feet should be flexed, toes pointing upwards.
    4. Establish grips: On the opponent's sleeves (e.g., Right Sleeve Grip, Left Sleeve Grip) or pant legs (e.g., Right Pant Grip, Left Pant Grip).
    5. Create a frame: Place your forearms on the opponent's biceps or shoulders, creating a wedge.
    6. Adjust hip angle: Slightly angle your hips to the left or right to prevent the opponent from easily stacking or passing.
    7. Maintain weight distribution: Keep your weight balanced on your hips, not your tailbone, to allow for mobility. Your shins should be parallel to the mat or angled slightly up.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Posture: Slouching or collapsing the upper back allows the opponent to drive forward and break your frame, risking a knee-on-belly or stack pass.
    • Overextending Legs: Fully straightening legs weakens the frame and leaves ankles vulnerable to leg attacks like ankle locks. Keep a slight knee bend.
    • Poor Hip Angle: Facing directly forward makes you susceptible to a stack pass. Angling hips slightly to one side creates a better defensive base and limits their forward drive.
    • Grip Failure: Gripping only the wrist or elbow is too far out. Secure grips on the sleeve or pant leg to control their limbs and posture effectively.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Mobility: Sit up, extend legs, flex feet, create frame with arms. Hold for 30 seconds. Repeat 5 times. (0% resistance)
    2. Partner Posture Drill: Opponent stands in front. You sit guard, establish sleeve grips, and practice creating a frame with forearms on their biceps. Opponent gently pushes forward. You resist by adjusting frame and hip angle. 10 reps per side. (25% resistance)
    3. Static Guard Hold: Opponent kneels in front. You establish Sitting Guard with sleeve grips. Opponent tries to step around. You maintain frame and hip angle. Hold for 30 seconds. Repeat 5 times. (50% resistance)
    4. Introduction to Sweeps: From Sitting Guard, practice transitioning to a basic scissor sweep. Opponent provides minimal resistance. 5 reps per side. (75% resistance)
    5. Controlled Pass Defense: Opponent attempts a basic knee-slice pass. You focus on maintaining frame and hip angle to prevent the pass. No submission attempts. 5 reps per side. (90% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling: Apply Sitting Guard in rolling situations, focusing on maintaining your frame and looking for sweep opportunities. Focus on positional control, not submissions. 3 x 3 minute rounds. (100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When opponent is postured up and attacking your closed guard, and you want to open it to create space for sweeps.
    • When opponent is standing and trying to pass your guard, and you want to establish an upright, defensive guard.
    • When you find yourself on your back and need to create a stable, attacking platform.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass Defense: If the opponent drives forward and attempts to stack you, immediately widen your base, push their hips away with your forearms, and angle your hips to the side to prevent them from crushing you.
    • Leg Drag Counter: If the opponent tries to peel your leg off to start a leg drag, maintain tight sleeve grips, keep your knees close to your chest, and use your free arm to push their hip away.
    • Knee Slice Counter: To defend a knee-slice pass, keep your shins tight to their hips, use your forearms to create a strong frame on their shoulders, and shrimp your hips away as they try to penetrate.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to sit up in Sitting Guard?

    Your back likely hurts because you are trying to sit up using pure spinal flexion. Instead, engage your core and drive through your heels as if you're trying to perform a hip bridge. This uses your glutes and hamstrings, taking pressure off your lower back and creating a stronger, more stable upright posture.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, your frame is paramount. Focus on keeping your knees bent and your shins tight to their hips. Actively use your forearms to push against their biceps and shoulders, creating a constant barrier. Angle your hips to the side to prevent them from driving straight forward and collapsing you.

    Q: The ideal time to transition to Sitting Guard from Closed Guard is when the opponent is postured up and trying to break your guard. By opening your guard and sitting up, you create space to implement your own offensive strategies, like sweeps or controlling distance with your frames, rather than being stuck in a defensive posture.
