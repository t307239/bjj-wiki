Mastering Sitting Guard in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Sitting Guard is an open guard position where you sit upright with your legs between you and your opponent. It allows for intricate sweeps and submissions by creating angles and control.

White belts often struggle by staying too flat or not utilizing their frames effectively, leading to passes. They must understand the importance of hip engagement.

The core principle is using your hips and legs to create a stable, mobile platform that dictates distance and angle, preventing the opponent from settling into a dominant position.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Start on your back, knees bent, feet flat on the mat. Sit up, bringing your hips slightly forward of your heels, creating a V-shape with your torso and thighs.
    2. Place your feet on the opponent's hips, shins perpendicular to the mat. Your knees should be wider than shoulder-width apart.
    3. Grip the opponent's sleeves with your hands, palms facing up, fingers pointing towards their elbows. Maintain a "frame" by keeping your elbows slightly bent, not locked.
    4. Engage your core and glutes to create tension through your legs, pushing your knees slightly outwards. This prevents them from easily collapsing your guard.
    5. Shift your weight onto your left hip to create an angle. Your right leg remains extended to maintain distance and control.
    6. Use your right foot to "push" off the opponent's hip, creating space to pivot your hips further.
    7. Simultaneously, adjust your grip to control their posture, pulling their sleeves downwards and slightly towards you. This breaks their posture and sets up attacks.
    8. Maintain a constant subtle pressure with your shins into their hips, preventing them from advancing their weight.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Sitting too upright with locked knees: This hyperextends the knee joint, risking ACL/MCL tears when the opponent applies forward pressure.
    • Gripping too loosely on sleeves: This allows the opponent to easily break your grips and pass, leading to uncontrolled scrambles and potential ankle or knee injuries from awkward falls.
    • Allowing opponent's hips to get too close without framing: This crushes your base, removes your angle, and opens you to knee-on-belly or side control transitions, risking shoulder and rib injuries from their weight.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drill: Sit-up and leg frame practice. Focus on achieving the V-shape and placing feet on hips, holding for 30 seconds. 10 reps.
    2. Solo Drill: Add sleeve grips. Practice pulling down and creating tension, maintaining the frame. 10 reps.
    3. Partner Drill (0% resistance): Opponent stands passively. Practice entering Sitting Guard, establishing grips, and maintaining posture. 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner Drill (25% resistance): Opponent applies light static pressure. Practice maintaining your frames and angles against their push. 5 reps per side.
    5. Partner Drill (50% resistance): Opponent attempts to advance their weight. Practice shucking their hips away and resetting your guard. 5 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (75% resistance): Focus exclusively on maintaining Sitting Guard and preventing passes for 2 minutes. Do not attempt submissions yet.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When an opponent attempts a guard pass by stepping in close.
    • When an opponent tries to establish a knee-on-belly or side control position from guard.
    • When you are on your back and have an opportunity to create space and establish control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass Defense: If the opponent stacks you, widen your knees and push outwards with your shins. Simultaneously, grab their head with one hand and their opposite arm with the other, pulling them forward to break their posture and create space to recover guard.
    • Knee Slice Pass Defense: As they attempt to bring their knee across, use your opposite leg to "hook" their thigh and pull it away. Simultaneously, frame with your other arm on their hip, creating distance and allowing you to shrimp out and re-establish your guard.
    • Toreando Pass Defense: If they try to circle around, use your leg frames to keep them at distance. Grab their far sleeve and pull them towards you, then pivot your hips to face them and re-establish control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to do Sitting Guard?

    Your back pain likely stems from rounding your spine in an attempt to sit up. Instead, focus on engaging your core and glutes to support your torso. Think of maintaining a slight "arch" in your lower back, like you're trying to push your belly button towards the mat, while simultaneously pushing your hips forward slightly.

    Q: How can I use Sitting Guard effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is key. Focus on maintaining frames with your forearms on their biceps and your shins on their hips to control distance and prevent them from collapsing your guard. Use your hip mobility to create angles and off-balance them, rather than relying on pure strength.

    Q: You should transition out of Sitting Guard when your opponent has established a strong base and is pressuring heavily, making it difficult to maintain angles or create opportunities. The most common mistake is staying too long and allowing them to settle into a dominant passing position without attempting to improve your own.
