Back defense in BJJ is about preventing an opponent from securing dominant control from behind you, typically the Back Mount position.

White belts often struggle by panicking, creating space, or turning their back further, which is the opposite of what's needed.

The core principle is to connect your body to the mat and create frames to deny the opponent's hooks and chest-to-chest pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting Position: Assume a defensive stance on your hands and knees, facing away from the opponent who is attempting to establish back control.
    2. Elbows In: Tuck your elbows tightly to your sides, preventing the opponent from easily sliding their arms under your armpits.
    3. Chin Tucked: Lower your head and tuck your chin towards your chest, protecting your neck from chokes and making your head a less accessible target.
    4. Hip Angle: Keep your hips low and heavy, almost scraping the mat. Avoid lifting your hips, which creates space for the opponent's hooks.
    5. Frame Creation (Arms): Extend your forearms and hands outwards, creating a 'frame' between your body and the opponent's chest. This is not about pushing with force, but about establishing a solid barrier.
    6. Foot Placement: Keep your feet flat on the mat, with knees slightly wider than shoulder-width for base. Avoid crossing your ankles.
    7. Weight Distribution: Distribute your weight evenly over your hands and knees, maintaining a stable base. Avoid leaning too far forward or backward.
    8. Scapular Retraction: Gently pull your shoulder blades together and down, creating a stronger, more stable upper body structure against pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Error: Lifting hips off the mat to 'escape' pressure. Damage: This creates space for the opponent to secure their hooks deeper, leading to a higher risk of rear-naked choke or body triangle entrapment. Correction: Keep hips low and heavy, focus on framing with arms and tucking chin.
    • Error: Turning head and neck outwards to 'see' the opponent. Damage: This exposes the carotid artery and increases the risk of a rear-naked choke or guillotine choke. It also compromises your base. Correction: Tuck chin to chest and use peripheral vision, keeping head protected.
    • Error: Spreading arms wide to create 'space'. Damage: This opens lanes for the opponent to insert hooks, secure their chest-to-chest connection, and isolate your limbs for attacks like armbars. Correction: Keep elbows tucked and use forearms to create a tight frame.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Movement (0%): Practice the defensive posture on your knees: tuck chin, bring elbows in, lower hips, spread hands slightly. Repeat 10 times.
    2. Partner Drill - Static Position (25%): Partner sits on your back (no hooks, no pressure). Practice tucking chin, elbows in, and creating frames with forearms against their chest. Hold for 15 seconds, repeat 5 times.
    3. Partner Drill - Light Pressure (50%): Partner applies very light pressure, no hooks. Focus on maintaining the defensive posture, resisting their attempts to flatten you. Hold for 20 seconds, repeat 5 times.
    4. Partner Drill - Hook Insertion (75%): Partner attempts to insert one hook. Focus on preventing it by keeping hips low and framing. If hook is in, focus on immediately tucking chin and creating frames. Hold for 30 seconds, repeat 5 times.
    5. Partner Drill - Simulated Back Mount (90%): Partner attempts to establish back mount, but you actively work to prevent the second hook and chest-to-chest. Focus on escaping to a defensive position on your knees. 1 minute rounds, 3 rounds.
    6. Live Rolling (100%): Focus on implementing back defense whenever your back is taken. Prioritize survival and escape over offensive moves. 3 minute rounds, 3 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent has secured hooks from behind.
    • When an opponent is driving their chest into your back.
    • When you feel your back is exposed and the opponent is moving to take mount from behind.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Turtle Up: Bring knees to chest, tuck chin, and cover your head with your forearms, creating a compact defensive shell. This is a temporary measure to prevent immediate submission and reset.
    • Bridge and Roll (Carefully): If the opponent is not fully controlling your hips, attempt a powerful bridge towards the side of their controlling hook, then roll to relieve pressure and potentially escape or achieve a better position. Requires good timing and hip mobility.
    • Wall Walk/Escape: If near a wall or cage, use it to your advantage. Push off the wall with your feet and drive your hips up to stand, breaking the opponent's grip and creating space to turn and face them.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to defend my back in BJJ?

    Your neck is likely hurting because you are overextending your cervical spine or allowing the opponent to get a dominant grip on your head and neck. When defending, always tuck your chin firmly to your chest. This protects your airway and prevents hyperextension. Avoid turning your head outwards; use peripheral vision to track the opponent while keeping your head protected.

    Q: How do I defend my back against a much bigger and stronger BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, your goal is to be as heavy and compact as possible, minimizing the space they can exploit. Keep your hips low and glued to the mat, tuck your elbows tightly to your sides, and create frames with your forearms. Focus on preventing them from establishing strong chest-to-chest pressure and securing both hooks. Your primary objective is survival and creating an opportunity to escape to a neutral position.

    Q: When should I stop trying to defend my back and just focus on escaping?

    You should shift your focus entirely to escaping once the opponent has secured dominant control, such as both hooks and a strong chest-to-chest connection, and you cannot effectively create frames. If you are unable to prevent them from driving forward or are in immediate danger of a submission like a rear-naked choke, prioritize breaking their grip and creating space to get to your knees or escape their control. Survival is paraMount.
