Back defense is crucial when an opponent secures a dominant position on your back, aiming for chokes or control.

White belts often panic and expose their necks or limbs, making escapes harder.

The key is maintaining a tight, compact posture while creating frames to prevent further advancement.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. If opponent has seatbelt grip (one arm over shoulder, one under armpit) and hooks are in:
    • Do NOT arch your back. Keep your spine neutral.
    • Turn your hips slightly towards the side of the arm that is under your armpit.
    1. Place your forearm (the one closer to the opponent's underarm) on their hip bone, creating a frame.
    • This frame prevents them from driving their hips into yours and tightening their control.
    1. Simultaneously, bring your knees towards your chest, tucking your chin.
    • This compact posture protects your neck and makes it harder for them to isolate limbs.
    1. With your framing forearm, push their hip away slightly as you begin to turn.
    • This creates a micro-space to initiate movement.
    1. Rotate your body towards the side of the framing arm, keeping your knees tucked.
    • Imagine 'rolling' your hips towards the mat.
    1. As you turn, focus on bringing your hips off the mat and establishing a base.
    • Your goal is to break their hooks and create an angle.
    1. Once your hips are off the mat and you have an angle, you can attempt to shrimp out or transition to a defensive guard.
    • Maintain your frame and keep your elbows tight to your body.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Arching your back aggressively: This can cause hyperextension of the lumbar spine, leading to acute back pain or disc injury.
    • Correct: Maintain a neutral spine and focus on hip movement.
    • Tucking your chin too far or too little: Tucking too far can restrict breathing and make it impossible to create space; not tucking enough exposes your carotid artery to a choke.
    • Correct: Keep your chin slightly tucked, enough to protect your neck but allowing for head movement.
    • Spreading your elbows wide while on your back: This opens up your arms for armbars or kimuras and makes it harder to frame effectively.
    • Correct: Keep your elbows tight to your ribs, forming a compact defensive shell.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip turn and frame creation motion without a partner. Focus on hip angle and frame placement. (10 reps each side)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Have a partner lightly hold your back with hooks in. Practice creating the frame and turning your hips. (5 reps each side)
    3. Partner drill (light control): Partner maintains seatbelt grip and hooks, offering minimal resistance. Focus on executing the turn. (5 reps each side)
    4. Partner drill (active hooks): Partner actively tries to maintain hooks while you practice the escape. (5 reps each side)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the escape during live rolling, with your partner defending your escape attempt passively. (2 minutes)
    6. Live rolling (full resistance): Apply the back defense techniques during live rolling with full resistance. (5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Opponent secures seatbelt grip with hooks in from behind.
    • Opponent has established a body triangle from the back.
    • Opponent is actively trying to flatten you out from the back.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Frame and Turn: As described in biomechanics. Place forearm on opponent's hip, turn hips towards the side of the framing arm, and shrimp out.
    • Chin Tuck and Elbow Protection: Keep chin tucked to chest, elbows tight to ribs to prevent chokes and arm attacks, while looking for opportunities to turn or bridge.
    • Bridging and Turning Towards Opponent's Legs: If opponent's hooks are not deep, a powerful bridge combined with a turn towards their legs can break their base and create space to escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to defend from the back in BJJ?

    Neck pain often stems from arching your back excessively instead of using your hips. When you arch, you create a gap that allows the opponent to sink their choke deeper, constricting your airway and straining your cervical spine. Always focus on tucking your chin and using your forearm to frame against their hip to create space, rather than just arching.

    Q: How do I defend my back against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on creating an impenetrable frame with your forearms against their hips and ribs. Keep your elbows tight to your body to prevent them from flattening you out. Your primary goal is not to escape immediately, but to survive the position by staying compact and preventing them from tightening their grips or landing submissions. Look for small openings to turn your hips and create angles.

    Q: When should I try to escape my back, and when should I just defend?

    You should actively attempt to escape your back when you feel you have created a small aMount of space, such as by establishing a frame or turning your hips. If your opponent is completely dominant, has a tight choke applied, or has flattened you out with no room to maneuver, your priority shifts to pure defense. Focus on tucking your chin, protecting your neck and arms, and waiting for the opponent to make a mistake or for a brief moment of opportunity to move.
