Essential Back Defense Techniques for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Back defense is the crucial set of actions to prevent your opponent from securing a dominant position, specifically the Back Mount, and applying submissions like the rear-naked choke.

White belts often fail by panicking, turning into the opponent's pressure, or forgetting fundamental body positioning, leading to immediate submission or positional disadvantage.

The core principle is maintaining a strong, compact base and actively creating frames with your forearms and elbows to prevent the opponent's hooks from penetrating, and to create space to turn.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Preventing the Seatbelt Grip: If the opponent has one arm over your shoulder (e.g., right arm), immediately bring your right forearm across your chest, creating a perpendicular frame against their bicep. Simultaneously, tuck your chin to your chest, preventing head access.
    2. Establishing Forearm Frames: As the opponent attempts to secure their second hook (e.g., left leg), position your left forearm and elbow firmly against their hip bone, creating a strong frame. Your hips should be slightly elevated off the mat, pushing into their frame.
    3. Hip Angle for Turning: To escape, you need to turn towards the side of the opponent's trapped arm (the side with their arm over your shoulder). This involves rotating your hips 90 degrees, so your right hip points towards their left hip.
    4. Creating Space with Elbows: While maintaining your forearm frames, actively drive your elbows outwards and slightly upwards, creating a small pocket of space between your body and theirs. This space is critical for initiating the turn.
    5. The 'Shrimp' Motion: To complete the turn, perform a modified shrimp. Push off the mat with your left foot (the foot on the side you are turning towards) and simultaneously drive your hips in that direction, using your forearm frames to guide your body away.
    6. Securing the Turn: As you rotate, keep your head down and your chin tucked. Aim to end up facing the opponent, ideally in a guard position or a neutral position, rather than facing away again.
    7. Hand Fighting: Throughout this process, if the opponent attempts to grab your collar or sleeve, use your free hand to peel their grips off by pushing their wrist away from your neck or body with a firm, direct pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Head Movement: Turning your head upwards or away from the opponent's choking arm exposes your carotid artery and neck, allowing them to secure a rear-naked choke more easily. The correct movement is to tuck your chin firmly to your chest, protecting your neck.
    • Knee Pressure on the Spine: If you try to 'bridge' or 'buck' upwards without proper hip control when the opponent has hooks in, your lower back can hyperextend and compress your lumbar spine. The correct movement is to create frames and turn your hips.
    • Elbow 'Chicken Winging': Tucking your elbows in tightly and lifting them upwards when the opponent is pressing into your back can cause your shoulder joint to become vulnerable to a kimura or Americana if they isolate your arm. The correct movement is to keep elbows slightly flared outwards to create frames, not tucked in.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Frame Practice (0% Resistance): Lie on your back. Practice establishing forearm frames against an imaginary opponent's hips and shoulders. Focus on the 90-degree angle of your forearms and the tightness of your core. 50 reps.
    2. Solo Turning Drill (0% Resistance): From a simulated back mount position (imagine hands on your shoulders), practice the hip rotation and shrimp motion to turn towards the 'opponent's' trapped arm. Focus on smooth hip movement. 50 reps.
    3. Partner Drill - Static Frames (25% Resistance): Your partner lies on their back. You sit on their back and attempt to secure hooks. They practice establishing forearm frames and preventing you from getting a deep seatbelt. They get 1 minute to resist, then switch. 5 rounds.
    4. Partner Drill - Static Turn (50% Resistance): Your partner is on their back, you have hooks in. They practice the turn sequence. You offer minimal resistance, allowing them to feel the movement. They get 1 minute to escape, then switch. 5 rounds.
    5. Partner Drill - Controlled Escape (75% Resistance): Your partner is on their back, you have hooks and a seatbelt grip. They attempt to escape. You actively defend the choke but allow them to work the frames and turn. They get 2 minutes to escape. 5 rounds.
    6. Live Rolling - Back Defense Focus (90% Resistance): In rolling, one partner starts in back mount and attempts to submit. The other partner's sole focus is on defending the back and escaping. No other submissions allowed for the defender. 3 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT BACK DEFENSE:
    • Immediately when you feel the opponent securing hooks from a standing position or transition.
    • When the opponent achieves a dominant back mount position in rolling or sparring.
    • When you are on your back and the opponent is attempting to take your back from guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS (DEFENSES):
    • Forearm Frame & Hip Escape: As described in biomechanics. The key is to create a rigid frame with your forearm against their hip and use your hips to push away, creating space. Your hips should angle away from their body.
    • Chin Tuck & Elbow Protection: Maintain a tight chin tuck to your chest, creating a barrier. Simultaneously, keep your elbows slightly out and angled to prevent them from being trapped or isolated, forming a defensive shell.
    • Turn to the Trapped Side: The most effective escape is to turn your body towards the side where the opponent's arm is trapped across your chest or shoulder. This uses their own limb against them and opens up space for you to re-establish guard or return to a neutral position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try to defend my back in BJJ?

    Your neck likely hurts because you are not tucking your chin effectively. When defending the back, the opponent aims to get their arm under your chin to apply pressure. If you keep your chin up or flat, their forearm presses directly onto your trachea and neck. Always tuck your chin firmly to your chest, creating a strong barrier that forces their arm to press against your shoulder or collarbone instead of your vulnerable neck.

    Q: How do I defend my back against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise framing are paraMount. Instead of trying to push their weight away directly, focus on creating small, strong frames with your forearms and elbows against their hips and shoulders. Use your hips to angle away, creating space. The key is to make yourself a difficult target by maintaining a compact structure and using their weight against them by turning towards their trapped arm, rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: When should I start defending my back, and how do I know if I'm too late?

    You should start defending your back the moment you feel a hook being established or an arm reaching for your neck. The 'too late' moment is when the opponent has both hooks secured and a tight seatbelt grip, with their chest pressed firmly against your back. At this point, your primary goal shifts from escaping to surviving and preventing the choke, often by focusing on breaking their grip and creating space to turn towards the side of their trapped arm.
