The Elevator Sweep is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to elevate your opponent and then sweep them to your side. White belts often struggle by pushing instead of pulling, creating a weak base. The key is using your hips to drive upward while controlling their posture.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a deep cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (forearm or wrist) on their left arm with your left hand. Maintain a closed guard, knees tucked to chest.
    2. Shift your hips slightly to your left, creating a small angle. Keep your spine pressed into their chest.
    3. Begin to drive your hips upwards, lifting your opponent's base off the mat. Your knees should drive towards your chest.
    4. As their base lifts, simultaneously pull their left arm across your body with your left hand and pull down on the collar with your right hand.
    5. Rotate your hips to your left, guiding their elevated arm and upper body in that direction.
    6. As they fall, extend your left leg to the ceiling, creating an 'elevator' motion to help lift them higher.
    7. Release the collar grip and transition to a knee-on-belly or side control position as they land.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to stand up and push the opponent's hips away: This can lead to a strained lower back, as the lumbar spine is hyperextended. Instead, drive your hips upward from a seated position.
    • Gripping too low on the sleeve or pants: This provides insufficient control and can lead to a wrist sprain (ulnar collateral ligament) if the opponent pulls hard or a shoulder strain (rotator cuff) if you yank incorrectly. Grip the forearm or wrist area.
    • Allowing the opponent to stack you by not lifting hips enough: This can result in a hyperextended neck or a compressed spine. Focus on the upward hip drive as the primary lifting mechanism.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip lift and arm pull motions without a partner for 5 minutes, focusing on spinal extension and hip flexion.
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sweep sequence, focusing on grip placement and hip drive. 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner lightly resists the hip lift, allowing you to feel the mechanics. 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner offers mild posture control, requiring you to actively pull and drive. 10 reps per side.
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent the sweep, requiring precise timing and grip strength. 10 reps per side.
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Apply the sweep in sparring, focusing on execution and recovery if it fails. 5 attempts.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is postured up and close, making it difficult for them to base out.
    • When you have secured strong collar and sleeve grips, controlling their posture.
    • When the opponent is attempting to pass your guard and their weight is forward.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stacking: If the opponent attempts to stack you, immediately tuck your chin to your chest and drive your hips into their chest, creating a frame with your legs.
    • Base out: If the opponent starts to lift you, immediately try to widen your base by extending your legs and pushing their hips away.
    • Grip break: If the opponent breaks your collar grip, immediately transition to a double sleeve grip or a pants grip to maintain control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my lower back hurt when I try the Elevator Sweep in BJJ?

    Your lower back likely hurts because you are trying to stand up and push the opponent's hips away with your legs, instead of driving your hips upwards from a seated position. Focus on the biomechanics of hip flexion and extension to generate the lift, keeping your spine relatively neutral.

    Q: Against a larger opponent, the Elevator Sweep relies heavily on timing and leverage. Secure your collar and sleeve grips with maximum tension, pulling their posture down before initiating the hip lift. Use your core to drive your hips up sharply, and consider transitioning to a knee-on-belly immediately upon successful sweep to maintain positional advantage.

    Q: The optimal time is when the opponent is postured up and leaning into your closed guard, making it difficult for them to effectively base out. If the sweep fails, immediately transition to a strong guard retention position like a closed guard or butterfly guard. Do not stay in a compromised position; focus on resetting your guard and looking for another opportunity.
