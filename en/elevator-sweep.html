The Elevator Sweep is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to lift and unbalance your opponent to a position of dominance.

Many white belts struggle by relying on raw strength instead of leverage, leading to inefficient movement.

The key is understanding how to create a fulcrum with your hips and drive your knees upwards to elevate your opponent's base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a double sleeve grip: Left hand grips opponent's right sleeve at the wrist, right hand grips opponent's left sleeve at the wrist. Keep forearms tight to opponent's biceps.
    2. Hook opponent's hips with your feet: Place the soles of your feet on opponent's hips, knees bent to 90 degrees, feet hip-width apart. This establishes your base and prevents them from stacking you.
    3. Shrimp your hips to the side: Simultaneously, drive your right hip outwards and slightly upwards, creating a diagonal line from your right hip to your left foot. Your left knee should angle slightly towards your opponent's left hip.
    4. Create frame with your arms: Extend your forearms, pushing against opponent's biceps to create space and prevent them from collapsing onto you. Maintain a slight tension.
    5. Initiate the 'elevator' motion: With your hips elevated and right arm driving opponent's left bicep, explosively extend your left leg while simultaneously driving your right knee upwards towards your chest. Your hips should lift your opponent.
    6. Drive upward and pivot: As opponent's weight shifts upwards, continue driving your right knee and hip upwards, pivoting your body to face your opponent's hips.
    7. Secure top position: As they fall forward, transition immediately to a dominant top position like side control or mount by posting your right hand on the mat and driving your hips forward.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to lift opponent solely by pulling sleeves: This strains the shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) and can lead to rotator cuff tears or dislocations. The correct movement involves hip elevation and knee drive, not just arm pulling.
    • Arching your back excessively to generate power: This places immense stress on the lumbar spine, risking herniated discs or muscle strains. Maintain a neutral spine and use leg and hip drive instead.
    • Allowing opponent to stack your hips: If your feet are too close or you don't shrimp effectively, opponent can drive their hips into yours, compressing your pelvis and potentially injuring your hip flexors or pubic bone. Maintain a wide base and active hip movement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill (no partner): Practice shrimping, hip elevation, and knee drive motions without a partner. Focus on the feeling of lifting your hips. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (static): Partner lies on their back in your closed guard. Practice grips, shrimping, and hip elevation to lift their hips off the mat. Partner remains still. (20 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner actively tries to prevent you from shrimping and lifting by posturing up. You focus on creating space and executing the sweep. (25% resistance, 30 reps)
    4. Partner drill (controlled movement): Partner actively defends with light pressure, allowing you to feel the timing and mechanics of the sweep. They offer minimal resistance to the sweep itself once initiated. (50% resistance, 40 reps)
    5. Sparring (controlled): Spar with a partner where the goal is to practice sweeps. Focus on executing the Elevator Sweep when the opportunity arises. Communicate and stop if something feels wrong. (75% resistance, 5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Elevator Sweep during regular sparring. Be mindful of your partner's reactions and be prepared to transition or defend. (100% resistance, 5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • Opponent's posture is upright: When they are postured high, their base is less stable and easier to elevate.
    • Opponent is leaning forward: This forward weight distribution makes them more susceptible to being lifted off their base.
    • Opponent is trying to pass your guard and their weight is on their toes: This indicates a forward lean which can be exploited.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Stack Pass Defense: If opponent attempts to stack pass by driving their weight forward and down, immediately widen your guard, post your feet on their hips, and drive your hips up to meet their pressure, creating a strong frame. Do not allow your hips to be compressed.
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: If they start to fall to one side after your sweep attempt, immediately hip escape away from their direction of fall and re-establish your guard or transition to a more advantageous position like open guard.
    • Leg Pummeling to Prevent Sweep: If they feel the sweep coming, they may try to pummel their legs out to the side to stabilize. Be ready to switch to a leg drag or other control position if they successfully remove a leg.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try the Elevator Sweep?

    Your back likely hurts because you are trying to lift your opponent using your back muscles instead of your hips and legs. When you arch your back excessively, you place undue stress on your lumbar spine. Instead, focus on driving your hips UPWARD towards the ceiling and extending your legs, using your glutes and hamstrings for power.

    Q: How can I Elevator Sweep a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, raw lifting strength is less effective. Focus on creating a significant leverage advantage. Ensure your feet are firmly planted on their hips, and use your shrimping motion to create a sharp angle. The upward drive of your knee and hip, combined with a precise upward pull on the sleeves, will exploit their center of gravity more effectively than trying to muscle them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Elevator Sweep?

    The Elevator Sweep is best attempted when your opponent is postured high and their weight is forward, making them unstable. If they are leaning heavily on their toes or attempting to break your guard by driving their hips down, it's a prime opportunity. Avoid attempting it when they are flat on their back or have their weight distributed evenly.
