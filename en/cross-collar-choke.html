Cross Collar Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This choke is typically applied from the Closed Guard position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries.

Many white belts struggle because they rely on brute strength, failing to understand the leverage points and precise grip placement.

The core principle is creating a constricting frame with your forearms and the opponent's own collar, using your hips to generate the final pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish two "lapel grip" with your right and left hands, gripping the opponent's left and right lapels respectively, approximately 2-3 inches from the opponent's neck.
    2. Ensure your grip depth is consistent and firm, fingers curled around the fabric.
    3. Bring your hips up slightly, creating a small space to begin framing.
    4. Place your left forearm across the opponent's upper chest, just below their collarbone, creating a frame.
    5. Simultaneously, place your right forearm underneath the opponent's chin, pressing it into their throat.
    6. Arch your back and drive your hips upwards, creating a posterior tilt of your pelvis.
    7. Squeeze your elbows together, driving your forearms inward towards each other, compressing the lapels against the sides of the opponent's neck.
    8. Maintain a strong base by keeping your feet hooked together behind the opponent's back.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive downward pressure with your arms on the opponent's head: This can cause hyperextension of the cervical spine, leading to neck sprains or worse. The correct action is to drive your hips upwards and squeeze your elbows inward.
    • Gripping too low on the lapels, near the opponent's sternum: This often results in a weak grip and allows the opponent to posture up easily, potentially causing wrist strain from over-extension. Grip higher, closer to the neck.
    • Forcing the choke by pulling the opponent's head down with your arms: This can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strain as you use your arms as levers against their neck. The power comes from hip drive, not arm pulling.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice establishing the grips and forearm frames on a training dummy or a willing partner without any resistance (0% resistance). Perform 10 repetitions.
    2. Partner drill (no movement): Your partner lies on their back, you are in closed guard. Practice establishing grips, forearm frames, hip lift, and elbow squeeze. No resistance (0% resistance). Perform 10 repetitions.
    3. Partner drill (light movement): Your partner attempts to break your guard or posture up slightly. You focus on maintaining grips and frames, and executing the hip lift and squeeze. 25% resistance. Perform 5 repetitions.
    4. Partner drill (controlled execution): Your partner allows you to execute the choke mechanics. Focus on the hip drive and elbow squeeze. 50% resistance. Perform 5 repetitions.
    5. Controlled sparring: Spar with a partner from closed guard. Attempt the choke when the opportunity arises, focusing on timing and grip integrity. 75% resistance. Perform 3 attempts.
    6. Live sparring: Spar with a partner, looking for opportunities to apply the Cross Collar Choke. Focus on correct execution under pressure. 90% resistance. Perform 2 attempts.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is postured down in your closed guard, their head is within reach.
    • When the opponent is attempting to pass your guard and comes within close range.
    • When the opponent is turtled and you have an opportunity to secure a lapel grip.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Posture Up and Frame: The opponent breaks your grip by posturing up, creating space with their hips and shoulders, and driving their elbows into your forearms to break your frames.
    • Grip Release and Elbow Escape: The opponent uses their hands to peel your grips off their lapels, then drives their elbows to the mat, creating space to escape their guard.
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: The opponent escapes your closed guard by hip escaping and creating distance, then re-establishes a guard pass position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Cross Collar Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you grip too low on the lapels, near the opponent's chest, or when you try to pull their head down forcefully with your arms. This puts excessive torque on your wrists. To fix this, ensure your grips are higher, closer to the opponent's neck, and that the primary force for the choke comes from driving your hips upwards and squeezing your elbows together, not pulling.

    Q: How can I effectively Cross Collar Choke a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, brute strength is your enemy. Focus on precise grip placement, ensuring your hands are deep and high on their lapels, near their neck. Use your hips to create the initial space and leverage, then drive upwards. Their size means they have more mass to compress, so a tight frame with your forearms and a strong hip drive are crucial for cutting off blood flow.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Cross Collar Choke from closed guard, and how do I know if it's working?

    The best time is when the opponent is slightly postured down, making their neck accessible. You'll know it's working when their face starts to turn red, their breathing becomes labored, and they begin to struggle significantly or tap. If they are easily posturing up or creating space, your grips might be too loose, or you're not using your hips effectively.
