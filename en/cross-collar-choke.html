This choke originates from the Closed Guard position, aiming to submit your opponent by restricting blood flow to the brain.

Many white belts struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage, often relying on brute strength which is inefficient and risky.

The key insight is using your opponent's own posture and your hip rotation to create the necessary pressure, not just pulling.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish Closed Guard: Sit with your back on the mat, legs wrapped around your opponent's torso, ankles crossed.
    2. Secure Right Collar Grip: Reach your right hand deep into your opponent's right collar, thumb down, palm facing your body.
    3. Secure Left Collar Grip: Reach your left hand deep into your opponent's left collar, thumb down, palm facing your body.
    4. Create Posture Break: With both hands on the collars, pull your opponent's head downwards towards your chest, simultaneously driving your hips slightly upwards.
    5. Initiate Hip Rotation: Rotate your hips to your right (clockwise), bringing your right shoulder towards your opponent's left shoulder.
    6. Apply Choking Pressure: As you rotate, arch your back and squeeze your knees together, creating a fulcrum with your hips and driving your right elbow downwards.
    7. Maintain Head Control: Keep your opponent's head pinned to your chest throughout the movement to prevent them from posturing up.
    8. Finish: Continue the hip rotation and back arch until your opponent taps.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Grip Depth: Gripping too shallowly on the collar. This can lead to the grip slipping, causing a sudden loss of control and potentially a shoulder strain for the attacker if they over-rotate.
    • Excessive Arm Pulling: Trying to choke solely by pulling the collars with straight arms. This puts immense strain on the rotator cuff and biceps, risking tears, and is biomechanically inefficient.
    • Ignoring Hip Rotation: Focusing only on pulling the head down without rotating the hips. This fails to generate sufficient leverage, leading to a weak choke and risking hyperextension of the attacker's own spine.
    • Knee Pressure Misalignment: Pressing knees directly into the opponent's ribs without proper hip angle. This can cause rib fractures for the opponent and strain the attacker's adductor muscles.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip Practice: Practice reaching and securing both collar grips on a training dummy or a willing partner's gi, focusing on depth and thumb position. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Posture Break & Hip Setup: From closed guard, practice breaking posture and initiating the initial hip rotation without any choking pressure. (25 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Controlled Choke Execution: With a partner in closed guard, practice the full sequence of grips, posture break, hip rotation, and pressure application with minimal resistance. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Half-Speed Choke Application: Execute the Cross Collar Choke at half speed, allowing your partner to feel the pressure build and tap early. Focus on smooth transitions. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Full Speed Choke with Tap: Perform the technique at full speed, aiming for a submission, but with your partner ready to tap immediately when they feel significant pressure. (10 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling Application: Attempt the Cross Collar Choke during live rolling, but prioritize positional control and understanding when the opportunity arises rather than forcing it. (5 attempts per rolling session, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent has their head close to your chest in your closed guard.
    • When your opponent attempts to posture up aggressively from your closed guard.
    • When your opponent frames poorly, leaving their neck exposed to your collar grips.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Escape Guard by Posturing Up: If the opponent is attempting the choke, immediately drive your hips back and posture up, creating space and breaking their leg control. This relieves the pressure on the neck arteries.
    • Defend Grips by Breaking Posture: If you feel the collar grips being established, immediately try to pull your head up and away, and reach for your opponent's arms to break their grip or create space.
    • Stall and Wait for Referee Intervention: In a competition setting, if caught, remain calm, don't panic, and try to survive until the referee can separate you. Focus on breathing through your nose.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Cross Collar Choke?

    Wrist pain often stems from gripping the collar with your palm facing upwards or your wrist bent at an extreme angle. Ensure your thumbs are down deep in the collar and your palms are facing your own body, creating a neutral wrist position. Also, avoid pulling solely with your arms; use your hips and core to generate the choking power.

    Q: How can I perform the Cross Collar Choke against a bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, you must focus more on leveraging their weight and posture against them. Instead of trying to pull them down, use your hip rotation and back arch to create a powerful lever. Ensure your grips are deep, and when you rotate your hips, drive your elbow down sharply to cut off blood flow.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Cross Collar Choke in BJJ?

    The Cross Collar Choke is most effective when your opponent is close to your chest, often when they are trying to pass your guard or are stuck in a compromised posture. If they are posturing up high or have significant space, the choke will be much harder to finish. Look for moments of stillness or when they are leaning into you.
