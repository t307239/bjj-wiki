This choke is initiated from Closed Guard, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries. It's a fundamental submission for beginners.

Many white belts struggle because they rely on raw strength and improper body positioning, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Focus on leverage, not brute force.

The core mechanical principle is creating a 'vise' effect by using your opponent's own collar against their neck, amplified by your hip and arm positioning.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a closed guard position, ensuring your legs are locked around your opponent's waist, ankles crossed behind their back. Maintain a relatively upright posture.
    2. Reach your right hand deep into your opponent's left lapel, gripping as far back as possible, thumb inside the collar.
    3. Reach your left hand deep into your opponent's right lapel, gripping as far back as possible, thumb inside the collar. Ensure your thumbs are both inside the lapels.
    4. Pull your opponent's head down towards your chest by drawing their lapels towards you. This breaks their posture and brings their neck closer.
    5. Shift your hips slightly to your left, angling your body 45 degrees away from your opponent's right side. This creates a fulcrum.
    6. Drive your right elbow down towards your hip, while simultaneously pulling your left elbow towards your left shoulder. This creates rotational pressure.
    7. Squeeze your knees together and arch your lower back slightly, tightening the choking mechanism. Maintain this pressure until submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grabbing the very edge of the lapel: This provides insufficient material to create choke pressure, leading to excessive force applied to the neck itself. This can strain the trachea and esophagus, causing pain and potential injury.
    • Bending arms in a 'chicken wing' motion: This dissipates the force and puts strain on the shoulder joint and rotator cuff. The correct movement involves driving one elbow down and pulling the other towards the shoulder.
    • Forcing the head down without breaking posture: This allows the opponent to maintain their posture and resist effectively, putting strain on your own neck and back. Focus on pulling the lapels to break their posture first.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo grip practice: Sit in closed guard on the mat, practice reaching and securing deep lapel grips with thumbs inside. (20 reps)
    2. Solo body positioning: From closed guard, practice the hip shift and slight angle change without a partner. (20 reps)
    3. Partnered static drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner in your guard, practice establishing grips and the hip shift, holding the position. (10 reps)
    4. Partnered dynamic drill (25% resistance): Practice executing the choke from the initial setup, with partner offering minimal resistance to the choke itself. (10 reps)
    5. Flow drilling (50% resistance): Practice transitioning into the choke from a neutral closed guard position, allowing partner to defend passively. (5 rounds, 1 minute each)
    6. Live rolling (75-100% resistance): Attempt the choke during rolling, focusing on proper mechanics and timing, accepting that some attempts will be defended.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up and their weight is on your hips.
    • When you have secured deep lapel grips and can control their head.
    • When they attempt to stack you or pass your guard from a close distance.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape the guard: The opponent can simply open your guard by placing their hands on your hips, widening their base, and pushing their hips away. This breaks the choke setup.
    • Posture up and relieve pressure: The opponent can straighten their posture, reducing the angle and leverage available for the choke. They can also use their hands to push your arms away.
    • Grip break and counter-submission: A skilled opponent can break your lapel grips by prying your thumbs out or by reaching inside your arms to peel your hands off. This often leads to a counter-attack like an armbar or kimura.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Cross Collar Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs because you are gripping too shallowly in the lapel, or your thumbs are not properly inside the collar. This forces your wrist to bend at an unnatural angle to maintain the grip. Ensure your thumbs are inside the fabric of the lapel, and try to keep your wrist straighter, using the palm and forearm to apply pressure.

    Q: How can I apply the Cross Collar Choke effectively against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, raw strength is your enemy. Focus intensely on breaking their posture by pulling their head down with your lapel grips *before* attempting the choke. Use your hip angle to create leverage; don't try to squeeze with your arms alone. Your legs in closed guard are your primary control mechanism; keep them tight and active.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Cross Collar Choke, and what if my opponent just pulls their head out?

    The Cross Collar Choke is best attempted when your opponent is postured up and you have secured deep, thumb-in lapel grips. If they pull their head out, it often means you didn't break their posture sufficiently or your hip angle wasn't sharp enough. Immediately reset by re-establishing your grips and driving their head down again, or transition to another sweep or submission.
