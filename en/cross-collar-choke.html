Precision Cross Collar Choke Technique for BJJ White Belts

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This choke is primarily initiated from the Closed Guard position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain. It is a fundamental lapel choke that can yield quick submissions when executed correctly.

White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength rather than leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. They may also fail to establish the correct body positioning, making the choke easy to defend.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to create a slight angle and your arms to create a "cutting" motion, compressing the carotid arteries against the opponent's own neck structure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, establish a standard "Spider Guard" grip: right hand grips opponent's left lapel deeply, left hand grips opponent's right lapel deeply. Ensure knuckles point towards the ceiling for maximum depth.
    2. Simultaneously, drive your hips slightly to your left, creating a 15-degree angle relative to your opponent's spine. Keep your knees tight to your opponent's hips, preventing them from posturing up.
    3. "Walk" your hips back, creating a small space. This allows you to "pull" their head down and slightly forward into your chest with your lapel grips.
    4. As their head comes down, use your left forearm to block their right shoulder, preventing them from creating space or turning into you.
    5. With your right hand's lapel grip, begin to "saw" or "cut" your forearm across their neck, moving from left to right.
    6. Simultaneously, pull your left elbow down towards your hip, creating tension with the left lapel grip and further compressing the carotid artery on the right side of their neck.
    7. Maintain a tight closed guard throughout the entire sequence, keeping your heels connected and hips mobile to control their base and prevent them from posturing up.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Neck Crank Risk: Applying pressure with the forearm directly across the trapezius muscle instead of the carotid artery can lead to a painful neck crank. The correct movement is to ensure your forearm is snug against the side of the neck, aiming for the artery, not the muscle bulk.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Gripping the lapel too shallowly and attempting to "pull" with the entire arm can cause your own shoulder joint to impinge. The correct grip is deep, and the movement is a "cutting" or "sawing" motion of the forearm, not a pull with the bicep.
    • Elbow Tendonitis: Using excessive wrist pronation to try and "torque" the choke can strain your wrist and elbow tendons. The correct biomechanics involve keeping your wrists neutral and using the "cutting" motion of the forearm and the downward pull of the opposite elbow to generate the choke pressure.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip and Body Position Drill: Practice establishing the grips and hip angle with no opponent. Focus on the feel of the deep lapel grips and the 15-degree hip angle. (50 reps)
    2. Static Opponent Drill (0% Resistance): With a partner lying passively on their back, practice the entire sequence from closed guard. Focus on smooth transitions and correct body mechanics. (25 reps)
    3. Light Resistance Drill (25% Resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the initial steps and establish the grips. They can slightly resist the head pull. (20 reps)
    4. Controlled Posture Resistance (50% Resistance): Partner attempts to posture up slightly, forcing you to use your guard and hip angle to maintain control while working the choke. (15 reps)
    5. Simulated Submission Drill (75% Resistance): Partner defends actively but without full strength, allowing you to practice the finishing mechanics of the choke. They can try to peel your hands off. (10 reps)
    6. Live Rolling Integration (90-100% Resistance): Attempt the Cross Collar Choke during live rolling rounds. Focus on recognizing the right opportunities and executing the technique under pressure. (As opportunities arise in 5-minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When opponent is postured up in your closed guard, creating a gap for grip entry.
    • When opponent attempts to "stack" you, giving you leverage to pull their head down.
    • When opponent's head is slightly turned, exposing the carotid artery more directly.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Defending the Grip: Immediately peel the choking hand's grip off the lapel by "walking" your fingers inward towards the opponent's neck, breaking their posture and creating space.
    • Posture Up and Base Out: If the choke is being set up, immediately "stack" your opponent by driving your hips forward and your shoulders into their hips, breaking their base and relieving pressure.
    • Head Escape: If the choke is being applied, try to "turn your chin" into the choking arm, making it difficult to apply pressure. Simultaneously, use your free hand to "frame" on their bicep and create space to escape the guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Cross Collar Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often stems from excessive pronation and trying to "torque" the choke with your wrist. The Cross Collar Choke relies on the "cutting" motion of your forearm across the carotid artery and the downward pull of your opposite elbow. Ensure your wrists remain relatively neutral and focus on the forearm and elbow mechanics for pressure, not wrist strength.

    Q: How can a white belt effectively apply the Cross Collar Choke against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on establishing deep, "sticky" lapel grips. Use your hips to create a significant angle (closer to 30 degrees if possible) and "pull" their head down into your chest. Do not rely on pulling their weight; instead, use their size against them by controlling their head and creating the choke mechanics.

    Q: I keep losing the Cross Collar Choke; my opponent just pulls their head out. What am I doing wrong?

    This usually indicates insufficient control of the opponent's posture and head. Ensure your closed guard is tight, and your hips are actively "walking" their head down and into your chest. Use your free arm to "frame" on their shoulder or bicep to prevent them from posturing up or turning out, which is what allows them to escape the choke's pressure.
