Cross Collar Choke: The Essential BJJ Technique Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This choke is typically initiated from the Closed Guard position, aiming to submit an opponent by restricting blood flow to the brain.

Many white belts struggle because they rely on brute strength rather than leverage, leading to inefficient and potentially injurious attempts.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to create space and your arms as levers, cinching the lapels around the carotid arteries.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From closed guard, secure a "cross collar grip" on your opponent's gi lapels: right hand on their left lapel, left hand on their right lapel. Grip the fabric near the collarbone.
    2. Slightly elevate your hips by bridging your glutes off the mat, creating a small gap between your torso and theirs.
    3. Drive your knees towards your opponent's armpits, maintaining a tight "closed guard" connection.
    4. With your right hand, pull their left lapel across their neck, tucking it firmly against their carotid artery.
    5. Simultaneously, with your left hand, pull their right lapel across their neck, also tucking it firmly against their other carotid artery.
    6. "Close the frame" by bringing your elbows towards each other, creating downward pressure and tightening the lapels.
    7. To finish, drive your hips down into the mat while simultaneously squeezing your elbows together, effectively compressing the lapels against their neck.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too high on the lapel: This can lead to a "choke" that compresses the trachea (windpipe) instead of the carotid arteries, causing pain and potential damage to the larynx. The correct grip is lower, near the collarbone.
    • Arching the back excessively to create space: This puts undue stress on the lumbar spine, risking a "back strain" or "herniated disc". Instead, elevate the hips by engaging the glutes and driving knees towards the armpits.
    • Squeezing with arms only: This leads to shoulder and elbow joint strain, potentially causing "rotator cuff tears" or "ligament damage". The primary force should come from hip drive and controlled elbow adduction.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip acquisition and hip elevation/drive motion without a partner. Focus on the feeling of the lapels creating pressure. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Opponent lies passively on their back. Practice the full sequence of grips, hip elevation, lapel positioning, and finishing squeeze. (25 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Opponent lightly resists the lapel pulls and hip elevation. Focus on maintaining control and completing the choke. (5 rounds, 1 min each)
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance - 50%): Opponent actively tries to prevent the lapel grips and hip movement. Work on breaking their posture and securing the choke. (5 rounds, 1 min each)
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Opponent defends more actively, trying to peel grips and escape. Focus on maintaining tight control and finishing the choke. (5 rounds, 1 min each)
    6. Live rolling (90-100%): Attempt the choke during sparring, understanding that the opportunity may not arise every time. Focus on recognizing the setup and executing with intent. (10 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is postured down in your closed guard, creating an accessible lapel.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their head is within striking distance of your lapel grips.
    • When your opponent is turtled and you can secure a grip on their collar from the side.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Grip defense: The opponent can peel your hand off the lapel by using their own hands to break your grip or by creating space with their elbows.
    • Posture escape: The opponent can posture up, breaking your closed guard and making the choke ineffective. They can achieve this by driving their hips back and straightening their legs.
    • Head and arm escape: The opponent can tuck their chin, turn their head away, or drive their head into your chest to prevent the lapels from cinching the carotid arteries. This often involves creating a "frame" with their arm.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Cross Collar Choke from closed guard?

    Wrist pain often occurs because you are relying on a "cupped" grip or a "flexed" wrist to generate force. Instead, maintain a neutral wrist position and use your forearm and shoulder to drive the lapels across. Ensure your grip is on the fabric itself, not digging into your knuckles, and focus on squeezing your elbows together to create the choke.

    Q: How can I effectively apply the Cross Collar Choke against a much bigger opponent from closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paraMount. Instead of trying to pull them down, use your hips to create a slight angle and elevation. Drive your knees into their armpits to disrupt their base. Then, focus on using your body weight and the mechanics of your arms as levers to cinch the lapels, rather than relying on pure pulling strength. A tight closed guard is essential to maintain control.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from trying a Cross Collar Choke to another technique?

    If your opponent successfully defends by posturing up, tucking their chin, or peeling your grips, continuing to force the choke is inefficient and can expose you. At this point, you can transition. For example, if they posture up, you can transition to a "scissor sweep" or attempt an "Armbar" if an arm is extended. If they defend by tucking their chin and creating a frame, you might consider transitioning to a "guillotine choke" if their head is still accessible.
