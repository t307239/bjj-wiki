The guard pass is the fundamental BJJ technique to advance from being controlled by an opponent's legs to achieving a dominant positional advantage like side-control.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of leverage, using brute strength instead of coordinated body mechanics, and anticipating the opponent's reactions.

The core principle is to break the opponent's structure and create an angle of attack that allows you to detach your hips from their leg base and establish control.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure a strong double-sleeve grip on the opponent's gi arms, palms facing each other, fingers extended. Your thumbs should be inside the sleeves.
    2. Base: Establish a wide, stable base with your feet shoulder-width apart, knees bent, and hips low, creating a stable tripod.
    3. Posture: Break the opponent's posture by pulling their sleeves downward and slightly forward, forcing their head towards their knees.
    4. Hip Angle: Drive your hips forward and slightly to one side, creating a 45-degree angle relative to the opponent's hips.
    5. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your lead leg, compressing the opponent's knee or thigh with your lead hip.
    6. Arm Control: Maintain sleeve grips and use your arms to control the opponent's upper body, preventing them from re-establishing guard.
    7. Leg Detachment: Once the opponent's structure is broken and your hip angle is established, step your trailing leg out and around their legs, detaching your hips from their guard.
    8. Advance: Drive your hips forward into the opponent's hip or torso, maintaining pressure and securing a dominant position like side-control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knees Buckling Inward: When attempting to step around the opponent's legs, if you allow your knees to fall inward (valgus collapse) while your feet are still close together, you risk tearing your ACL or MCL. Correct: Maintain a wide base and step with intent, ensuring your knees track over your toes, keeping your hips wide.
    • Elbow Hyperextension: When pulling on the opponent's sleeves to break posture, if you lock out your elbows completely and pull with excessive force, you can hyperextend your elbow joint, leading to ligament damage. Correct: Keep a slight bend in your elbows and use your lats and back to drive the movement, not just your arms.
    • Spinal Flexion Under Pressure: If you allow your upper back to round excessively while the opponent is pushing into you or trying to regain guard, you can strain your lower back muscles or even compress spinal discs. Correct: Maintain a neutral spine, engaging your core to support your torso and keep your chest up.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Footwork & Grip Practice (0%): Practice establishing the wide base, driving hips forward, and stepping around imaginary legs for 1 minute, 5 repetitions.
    2. Solo Posture Break & Grip Isolation (0%): Practice the sleeve grip and posture break motion against a training dummy or wall for 1 minute, 5 repetitions.
    3. Partner Static Posture Break (25%): With a partner lying on their back, practice establishing sleeve grips, breaking their posture, and driving your hips forward without them resisting. Focus on hip angle and weight transfer. 5 reps each side.
    4. Partner Controlled Guard Pass (50%): Partner maintains a loose closed guard. You practice establishing grips, breaking posture, and executing the hip angle and weight transfer to step around. Partner offers minimal resistance. 5 reps each side.
    5. Partner Dynamic Guard Pass (75%): Partner actively tries to reset guard. You focus on maintaining grips, posture break, hip angle, and a clean step around. Opponent resists slightly. 3 reps each side.
    6. Live Rolling - Guard Pass Focus (100%): During rolling, dedicate rounds to specifically attempting the guard pass. Focus on executing the mechanical steps. 3 rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's hips are close to yours in their closed guard, making it difficult for them to create space.
    • When the opponent's knees are not tightly locked, allowing you to create an opening with your hips.
    • When the opponent attempts a sweep and their base momentarily falters, creating an opportunity to advance.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Knee Shield/Open Guard: The opponent uses their leg to create a frame (e.g., knee shield in half guard, or a leg extended in open guard) to prevent your hips from getting close. They will use their foot to push on your hip or shoulder to maintain distance.
    • Hip Escape & Re-Guard: If you fail to establish a strong posture break and hip angle, the opponent will use their hips to shrimp away, creating space to re-establish their guard or sweep you.
    • Standing Up & Posture Control: If the opponent successfully stands up within your guard, they can often break your grips and pass by simply lifting you and stepping over your legs.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to pass the guard?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are allowing your knee to collapse inward (valgus stress) when stepping around your opponent's legs. This happens when your base is too narrow or you're not properly rotating your hips. To fix this, widen your stance, ensure your knees track over your toes, and focus on driving your hips forward and out to the side, creating a stable, neutral knee alignment.

    Q: How can I guard pass against a bigger opponent with strong closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, brute strength is your enemy. Focus intensely on breaking their posture first by pulling their sleeves down and slightly forward, using your back and lats. Then, create a significant hip angle (45 degrees) and drive your hip into their thigh or hip to off-balance them before attempting to step around. This creates leverage they cannot easily counter.

    Q: Why can't I get my hips past my opponent's legs when I try to guard pass?

    You're likely not creating a sufficient hip angle or breaking their posture effectively. If you try to pass with your hips square to theirs, their legs act as a solid barrier. You must first break their posture to make them unstable, then drive your hips at a 45-degree angle to their body. This slight rotation allows your hips to clear their leg structure more easily.
