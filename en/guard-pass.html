The Guard Pass aims to move from a position where your opponent controls your legs (guard) to a dominant position like Side Control. White belts often struggle by focusing on brute force instead of leverage.

The key is understanding how to break the opponent's structure and create space to move your hips around their legs.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting Position: Opponent is in closed guard, you are inside their guard, on your knees or feet.
    2. Grip: Secure a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand on their left collar and your left hand on their right collar. Keep your elbows tucked.
    3. Posture: Maintain an upright posture, preventing them from pulling you down. Keep your head up and shoulders back.
    4. Break the Guard (Kneeling): Place your knees slightly wider than shoulder-width apart for a stable base. Drive your chest towards their hips, simultaneously pushing their knees down and away with your forearms.
    5. Weight Distribution: Shift your weight forward, onto the balls of your feet if standing, or onto your knees and shins if kneeling.
    6. Hip Movement: As you break the guard, angle your hips to the side, away from the leg you intend to pass first (e.g., angle right if passing their right leg).
    7. Leg Separation: Use your hands to pry their knees open and separate their legs. Your goal is to create a clear path for your hips.
    8. Hip Penetration: Drive your hip into the space created, moving your hips past their knee line.
    9. Secure Position: Once past their legs, establish side control by securing a cross-face grip and hip-to-hip connection.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Neck Injury Risk: When breaking the guard, white belts often pull their opponent's head directly towards them. This can hyperextend the cervical spine, causing neck sprains or disc damage. The correct action is to use your chest and forearms to drive into their hips and push their knees down, maintaining your head in a neutral position.
    • Knee and ACL Injury Risk: Attempting to force a standing guard pass by stepping directly over an opponent's legs without proper hip angle or breaking their structure can lead to knee hyperextension or ligament tears. Instead, break their guard first, create space, and then use your hips to drive past their legs.
    • Shoulder and Rotator Cuff Injury Risk: Grabbing sleeves or pants with an overextended grip and trying to yank the opponent's legs open can strain the rotator cuff. Focus on controlling their hips and knees with your forearms and hands in a braced position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip and Posture Drill: Practice securing cross-collar grips and maintaining upright posture for 30 seconds. Repeat 5 times.
    2. Kneeling Guard Break Simulation (0% Resistance): From a kneeling position, practice driving your chest forward and pushing imaginary knees down and away. Focus on hip angling. 10 repetitions.
    3. Partnered Guard Break (25% Resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice breaking their closed guard by driving your chest into their hips and pushing their knees down. Partner offers minimal resistance. 10 repetitions.
    4. Partnered Guard Break and Hip Angle (50% Resistance): Practice breaking their closed guard and then immediately angling your hips to the side to create space. Partner offers moderate resistance. 10 repetitions.
    5. Pass Attempt with Hip Penetration (75% Resistance): From a broken guard, practice driving your hip past their leg and establishing side control. Partner actively tries to re-guard. 5 repetitions per side.
    6. Live Rolling (100% Resistance): Attempt the guard pass during a rolling session. Focus on executing the mechanics learned without solely relying on strength. 5-minute rounds.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent has established a tight closed guard and is posturing up, creating an opportunity to break.
    • When your opponent attempts to pull guard from a standing position, allowing you to secure grips and posture.
    • When your opponent's guard is slightly open, providing an entry point for your hands.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Re-guarding: As the passer drives their hip past, the defender can quickly bring their opposite leg back across to re-establish guard. This is countered by maintaining hip-to-hip connection and not allowing space.
    • Shrimping/Bridging: The defender can shrimp away or bridge to create space and potentially sweep. This is countered by the passer keeping their weight low and their base wide, preventing the opponent from generating leverage.
    • Technical Stand-up: If the passer loses posture or their base, the defender can attempt a technical stand-up to escape or re-establish guard. This is countered by the passer maintaining strong grips and control over the opponent's hips and upper body.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my back hurt when I try to pass the guard in BJJ?

    Back pain during guard passing often stems from excessive rounding of the lumbar spine, a common result of trying to force the pass rather than using proper biomechanics. When you round your back, you lose your core stability and place undue stress on your spinal erector muscles and intervertebral discs. To fix this, focus on maintaining a neutral spine, engaging your core, and driving with your hips and legs rather than just your back. Ensure your posture is upright when breaking the guard.

    Q: How do I pass the guard of a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, brute strength is a losing battle. Instead, focus on leverage and breaking their structure. Use your grips to control their hips and shoulders, preventing them from using their size to smother you. Angle your body significantly to create space and drive your hips past their legs, rather than trying to force your way through their center. Think about cutting off their base and creating off-balancing opportunities.

    Q: When should I try to pass the guard in BJJ, and what if they keep their guard closed?

    You should attempt a guard pass when you have good posture and control, and ideally, when your opponent's legs are not fully locked. If they maintain a tight Closed Guard, your primary goal is to break it. Focus on controlling their hips with your forearms or hands, driving your chest into their hips, and then using a hip angle to create space to start moving around their legs. Don't try to force the pass before breaking the guard; that's where most white belts get stuck.
