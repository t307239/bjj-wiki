This technique starts from a position where your opponent has established a Closed Guard, controlling your hips and legs. The goal is to break their grip and establish a dominant side-control or mount position.

White belts often struggle with guard passing due to a lack of understanding of leverage and body mechanics, leading to them being stuck or swept.

The key mechanical insight is to use your body weight and structure to create angles and break their base, rather than relying solely on brute strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Secure a double-sleeve grip on your opponent's gi, palms facing inward, thumbs outside the fabric. Your knuckles should be close to their elbow joints.
    2. Base: Establish a wide, stable base with your feet shoulder-width apart, knees bent, and hips low. Your weight should be distributed evenly.
    3. Hip Angle: Turn your hips 45 degrees away from your opponent's legs. This creates an angle that makes it harder for them to maintain their guard.
    4. Pressure: Drive your chest down towards their hips. This flattens their back and makes it difficult for them to hip escape.
    5. Break: With your sleeve grips, pull their arms outward and upward simultaneously. This breaks the 'lock' of their closed guard.
    6. Leg Clearance: As their guard breaks, step your lead leg (the one on the side you are facing) back and out, clearing their legs.
    7. Weight Transfer: Immediately transfer your weight onto your hips and forward onto your opponent's chest as you drive your knee through their hip line to achieve side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Twist: Attempting to force a pass by driving your knee directly forward while your opponent's legs are still locked can cause your knee joint (specifically the ACL or MCL) to twist under shear force, leading to a tear.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Trying to "muscle" through a guard by pulling your opponent's arms too aggressively with straight elbows can lead to shoulder impingement or rotator cuff strain as your arm bone jams into the shoulder socket.
    • Lower Back Strain: Arching your lower back excessively to try and 'lift' your opponent's legs, instead of using hip pressure and body mechanics, puts immense strain on your lumbar spine, risking muscle pulls or disc injury.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Grip & Angle Drill: Practice securing double-sleeve grips and then stepping your hips back to a 45-degree angle while maintaining a low base. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Solo Guard Break & Step: From the 45-degree angle, practice the outward and upward pull to break the guard, followed by stepping your lead leg back. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    3. Partnered Guard Break & Step (No Resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice breaking their closed guard and stepping your lead leg back. Focus on the mechanics. (25 reps, 0% resistance)
    4. Partnered Pass Entry (Light Resistance): Your partner attempts to maintain closed guard. You practice breaking it and stepping back. They offer minimal resistance to your initial movement. (25 reps, 25% resistance)
    5. Controlled Pass Attempt: Your partner tries to maintain guard. You execute the break, step back, and drive forward to side control. Partner offers moderate resistance to your pass but doesn't actively try to sweep or submit. (20 reps, 50% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling - Guard Pass Focus: Roll with a partner, specifically focusing on executing this guard pass when the opportunity arises. Allow for full resistance. (5 minute rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent has a tight, unbroken closed guard and you are struggling to create space or move.
    • When your opponent is trying to create distance with their legs, making it difficult for them to maintain a tight lock.
    • When you feel your opponent's hips are elevated, making their guard less stable.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape & Re-Guard: If the pass is initiated, the defender can hip escape to create space and re-establish guard or switch to a different guard. This involves bridging their hips away from the attacker's pressure.
    • Flower Sweep: As the attacker breaks the guard and steps back, the defender can use their legs to hook the attacker's ankle and hip, then bridge to sweep them over. This requires precise timing of the leg hook and bridge.
    • Underhook & Bridge: If the attacker gets too close, the defender can secure an underhook on the attacker's arm and bridge their hips to create space and potentially reverse the position or scramble back to guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to break my opponent's guard in BJJ?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you grip too tightly with your fingers extended and pull with straight arms. This puts excessive torque on the wrist joint. Instead, aim for a firm grip with your thumbs wrapped, and pull using your biceps and back muscles, keeping your elbows slightly bent. Think of 'cupping' their sleeves, not 'grabbing'.

    Q: How do I guard pass against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and angles are critical. Focus on breaking their posture by pulling their upper body down towards your hips. Use your hips to create 45-degree angles and drive your weight into their center of mass, rather than trying to lift or push them. Emphasize controlling their frames and hips.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a guard pass in BJJ?

    The optimal time to attempt a guard pass is when your opponent's posture is broken, or they are trying to create space to improve their guard. Look for moments when their hips are off the mat, or when they are reaching to adjust their grip. Avoid passing when they have strong posture and a tight, connected guard.
