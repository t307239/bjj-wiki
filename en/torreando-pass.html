The Torreando Pass is initiated from your feet against a Closed Guard, aiming to establish side control. White belts often struggle by attacking too directly, neglecting the opponent's frames.

The key is to use hip movement and head positioning to create angles, not brute force.

Understanding the opponent's hip rotation is crucial to finding your opening.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip opponent's pant legs just above the knees with your hands. Maintain a hip-width stance, knees slightly bent, spine neutral.
    2. Step your left foot back, creating a small base. Keep your right foot planted firmly.
    3. Drive your hips down and slightly to your right. Your right shoulder should be low, near the opponent's left hip.
    4. Use your grip to slightly lift and pull the opponent's left leg towards your chest.
    5. Pivot your right foot 90 degrees to the right, turning your hips to face the opponent's left side.
    6. Step your left foot forward, clearing the opponent's right leg. Your hips should now be perpendicular to their body.
    7. Release grips and step your right foot over the opponent's hips, moving towards side control. Maintain head pressure on their chest.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to rip legs apart with straight arms: This can hyperextend the elbow joint, leading to ligament tears (UCL/MCL).
    • Stiff-legged stepping around: This puts immense pressure on the knee joint, risking meniscus tears or ACL sprains.
    • Lack of hip rotation and driving forward: This leads to the opponent easily re-guarding or sweeping, potentially causing ankle sprains from awkward foot placement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the footwork and hip movement without a partner. Focus on the pivot and hip drive. 10 reps each side.
    2. Partner drill (0% resistance): Partner lies on their back, maintaining a closed guard. You execute the Torreando Pass with no resistance. 5 reps each side.
    3. Partner drill (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel their legs slightly. Focus on grip control. 5 reps each side.
    4. Partner drill (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to maintain closed guard, but doesn't actively fight the pass. 5 reps each side.
    5. Partner drill (75% resistance): Partner actively tries to defend the pass by framing and hip escaping. Focus on maintaining angles. 3 reps each side.
    6. Live rolling (90% intensity): Execute the Torreando Pass during rolling, focusing on timing and proper execution. 5 attempts.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent's legs are tightly crossed in a closed guard, creating a clear target.
    • Opponent extends their legs slightly, creating a momentary gap.
    • Opponent attempts to open their guard from a static position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Framing with forearms: Opponent places forearms between their hips and your chest, preventing you from getting close.
    • Hip escape and re-guard: Opponent uses their hips to create space and bring their legs back into guard.
    • Leg pummeling: Opponent actively fights to keep their legs together, preventing you from isolating one.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Torreando Pass?

    Knee pain often occurs when you step around the opponent's legs with a straight or locked knee. To avoid this, always maintain a slight bend in your knees throughout the entire movement. Ensure your hips are driving forward and turning, rather than just stepping around stiffly. This protects the knee joint from excessive torsion.

    Q: How do I Torreando Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus heavily on off-balancing them before the pass. Use your grips to slightly lift and pull one of their legs, forcing their hips to shift. Then, use your own hip drive and a sharp pivot to create an angle. Don't try to muscle past them; use leverage and speed to get to the side. Your head position should be low, driving into their chest.

    Q: When is the best time to use the Torreando Pass instead of a knee-slice?

    The Torreando Pass is most effective when the opponent's legs are relatively high and tight, specifically in a closed guard. It allows you to quickly dismantle that guard structure by isolating one leg and using your hips to turn. The knee-slice pass is generally better when the opponent's legs are lower and they are trying to create space, or when you've already established a strong cross-face.
