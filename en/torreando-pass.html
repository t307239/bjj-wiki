Torreando Pass: Your Biomechanically Sound BJJ Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Torreando Pass is a dynamic guard passing technique initiated from open or Half Guard. It aims to bypass the opponent's legs and establish side control.

White belts often struggle by committing too early, losing balance, or failing to break grips effectively.

The key is a controlled, explosive hip rotation combined with a strong base, forcing the opponent to react and open their guard.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand, thumb inside. Your left hand secures the opponent's left sleeve, palm facing you.
    2. Plant your right foot outside the opponent's left hip, creating a 45-degree angle with your torso. Keep your base wide, feet shoulder-width apart.
    3. Drive your left knee towards the mat, directly in front of the opponent's right hip, acting as a wedge to prevent them from closing their guard.
    4. Simultaneously, begin to rotate your hips counter-clockwise, driving your right shoulder towards the opponent's left hip.
    5. As your hips rotate, shift your weight onto your right foot and drive your left leg backwards in an arc, stepping over the opponent's legs.
    6. Maintain your cross-collar grip and sleeve grip, pulling the opponent's upper body towards your right side as you step.
    7. Continue the hip rotation and backward leg swing, aiming to land in side control with your right hip flush against the opponent's left hip.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to step over without a solid base: This can lead to your ankle or knee ligaments (e.g., anterior talofibular ligament in the ankle, medial collateral ligament in the knee) being overstretched or torn as your body weight collapses onto an unsupported limb.
    • Releasing grips prematurely: If you let go of the collar or sleeve too early, the opponent can easily re-establish guard or sweep you. Maintain grip integrity throughout the movement.
    • Standing too upright: A common mistake is not lowering your hips enough. This reduces your base and leverage, making you susceptible to sweeps and compromising the rotational force needed for the pass. Keep your base low and wide.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the footwork and hip rotation without a partner. Focus on the 45-degree angle and the backward leg swing. (50 reps)
    2. Partnered drilling (no resistance): With a willing partner, execute the pass from a closed guard. Focus on grip fighting and the initial hip drive. (25 reps)
    3. Partnered drilling (light resistance): Partner attempts to keep their guard closed. Focus on breaking grips and the hip rotation. (25 reps)
    4. Partnered drilling (active guard): Partner uses light movement to try and frame or recover guard. Focus on maintaining your base and driving through. (25 reps)
    5. Situational sparring: Start from a guard pass scenario where the opponent has their legs slightly open or is in an open guard. (5 rounds, 1 minute each)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate the Torreando Pass into your regular rolling sessions. (10% of rolling time)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent's guard is open, with legs extended or in a loose spider guard.
    • Opponent attempts a sweep or submission, creating an opening.
    • You have successfully broken grips and created space to initiate the pass.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Recovery (e.g., Triangle Choke or Sweep): If the passer commits too early, the opponent can trap an arm and leg to set up a triangle choke or use the momentum to sweep.
    • Hip Escape and Re-Guard: A well-timed hip escape by the passer can evade the counter and allow them to reset or continue passing.
    • Grip Fighting and Frame Creation: The defender can actively fight grips and use their forearms to create frames, preventing the passer from getting the necessary angle and leverage.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Torreando Pass?

    Knee pain during the Torreando Pass often stems from improper weight transfer and a lack of hip rotation. If you're not rotating your hips effectively, you're likely forcing your knee to bear excessive rotational stress, which can damage the meniscus or ligaments. Ensure your hips are leading the movement and your base is wide and stable before driving your leg back.

    Q: How can I Torreando Pass against a much bigger opponent who has strong grips?

    Against a larger opponent, prioritize grip fighting to break their control before initiating the pass. Use your legs to create frames and inch your hips away to generate space for your grip fighting. Once grips are broken, focus on driving your shoulder into their hip and using your body weight to initiate the rotation, rather than relying solely on upper body strength to pull them over.

    Q: When is the best time to initiate the Torreando Pass versus a Knee Slice Pass?

    The Torreando Pass is ideal when the opponent's legs are extended or in a more open guard, allowing you to easily step over. The Knee Slice Pass is more effective when the opponent's guard is tighter or when you want to apply immediate pressure to flatten them out. If they are actively framing and keeping their knees close, the Knee Slice is often a better choice.
