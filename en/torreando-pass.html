Torreando Pass: A White Belt's Guide to BJJ Guard Passing

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Torreando Pass is an open-guard passing technique designed to circumvent an opponent's legs and establish Side Control. It requires precise hip movement and weight distribution to be effective.

White belts often struggle with the Torreando Pass due to a lack of body awareness and an impulse to rely on brute strength rather than technique. This leads to inefficient movement and increased injury risk.

The key mechanical insight for the Torreando Pass is controlling the opponent's hips while creating space to pivot your own hips and step around their legs. This creates an angle that breaks their base and prevents them from re-guarding.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip the opponent's pants at the ankle level with your palms facing each other, fingers gripping the fabric securely. Your thumbs should be on the outside of the fabric.
    2. Maintain a low base with your knees bent and hips below the opponent's hips. Your chest should be close to their thigh, creating a framing pressure.
    3. Shift your weight onto your lead leg, slightly lifting the opponent's leg on that side. Your hips should angle towards their opposite hip.
    4. Begin to pivot your hips, turning your torso away from the opponent. Your lead leg remains planted, acting as an anchor.
    5. As you pivot, step your trailing leg around the opponent's hips. The goal is to clear their leg line with your stepping leg.
    6. Maintain constant pressure with your chest against their thigh to prevent them from sitting up or bringing their knees to your chest.
    7. Once your trailing leg has cleared their hips, your hips should be perpendicular to their body. You are now in a position to drive forward into side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping too high on the pants (e.g., knee or thigh) allows the opponent to maintain their base and re-guard. This can lead to a hyperextension of your knee joint as you try to force the pass, potentially damaging the ACL or MCL.
    • Not maintaining a low base and instead standing upright exposes your spine to potential hyperextension if the opponent attempts a sweep or takedown. This can cause lower back strain or disc injury.
    • Stepping too close to the opponent's hips without pivoting your own hips can result in your hips colliding with theirs. This awkward impact can strain your hip flexors or cause groin pulls.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip pivot and leg stepping motion without a partner. Focus on the coordination of hip angle and footwork. (10 reps each side, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice the grip and the hip pivot, stepping around their legs as if they were static. (10 reps each side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): The partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the hip control and the slight leg lift. (10 reps each side, 25% resistance)
    4. Controlled pass: The partner actively tries to maintain their guard but doesn't aggressively counter. Focus on maintaining hip pressure and clearing the legs. (5 reps each side, 50% resistance)
    5. Passing against a passive guard: The partner attempts to block but doesn't actively attack. Work on your timing and hip movement to break their guard. (5 reps each side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Torreando Pass against a resisting opponent. Focus on executing the mechanics learned and adapting to their reactions. (3-5 attempts per round, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent has an open guard and their knees are not tightly controlled.
    • When the opponent attempts to create distance by pushing your hips away.
    • When you have established a strong grip on their pants and can control one of their legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Drag: If the opponent's leg is exposed, grab their ankle and drag it across their body, transitioning to a leg drag position.
    • Knee-Slice Pass: If you can't get around their hips, drive your knee across their body and cut through their guard, similar to a knee-slice pass.
    • Re-Guard: The opponent can attempt to bring their knees back to your chest or hips to re-establish guard, so be ready to adjust your position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Torreando Pass?

    Your knee might be hurting because you are not maintaining a low, stable base and are allowing your knee to hyperextend or twist. Ensure your hips are always below your opponent's hips, and when you pivot, your supporting knee should be slightly bent, not locked straight. Focus on driving your hips *around* their legs, not pushing *through* them.

    Q: How do I Torreando Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision become even more critical. Focus on getting under their hips and creating a strong frame with your chest against their thigh. Instead of lifting their leg, aim to control the angle of their hip with your grip and body weight, then pivot your own hips to create the necessary space to step around.

    Q: When is the best time to initiate the Torreando Pass in BJJ?

    The Torreando Pass is most effective when the opponent's guard is open or loosely controlled, and they are not actively pressuring you forward. It's ideal when they attempt to create space by pushing your hips, as this action often creates the initial opening for you to grip and begin the pivot. Avoid attempting it when their guard is tightly closed or when they are actively trying to sweep you.
