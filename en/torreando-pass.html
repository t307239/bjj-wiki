Torreando Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Torreando Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against an opponent's guard, aiming to bypass their legs and achieve Side Control.

White belts often struggle due to insufficient hip mobility and a tendency to rely on brute strength rather than precise weight transfer.

The key insight is maintaining constant forward pressure while circularly repositioning your hips to create an angle, forcing the opponent to detach their legs.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a strong cross-collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (or wrist) with your left hand. Your hips should be angled slightly left, creating a 30-degree angle to your opponent.
    2. Step your left foot forward, placing it slightly outside your opponent's left hip. Keep your base wide and your knees bent.
    3. Apply upward pressure on the collar grip and forward pressure on the sleeve grip, forcing your opponent to react and potentially extend their legs.
    4. Simultaneously, pivot on your left foot, turning your hips to face your opponent's left side. Your right leg will follow, swinging in an arc.
    5. Drive your right knee towards the mat outside your opponent's hip, maintaining a low center of gravity and continuous forward pressure.
    6. As your right knee touches down, extend your left leg back, creating space and preventing your opponent from re-establishing guard.
    7. Drive your hips forward and lower your chest towards your opponent's torso, securing a stable side control position with your grips still engaged.
    8. Transition to your preferred side control grip, typically underhooking their far arm and controlling their head or shoulder.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Lunging forward with straight legs: This can overextend the knees and place excessive strain on the ACL and meniscus, leading to tears. The correct movement is to maintain bent knees and drive forward with your hips.
    • Gripping too tightly on the sleeve and pulling directly towards you: This can cause wrist sprains or even elbow hyperextension if the opponent resists strongly. Instead, use the grip to create a pulling angle and guide their arm as you circle.
    • Allowing your hips to become square with your opponent's: This creates a "wall" that your opponent can easily use to push you back or re-guard. Maintain a constant 30-45 degree hip angle throughout the pass.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip pivot and leg arc motion without a partner, focusing on smooth transitions and maintaining a low base. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Have a partner lie on their back with their guard closed. Practice establishing grips and executing the Torreando Pass, allowing them to offer minimal resistance. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (static): Partner attempts to keep guard closed. Focus on breaking the guard and executing the pass. (30 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (dynamic): Partner actively tries to reset guard. Focus on maintaining pressure and finishing the pass. (30 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Torreando Pass during live rolling with a focus on timing and proper execution. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full): Integrate the Torreando Pass into your live rolling strategy, aiming to use it against various guard types. (5 rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent has a closed guard that is difficult to break directly.
    • Opponent attempts to shrimp away, creating space you can exploit.
    • Opponent's hips are slightly elevated, making their legs less stable.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Re-guarding: As the passer circles, quickly bring your knees back to your chest and feet to their hips to re-establish closed guard.
    • Hip Escape and Underhook: If the passer gets too far past your legs, shrimp your hips away and attempt to secure an underhook to prevent them from settling into side control.
    • Sweep: If the passer commits too heavily to one side and loses balance, use their momentum against them with a sweep (e.g., scissor sweep if you can get a grip on their arm and pant leg).

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the Torreando Pass and my opponent keeps their legs tight?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are trying to force the pass with straight legs and excessive torque, rather than using your hips and a circular motion. When your opponent's legs are tight, you need to focus on driving your hips forward and angling your body, allowing your knee to bend and absorb the pressure as you step around. Imagine your leg is a pendulum, not a rigid lever.

    Q: How can I effectively Torreando Pass against a much bigger opponent who has a very strong closed guard?

    Against a larger opponent, your weight distribution and hip pressure are paraMount. Instead of pulling their collar directly up, use your grip to create a slight upward and outward angle, forcing them to open their guard. Then, focus on driving your hips forward and circling with your base low, making it difficult for their larger frame to adjust and re-guard. Your goal is to get your hips past their hips.

    Q: When is the best moment to initiate the Torreando Pass in a live roll, and how do I avoid getting stuck in their guard?

    The best moment to initiate the Torreando Pass is when your opponent is slightly off-balance or when you've created a small opening by controlling their posture. To avoid getting stuck, maintain constant forward pressure and keep your base wide and low. As you circle, your hips should be moving *through* their guard, not just around it, and always be ready to adjust your base if they try to re-guard.
