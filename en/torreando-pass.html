The Torreando Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against an opponent's guard, aiming to bypass their legs and achieve Side Control.

Many white belts struggle because they rely on brute force or incorrect weight distribution, leading to predictable and easily countered attempts.

The core principle is using hip rotation and leg movement to create a dynamic angle, forcing the opponent to react and exposing their base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a collar grip with your right hand on the opponent's left collar, and a sleeve grip with your left hand on their right sleeve.
    2. Position your hips at a 45-degree angle to the opponent's hips, maintaining a base width slightly wider than your shoulders.
    3. Step your right foot forward and to the side of the opponent's right hip, drawing an arc with your foot.
    4. Simultaneously, pivot your left foot, turning your hips and chest towards the opponent's head.
    5. Transfer your weight onto your right foot, driving your right knee towards the mat behind the opponent's right leg.
    6. Use your left hand to pull the opponent's right sleeve across their body, creating a rotational force.
    7. Drive your hips forward and down, lowering your center of gravity and stepping your left leg over the opponent's now-exposed hip to secure side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly attempting to stand up straight while holding grips: This places excessive strain on the lumbar spine, risking a herniated disc or muscle tear due to hyperextension.
    • Lunging forward with your hips too low and feet too close: This can lead to your knees crashing into the opponent's shins, risking tibial plateau fractures or ligament sprains in the knee from impact.
    • Releasing grips too early or too late: A premature release allows the opponent to recover guard; a late release can result in your own arm being trapped and potentially injured, risking an armbar or kimura due to a compromised limb position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the footwork and hip movement without a partner. Focus on the arc of the stepping foot and the hip rotation. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drilling (static): Opponent lies on their back, legs extended. You practice the grip establishment and the step-and-pivot motion, aiming for the correct hip angle and weight transfer. (20 reps per side, 0% resistance)
    3. Partner drilling (passive guard): Opponent maintains a loose closed guard. You practice initiating the pass, focusing on breaking the guard with your hip pressure and stepping around. (30 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drilling (active guard): Opponent attempts to maintain their guard and resist the pass. Focus on maintaining your base and executing the hip rotation to clear the legs. (40 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (limited): Focus on attempting the Torreando Pass for the first 30 seconds of each round. Opponent actively defends but not with full intensity. (5 rounds, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full): Attempt the Torreando Pass as a primary passing strategy, integrating it with other passes. (10 rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent has a loose or broken closed guard, with their legs extended.
    • Opponent is attempting to establish a spider guard or de la riva guard, exposing their hips.
    • You have successfully created distance and are in a kneeling or staggered stance.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Recovery: As the opponent steps around, they can immediately try to re-establish their closed guard by bringing their legs back across their body or by framing with their arms and shins.
    • Hip Escape and Sweeping: If the passer's weight is too forward, the opponent can hip escape to create space and attempt a sweep by bridging or shrimping to regain guard or achieve a dominant position.
    • Counter-Pass: A skilled opponent might use the momentum of the passer's step to transition into their own pass, such as a leg drag or knee slice, if the passer overcommits and exposes their back.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try to Torreando Pass?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are over-extending your arm to grip the sleeve or collar, or you are not using your body's rotation to break the guard. Ensure your grip is firm but not strained, and focus on driving your hip into the opponent's leg while using your free arm to pull their sleeve across their body, creating a rotational force that breaks their guard, rather than relying solely on arm strength.

    Q: How do I Torreando Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on using your footwork and hip movement to create angles and distance. Instead of trying to power through, use your grip to control their posture and then pivot sharply, driving your hip into their knee or thigh to off-balance them. Your goal is to get your hips low and move around their mass, rather than trying to go through it. Use precise timing and explosiveness in your step.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Torreando Pass instead of a knee slice?

    The Torreando Pass is best attempted when the opponent's guard is somewhat open or broken, and their hips are accessible. If they are maintaining a tight Closed Guard or have a strong structure, a knee slice might be more effective for penetrating their guard. The Torreando is excellent when you can create a clear angle and force them to react by turning their hips away from you, making their legs less of a barrier.
