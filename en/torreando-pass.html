Torreando Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Torreando Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position against a Closed Guard, aiming to bypass the opponent's legs to achieve side control.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of understanding of weight distribution and proper hip engagement, leading to stalled passes and overextension.

The key mechanical insight is using your hips to drive through the opponent's leg line, creating a fulcrum with your own base.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip on the opponent's left sleeve with your left hand.
    2. Step your right foot forward, placing it outside the opponent's left hip, creating a slight angle of approximately 30 degrees.
    3. Simultaneously, drive your left knee towards the mat behind your right foot, beginning to lower your base.
    4. Grip the opponent's left pants near the knee with your right hand, while maintaining the collar grip with your left hand.
    5. Drive your hips forward and down, angling your pelvis towards the opponent's left side.
    6. Use your right leg to 'step over' the opponent's left leg, aiming to place your right shin across their hip line.
    7. Shift your weight forward onto your right shin and hip, compressing the opponent's legs and clearing their guard.
    8. Maintain your collar and pants grips and transition to side control, driving your chest into their chest.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'force' the pass by pulling hard on the collar and sleeve without hip drive: This can strain the shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) and elbow (humeroulnar joint) due to excessive torque, leading to sprains or tears. The correct action is to use hip drive to create space, then pull to guide the opponent's posture.
    • Stiff-legging the 'step over' leg: This places immense stress on the knee joint (tibiofemoral joint) and can cause ACL or meniscus tears. The knee should be slightly bent, allowing for a smooth, controlled movement.
    • Rushing the transition to side control and 'diving' headfirst: This can lead to neck hyperextension or compression injuries (cervical spine) if the opponent counters with a sweep or submission. Maintain a stable base and controlled movement throughout the transition.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the leg movement and hip drive sequence without a partner, focusing on body mechanics. (10 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Opponent holds a static closed guard. Execute the Torreando Pass, focusing on grip placement and hip angle. (10 reps)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Opponent offers minimal resistance by slightly gripping your arms or hips. Focus on breaking their guard and clearing the legs. (15 reps)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance - 50%): Opponent actively tries to maintain guard or create frames. Focus on hip pressure and maintaining base. (20 reps)
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Opponent actively defends and attempts to recover guard. Focus on finishing the pass and securing side control. (25 reps)
    6. Live rolling (90%): Implement the Torreando Pass in live rolling with a focus on timing and reaction to opponent's movements. (5 mins)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent's hips are tight and they are attempting to close the guard.
    • When the opponent's legs are extended and they are trying to create distance.
    • When you have established a strong collar and sleeve grip, allowing you to control their posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Recovery (Scissor Sweep/Hip Bump Sweep): As the passer steps over, the defender can use their legs to create a frame and sweep the passer by driving their hips. This requires precise timing of the leg and hip movement.
    • Leg Conversion (De La Riva/Spider Guard): If the passer's step over is too high, the defender can thread their leg through and establish a De La Riva or Spider Guard to regain control of the situation.
    • Counter-Pass (Stack Pass/Knee Slice): If the passer commits too heavily to the Torreando and their base is compromised, the defender can attempt to stack them or drive a knee slice pass past their now exposed legs.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why do my knees hurt when I try to Torreando Pass?

    Knee pain during the Torreando Pass often stems from stiff-legged movements and improper weight transfer. If you are not bending your stepping leg, you are forcing it to absorb all the impact and can hyperextend it, straining the ligaments. Ensure your stepping knee has a slight bend and your hips are driving forward to absorb the impact, not your knees taking the brunt.

    Q: How can I Torreando Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are more crucial than brute strength. Focus intensely on controlling their posture with your collar and sleeve grips to prevent them from using their weight effectively. Drive your hips forward with maximum force into their leg line to create the necessary space, and use your angle to off-balance them before committing to the step-over. Do not try to muscle past them; use biomechanical advantage.

    Q: When is the best time to start the Torreando Pass in BJJ?

    The optimal time to initiate the Torreando Pass is when the opponent has just committed to closing their guard or is attempting to establish a strong grip on you from closed guard. This is when their hips are often engaged to create a tight seal. By recognizing this moment and immediately attacking with your grips and hip drive, you exploit a brief window of opportunity before they can solidify their position or set up a sweep.
