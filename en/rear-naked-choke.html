Rear Naked Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide to BJJ

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This choke is primarily applied from the back-Mount position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain.

White belts often struggle due to insufficient control and improper choking mechanics, leading to ineffective attempts.

The key is isolating one carotid artery and applying pressure with the forearm and bicep, not just squeezing.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the back position with both hooks (feet inside opponent's thighs).
    2. Place your right arm across opponent's neck, ensuring the crook of your elbow is under their chin.
    3. Grip your own right bicep with your left hand (this is the 'seatbelt' grip).
    4. Bring your right hand behind opponent's head, thumb-side down, to grip your left wrist.
    5. Flatten your back and drive your hips forward slightly to create a stable base.
    6. Contract your right bicep and forearm, squeezing your opponent's right carotid artery against their own shoulder.
    7. Use your left hand on your right wrist to pull your right forearm deeper into the choke, creating a perpendicular pressure vector.
    8. Ensure your head is positioned on the side of the choke, slightly tilting your opponent's head away from the pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to choke by simply squeezing with your arms: this strains your shoulder and bicep muscles, risking rotator cuff tears, and fails to create proper arterial compression. Correct by focusing on the forearm-to-forearm pressure.
    • Grabbing your own neck instead of your bicep/wrist: this can cause neck strain and limits your ability to apply consistent pressure, potentially leading to cervical facet joint irritation. Correct by maintaining the 'seatbelt' grip on your bicep or wrist.
    • Allowing opponent to turn into the choke without adjusting your base: this can lead to your arm being trapped and potentially twisted, risking elbow hyperextension or ligament damage. Correct by maintaining hip connection and adjusting your base to follow their movement.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip sequence and body positioning without a partner (0% resistance), 10 reps.
    2. Partner drill: Assume back mount, practice the grip and slight hip drive (25% resistance), 5 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill: Add controlled choking pressure without full resistance (50% resistance), 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner drill: Apply the choke with increasing pressure, allowing partner to tap early (75% resistance), 5 reps per side.
    5. Live rolling: Focus on securing back mount and attempting the choke when opportunities arise (90% resistance), 3 minutes.
    6. Live rolling: Full application with intention to finish, understanding tap out signals (100% resistance), 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have successfully secured the back mount position with both hooks in.
    • When your opponent is turtled or attempting to defend from their back.
    • When you have established control and can prevent them from turning into you.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Chin Tuck: Opponent tucks their chin tightly to their chest, preventing the choking arm from reaching the carotid artery. The counter is to use your free hand (the one gripping your wrist) to pry their chin up, creating space for the choke.
    • Turning into the Choke: Opponent attempts to turn their head or body towards the choking arm. The counter is to maintain your hip connection and adjust your base, following their movement while keeping the choke tight, or to switch to a body triangle.
    • Arm Escape (Arm-Drag Defense): Opponent tries to pull the choking arm across their body. The counter is to maintain your grip and continue the squeeze, or to transition to a different submission if they create an opening.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try the Rear Naked Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you are gripping your own wrist too tightly or in an awkward angle, causing hyperextension. The pressure should primarily come from your forearm and bicep squeezing the carotid artery. Instead of a death grip on your own wrist, use it as a stable anchor point to drive your choking forearm deeper. Ensure your thumb is pointed towards the ceiling when you grip your own wrist.

    Q: How can I apply the Rear Naked Choke against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are crucial. Focus on achieving a tight 'seatbelt' grip (your hand on their far shoulder) to control their posture and prevent them from posturing up. When applying the choke, ensure your hips are driving forward to create a stable base and that your choking forearm is deep under their chin, not just on their jaw. The pressure comes from your bicep and forearm, not brute strength.

    Q: When is the best time to go for the Rear Naked Choke?

    The optimal time to attempt the Rear Naked Choke is immediately after securing the Back Mount position, before your opponent has time to establish strong defensive posture or escape. Look for moments when they are distracted or trying to scramble out of your hooks. A common mistake is waiting too long, allowing them to create space and defend effectively. Prioritize getting the choke set up quickly and decisively.
