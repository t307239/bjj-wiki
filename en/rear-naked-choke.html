Rear Naked Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

This technique is primarily initiated from the **back-Mount** position, aiming to secure a submission by restricting blood flow to the brain.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of positional control, leading to the opponent escaping or the choke being ineffective and potentially dangerous.

The key mechanical insight is establishing a secure "seatbelt" grip and then using your bicep and forearm to apply controlled pressure, not brute force.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Establish a secure **seatbelt grip**: Your non-choking arm wraps over the opponent's shoulder, hand gripping your own bicep. Your choking arm slides under their chin, palm facing away from you.
    2. Lock your hands: Interlace your fingers or grip your own bicep with your choking hand's thumb.
    3. Isolate the head: Pull the opponent's head towards your chest, creating a slight flexion at their neck.
    4. Drive your shoulder into their neck: Your choking shoulder should press firmly into the side of their carotid artery.
    5. Engage your choking arm's bicep: Flex your biceps to create a constricting force.
    6. Use your forearm: Simultaneously, apply pressure with your forearm against the back of their neck.
    7. Maintain hip pressure: Keep your hips elevated and close to the opponent's back to prevent them from creating space and turning in.
    8. Adjust your grip: Ensure your choking hand's thumb is pointing upwards, towards their ear, for optimal pressure on the carotid artery.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to squeeze with only arm strength: This can lead to bicep tears and shoulder impingement in the choking arm, as the muscles are not designed for sustained, high-force compression. Instead, engage your core and use your body weight.
    • Grabbing the opponent's chin or jaw with the choking hand: This can cause wrist sprains and damage to the temporomandibular joint (TMJ). The choke should be applied to the side of the neck, compressing the carotid arteries.
    • Letting your hips drop too low when on their back: This creates space for the opponent to turn in and escape the position, risking a sweep or reversal, and allowing them to break your grip easily. Keep your hips elevated and connected.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0%): Practice the grip sequence and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the hand placement and arm angles. (10 reps)
    2. Static partner drill (25%): With a compliant partner in back-mount, practice establishing the seatbelt and then the choke grips without any resistance. (10 reps)
    3. Controlled choke application (50%): Partner remains still. Apply the choke with light pressure, focusing on the feeling of your bicep and forearm compressing the neck. Partner taps early. (10 reps)
    4. Partner resistance on grips (75%): Partner offers slight resistance to your grip establishment. Focus on securing the seatbelt and then smoothly transitioning to the choke. (10 reps)
    5. Full back control, no submission attempt (90%): Maintain back mount and seatbelt control for 30 seconds, focusing on hip position and preventing escapes. (5 reps)
    6. Live rolling (100%): Attempt the Rear Naked Choke from back mount during sparring, focusing on maintaining position and applying the choke correctly. (3 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have secured **back-mount** with a strong seatbelt grip.
    • When the opponent is turtled and you are able to establish hooks.
    • After a sweep or takedown where you land in a dominant back control position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • **Escape the seatbelt**: If the opponent's choking arm is not fully secured, try to peel their arm off your shoulder and create space to turn into them or escape the position.
    • **Defend the neck**: Tuck your chin to your chest and try to create space with your arms by pushing against the opponent's choking arm or shoulder. This is most effective before the choke is fully locked in.
    • **Break the grip**: If the opponent has locked their hands, focus on creating space by shrimping your hips away and then using your arms to pry their hands apart, specifically targeting the grip on your own bicep.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Rear Naked Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs if you are gripping too tightly with your choking hand around your own bicep, or if your hand is angled incorrectly. Ensure your choking hand's palm is facing away from you, and your thumb is pointing upwards towards their ear. The primary pressure comes from your bicep and forearm, not from a death grip on your own arm.

    Q: How can I do a Rear Naked Choke against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on superior body mechanics and leverage rather than raw strength. Ensure your seatbelt grip is tight and your hips are elevated, preventing them from using their weight effectively. Drive your shoulder into their neck and use your bicep and forearm to create a controlled, consistent pressure, letting the mechanics do the work.

    Q: I can't get the Rear Naked Choke to work, is it because I'm not pulling hard enough?

    Pulling harder is often the opposite of what's needed. The Rear Naked Choke is a blood choke, not a windpipe choke. The effectiveness comes from compressing the carotid arteries with your bicep and forearm, supported by your shoulder and controlled by your body position. Focus on securing the proper grip and creating a tight, consistent pressure by squeezing your biceps and rotating your forearms.
