Rear Naked Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Rear Naked Choke is typically applied from the back-Mount position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain. White belts often struggle by relying on brute strength instead of proper leverage and choke mechanics.

The key to a successful Rear Naked Choke lies in creating a "choke frame" with your arms and creating spinal extension in your opponent, not just squeezing.

Understanding the pressure on the carotid arteries and the jugular veins is paramount for effective application and avoiding injury.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the 'S' Grip: Place your choking arm's hand (e.g., right hand) across the opponent's neck, thumb-side down, palm facing their throat. Your non-choking arm (e.g., left arm) reaches over the opponent's shoulder, grabbing your own right bicep.
    2. Lock the Grip: Deeply insert your right forearm under their chin, ensuring it contacts their windpipe and one carotid artery. Your left bicep should be firmly pressed against the side of their neck, creating a "frame."
    3. Secure the Back Mount: Maintain both hooks (feet) inside the opponent's thighs, knees driving towards their hips, preventing them from turning away. Keep your chest tight to their back.
    4. Initiate Spinal Extension: Lean your upper body slightly backward, creating a subtle pulling motion with your choking arm's elbow and your non-choking arm's bicep. This extends their spine.
    5. Compress the Carotid Arteries: Squeeze your biceps together and slightly drive your elbow back. This action compresses both carotid arteries simultaneously, restricting blood flow.
    6. Control Head Position: Use your forehead to gently control the angle of their head, ensuring it's not turned away from the choke. Your head should be tight to their head.
    7. Maintain Pressure: Keep your hips low and close to their hips. Any forward or backward pressure should be controlled and synchronized with your choke mechanics.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke by simply squeezing the neck with your arms: This can damage the trachea (windpipe) and cervical vertebrae due to excessive pressure on the spinal column. The correct method is to compress the carotid arteries.
    • Gripping the opponent's head with your choking arm's forearm instead of their neck: This can strain your shoulder joint (rotator cuff) and lead to a weak choke. The forearm must be positioned under the chin, against the neck.
    • Trying to "pull" the opponent's head down with your choking arm: This shifts the pressure to the opponent's jaw and can cause neck strain for both parties. Focus on creating spinal extension and compressing the arteries.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo grip practice: From a seated position, practice forming the 'S' grip and bicep lock 20 times per side.
    2. Partnered visualization drill (0% resistance): With a partner in back mount, practice assuming the correct position and grip without any pressure. 10 repetitions.
    3. Static choke application drill (25% resistance): Partner remains still. You apply the choke, focusing on proper grip and forearm placement. Hold for 5 seconds. 5 repetitions.
    4. Controlled choke drill (50% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance by trying to slightly turn their head. You focus on maintaining the choke and adjusting your body. 5 repetitions.
    5. "Escape" drill (75% resistance): Partner attempts a basic escape from the back mount. You maintain the choke position, focusing on re-securing if they create space. 5 repetitions.
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Apply the Rear Naked Choke in rolling situations, focusing on correct setup and application. Aim for 3 successful applications within a 5-minute round.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have secured dominant back-mount position with both hooks in.
    • When the opponent is posturing up and creating space, allowing you to reach their neck.
    • After a successful sweep or takedown that transitions you directly to their back.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Tap": The most effective counter is to acknowledge the submission and tap the opponent or the mat to avoid injury.
    • "Defend the choking arm": If the choking arm is not yet fully secured, the opponent can try to peel the choking arm off their neck with their own hands.
    • "Turn into the choke": While difficult, a skilled opponent might try to turn their head into the choking arm, creating space and relieving pressure. This is often a last resort.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Rear Naked Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs if you are gripping too tightly with your choking hand or if the pressure is being applied through your wrist. Ensure your forearm is deep under the chin, and focus on squeezing your biceps together while driving your elbow back slightly. This distributes the pressure across your forearm and bicep, not just your wrist.

    Q: How can I apply a Rear Naked Choke against a bigger opponent when I'm smaller?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are key. Focus on getting your choking arm extremely deep under their chin, ensuring your forearm is flush against their carotid artery. Use your body weight by keeping your hips low and driving your chest into their back. Lean back slightly to create spinal extension, which makes the choke tighter without needing excessive strength.

    Q: I can't seem to finish the Rear Naked Choke, it feels loose. What am I doing wrong?

    A common issue is not creating a tight "choke frame." Ensure your non-choking arm is firmly locked onto your own bicep, creating a strong wall on the side of their neck. Also, verify you are leaning back slightly to extend their spine and simultaneously squeezing your biceps together. This two-pronged approach of frame and compression is crucial.
