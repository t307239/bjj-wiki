Your First Rear Naked Choke: A White Belt's Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This choke is primarily applied from the back-Mount position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain. It's a submission that can end a match quickly.

White belts often struggle because they rely on raw strength instead of leverage and precise body positioning. This leads to inefficient application and missed opportunities.

The key is creating a strong "figure four" grip that compresses the carotid arteries against the trapezius muscle and the opponent's own shoulder.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the opponent's back with both hooks in (feet placed on the inside of their thighs).
    2. Place your right arm across the opponent's neck, allowing their head to rest in the crook of your elbow.
    3. Reach your left hand to grip your own right bicep, creating the "figure four" frame.
    4. Turn your right palm inwards so your thumb is pointing towards your face, which helps engage the carotid artery.
    5. Drive your right shoulder forward and down, tucking your chin to your chest to create a strong, stable frame.
    6. Squeeze your biceps and forearms together, while simultaneously lowering your chest and tightening your grip.
    7. Maintain your body weight distribution by keeping your hips low and your base wide, preventing the opponent from escaping your back control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke by simply pulling back with your arms: This can strain your shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) and is mechanically inefficient for blood restriction. The correct movement involves forward shoulder pressure and a squeezing action.
    • Gripping too low on the opponent's neck: This can choke the trachea (windpipe) instead of the carotid arteries, leading to a less effective choke and potential vocal cord damage. Always aim for the side of the neck, just below the jawline.
    • Allowing your head to be pulled away from the opponent's head: This creates space and breaks the choke's integrity, potentially leading to a strained neck (cervical spine) if the opponent uses it to escape. Keep your head tight and glued to the opponent's head.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the "figure four" grip and shoulder tuck motion on a dummy or pillow, focusing on the squeeze. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill (static): With a willing partner on their hands and knees, practice securing the back and applying the choke without resistance. Focus on grip and body positioning. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner allows you to take the back and attempts minimal defense. Focus on maintaining control and executing the choke. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Partner actively tries to defend by shrugging their shoulders or turning their head. Focus on adjusting your grip and pressure. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    5. Positional sparring (back control): Start with you in back mount, partner tries to escape. If they fail to escape within 30 seconds, you attempt the choke. (10 rounds, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Attempt the Rear Naked Choke during rolling if the opportunity arises. Focus on correct application, not just finishing. (5 minutes, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have secured strong back control with both hooks in.
    • When the opponent is turtled and you can isolate one arm for the choke.
    • When the opponent attempts to bridge or roll and you can flatten them out on their back.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Chin tuck and head rotation: The opponent tucks their chin tightly to their chest and rotates their head away from the choking arm, creating space.
    • Shoulder shrug: The opponent elevates their shoulder on the choking side, making it difficult for the arm to reach the carotid artery.
    • Elbow frame: The opponent uses their free arm to create a frame between their neck and your choking arm, preventing a tight grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do a Rear Naked Choke?

    Wrist pain often occurs when you're gripping your own bicep too far down the arm or with your palm facing outwards. Ensure your thumb is pointing towards your face when gripping your bicep, and your palm is facing inwards. This alignment utilizes the forearm and bicep muscles more effectively and protects the wrist joint from hyperextension.

    Q: How can a white belt finish a Rear Naked Choke against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are paramount. Focus on getting your choking arm deep under their chin, then use your shoulder and chest to drive forward, not just your arm strength. Squeeze your biceps together to create a consistent pressure that compresses their carotid arteries. Ensure your hooks are tight to prevent them from turning into you.

    Q: When should I stop trying to force the Rear Naked Choke if it's not working?

    If you've applied the choke and the opponent is actively defending by tucking their chin or creating a frame, and you're not getting any reaction after 5-7 seconds of consistent pressure, it's time to disengage. Continuing to force it can lead to fatigue or allow the opponent to create an escape opportunity. Instead, transition to another technique or reassess your position.
