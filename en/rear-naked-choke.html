Rear Naked Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

WHITE★★☆☆☆Beginner
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

The Rear Naked Choke is initiated from the back-Mount position, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain for a submission.

White belts often struggle by using excessive, uncontrolled force rather than precise leverage, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is compressing the carotid arteries against the trapezius muscle using your bicep and forearm.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the back-mount position, ensuring both hooks are inside the opponent's thighs, with your chest pressed firmly against their upper back.
    2. Reach your right arm across the opponent's neck, placing your right bicep against their carotid artery on the right side.
    3. Your right forearm should run across the front of their neck, with your right hand gripping your own left bicep, creating a "frame" against their shoulder.
    4. Simultaneously, bring your left arm underneath their chin, reaching your left hand to grip your right forearm, securing the "choke grip."
    5. Drive your right shoulder forward and down, simultaneously pulling your left forearm back and up, creating opposing forces.
    6. Lower your hips slightly while maintaining chest pressure, ensuring your weight is distributed over their upper torso.
    7. Squeeze your right bicep into their right carotid artery and use your left forearm to apply counter-pressure against the left side of their neck, effectively strangling both arteries.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grasping the opponent's head with the choking arm: This often leads to the opponent's chin tucking, making the choke ineffective and straining the rotator cuff of the choking arm due to excessive internal rotation.
    • Applying pressure solely with the forearm across the throat: This can cause discomfort and damage to the trachea (windpipe) instead of the carotid arteries, leading to a non-submission and potential injury.
    • Over-extending the hips backward while choking: This shifts your weight off the opponent, allowing them to escape and potentially hyperextending your own lumbar spine, risking a back injury.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of grips and arm positioning (0% resistance): 10 reps per side.
    2. Partner drilling with light positional control, focusing on securing back-mount and initiating the grip without pressure (25% resistance): 5 reps.
    3. Partner drilling with controlled application of choke pressure, focusing on the opposing arm movement (50% resistance): 5 reps.
    4. Partner drilling with increasing pressure, allowing the partner to tap early (75% resistance): 5 reps.
    5. Drilling the full Rear Naked Choke sequence with light defensive pressure from the partner (90% resistance): 3 reps.
    6. Live rolling, focusing on securing the back and attempting the Rear Naked Choke when opportunities arise (100% resistance): 2 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    • When you have successfully secured the back-mount position with both hooks in.
    • When the opponent attempts to turn into you from the back and you can maintain control.
    • After a successful sweep or takedown that transitions you directly to their back.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to do the Rear Naked Choke?

    Wrist pain during the Rear Naked Choke often stems from gripping your own bicep too tightly with the choking hand or over-extending your wrist backward. Instead, focus on a relaxed grip on your bicep and keep your wrist in a neutral or slightly flexed position, allowing your forearm to apply the pressure, not your wrist joint.

    Q: How can I do the Rear Naked Choke against a bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise body positioning are crucial. Ensure your hooks are deep and your chest is firmly pressed against their back to prevent them from using their size to create space. Drive your shoulder forward and use your hips to create a strong frame, rather than relying on brute strength.

    Q: I can't seem to finish the Rear Naked Choke, even when I think I have it. What am I doing wrong?

    The most common reason for this is not achieving the correct "choke grip." You need to ensure your choking arm's bicep is pressed against one carotid artery, and your forearm is across the front of their neck, with your other hand gripping your forearm. The finishing pressure comes from the opposing forces of your bicep and forearm compressing the arteries, not just squeezing with your hands.
