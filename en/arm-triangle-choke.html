Arm Triangle Choke: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Arm Triangle Choke is a submission applied from various dominant positions, most commonly mount or side control, aiming to restrict blood flow to the brain. White belts often struggle due to insufficient understanding of leverage and pressure, leading to ineffective attempts and potential injury. Success hinges on creating a stable, crushing platform with your body, rather than relying solely on arm strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From mount, secure your opponent's head with your armpit, creating a Gable grip with your hands behind their head.
    2. Drive your chest into their chest, ensuring your hips are heavy and your knees are wide for base.
    3. Step your leg closest to their head over their neck, positioning your shin across their airway.
    4. Drive your opposite shoulder into their neck, perpendicular to their spine.
    5. Tighten the choke by squeezing your knees together and crunching your torso forward, compressing their carotid artery against your shoulder.
    6. Maintain head control with your Gable grip, preventing them from posturing up or turning their head.
    7. Ensure your hips remain heavy, preventing them from bridging or escaping by tilting their weight.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the choke by simply pulling their head down with your arms: This can strain your own shoulder joint (rotator cuff and labrum) by forcing it into an unnatural external rotation and abduction. The correct action is to drive your shoulder into their neck and use your body weight.
    • Not establishing a strong base with your knees and hips: This allows your opponent to bridge or shrimp, potentially causing you to lose position and leading to awkward twists that can injure your lumbar spine. Maintain a wide, stable base.
    • Allowing their head to turn away from the choke: This can lead to hyperextension of your own neck as you try to chase their head, risking cervical strain. Keep their head trapped with your armpit and chest.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling of the body positioning and hip pressure without a partner (10 reps).
    2. Partner drilling with zero resistance, focusing on grip placement and body alignment (20 reps).
    3. Partner drilling with light resistance, focusing on driving the shoulder and closing the space (30 reps).
    4. Partner drilling with 50% resistance, practicing the full sequence with controlled pressure (40 reps).
    5. Partner drilling with 75% resistance, simulating a realistic submission attempt (50 reps).
    6. Live rolling, attempting the Arm Triangle Choke with full intent and resistance (5 minutes, 3 rounds).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a stable mount position and your opponent is turtled or trying to escape.
    • When you have secured side control and your opponent is facing away or attempting to turn into you.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and you can transition to a controlling position like mount or side control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Opponent turns their head away: Trap their head with your armpit and drive your shoulder into their neck, then use your free hand to secure their far arm and pull it across their body to complete the choke.
    • Opponent bridges and turns into you: Use your Gable grip to maintain head control, step your leg over their head, and use your hips to drive forward and down, creating a tight lock.
    • Opponent attempts to stack you: Maintain a tight grip and drive your shoulder into their neck, keeping your hips heavy. If they get underneath you, transition to a knee-on-belly or side control to re-establish control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Arm Triangle Choke from mount?

    Your neck might hurt because you are relying too much on your arms to pull their head down instead of using your body weight to create compression. Ensure your shoulder is driving into their neck and your torso is crunching forward. Keeping your hips heavy and your base wide prevents awkward neck movements.

    Q: How do I finish the Arm Triangle Choke against a much larger and stronger opponent from mount?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on establishing a very tight lock and maximizing your body weight. Ensure your shin is firmly across their airway and your shoulder is deep on their neck. Squeeze your knees together and use your entire torso to apply pressure, rather than just your arms. Control their posture by keeping your head tight to their head.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to the Arm Triangle Choke from side control?

    The optimal time is when your opponent is trying to turn into you or is posturing up in an attempt to escape. As they turn, you can secure their head and arm, driving your shoulder into their neck. This moment of transition is when their body is less organized and more vulnerable to the choke.
