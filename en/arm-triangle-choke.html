The Arm Triangle Choke is a submission that cuts off blood flow to the brain by compressing the carotid artery using the opponent's own shoulder and arm against your chest and bicep.

White belts often struggle by trying to force the choke with raw strength instead of creating the correct angles and pressure points.

The key is understanding how to use your body as a vise, leveraging your hips and chest to create a complete seal and maximum compression.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard, secure a Collar Grip with your right hand deep in the opponent's left collar, thumb inside.
    2. With your left hand, secure a Sleeve Grip on the opponent's right sleeve, specifically the wrist area.
    3. Begin to break the opponent's posture by pulling their head down with your right arm and driving your hips up.
    4. As the opponent defends by posturing up, slide your right arm across their neck, ensuring your bicep is pressing against their carotid artery.
    5. Your right shoulder should be pressed into the opponent's left shoulder, creating a crossface.
    6. Simultaneously, bring your hips towards the opponent's head, creating a perpendicular angle to their torso.
    7. Trap the opponent's right arm by securing your right hand to your left bicep (your right bicep is now against their neck).
    8. Drive your chest into the opponent's shoulder and trap their arm, creating a tight seal.
    9. Squeeze your arms together and drive your hips forward, compressing the carotid artery.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'squeeze' the choke with your arms alone without proper body positioning: This can lead to excessive strain on your shoulder joint (rotator cuff impingement) and elbow joint (ligament strain) as you try to force closure.
    • Correct: Focus on driving your hips forward and chest into the opponent's shoulder to create the necessary pressure.
    • Arching your back excessively to try and create leverage: This puts immense stress on your lumbar spine, risking facet joint strain or disc compression.
    • Correct: Keep your core engaged and drive with your hips, maintaining a neutral spine.
    • Overextending your neck to 'reach' for the choke: This can lead to hyperextension of the cervical spine, potentially causing muscle strains or nerve impingement.
    • Correct: Ensure your arm is already across the neck before initiating the final squeeze, maintaining a neutral neck position.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip transitions and body positioning without a partner. Focus on the hip angle and arm placement. (50 reps, 0% resistance)
    2. Partner drill: With a compliant partner in closed guard, practice the initial posture break and sliding the arm across the neck. Focus on the setup. (30 reps, 25% resistance)
    3. Partner drill: Progress to trapping the opponent's arm and securing your bicep grip. Focus on the 'squeeze' mechanics, not submission. (30 reps, 50% resistance)
    4. Partner drill: Work on completing the choke from the established position, with the partner tapping early. Focus on the full sequence. (20 reps, 75% resistance)
    5. Flow roll: Attempt the Arm Triangle Choke during a light rolling session, focusing on timing and recognizing opportunities. (5 rounds, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling: Apply the Arm Triangle Choke in a full-speed rolling scenario, aiming for successful submission. (10 rounds, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When the opponent is turtled or attempting to escape from your closed guard.
    • When the opponent is posturing up and you have established a strong collar grip.
    • When you have successfully isolated an arm and created an angle from side control.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Hip Escape to create space and relieve pressure: When the choke is being applied, the opponent can hip escape away to create space, breaking the seal.
    • Grapevine legs and bridge: The opponent can use their legs to grapevined your legs and bridge their hips, creating an angle to escape the pressure.
    • Posture up and create frames: The opponent can attempt to regain posture by pushing your hips away and creating frames with their hands on your shoulders.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my wrist hurt when I try to finish the Arm Triangle Choke?

    Wrist pain during the Arm Triangle Choke is often due to gripping too tightly with your fingers and trying to 'bend' your wrist to create pressure. Instead, focus on keeping your wrist neutral and driving your chest and hips forward. Your bicep should be the primary 'squeezing' point against the carotid, not your forearm or fingers.

    Q: How can I apply the Arm Triangle Choke against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is to prioritize superior positioning and leverage over brute strength. Ensure your arm is deep across their neck and your bicep is firmly against their carotid. Drive your hips towards their head and use your chest to pin their shoulder, creating a tight seal. Focus on maintaining a perpendicular angle to their body; this magnifies your pressure.

    Q: When should I switch from a collar grip to a bicep grip in the Arm Triangle Choke?

    You don't typically switch from a collar grip to a bicep grip. The collar grip initiates the setup by breaking posture and controlling the head. Once your arm is across the neck, you then secure your *own* bicep with your free hand (e.g., right hand grips left bicep) to complete the choke. The opponent's arm is trapped between your chest and your own arm, not directly gripped by your free hand.
