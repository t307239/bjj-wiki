Arm Triangle Choke: A Precise BJJ Technique Guide for White Belts

BLUE★★★☆☆Intermediate
Blue Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Blue Belt Guide

The Arm Triangle Choke is typically applied from side-control or mount, aiming to constrict blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries.

White belts often struggle with proper pressure and body alignment, leading to ineffective chokes and potential self-injury.

The key is to create a strong "figure-four" lock with your arms and use your chest and shoulder to drive into the opponent's neck and arm.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From side-control, establish a cross-face with your far arm, placing your forearm across the opponent's face, perpendicular to their jawline.
    2. Secure a "collar grip" with your near hand, digging your fingers deep into the opponent's gi at the base of their neck.
    3. Begin to "turtle" your body slightly by driving your near hip forward and away from the opponent's head, creating space.
    4. Thread your near arm (the one on the same side as the opponent's head) underneath their armpit and across their chest, aiming for your far shoulder to touch their neck.
    5. Lock your hands: Grip your own bicep of the arm that is under their armpit (this forms the "figure-four" grip).
    6. Drive your chest and shoulder into the opponent's carotid artery and the back of their elbow, creating a crushing pressure.
    7. Rotate your hips slightly towards the opponent's head, using your core to tighten the choke and your legs to maintain a stable base.
    8. Maintain downward pressure with your chest and a slight upward pull on your own bicep to complete the constriction.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Trying to "squeeze" with arm strength alone: This overloads the shoulder and elbow joints, risking rotator cuff tears or elbow hyperextension.
    • Incorrect head placement, allowing the opponent to turn into you: This can lead to a knee or elbow strike to your face, or a compromised neck position.
    • Not driving your chest into the opponent's neck: This fails to constrict the carotid artery effectively, requiring excessive, potentially injurious, squeezing.
    • Allowing the opponent to "stack" you: This puts immense pressure on your spine and can lead to disc injury, especially if your base is narrow.
    • Releasing the collar grip prematurely before securing the bicep: This allows the opponent to escape the initial setup and potentially counter.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hand fighting, arm threading, and figure-four grip connection without a partner. (50 reps each side)
    2. Static partner drill (0% resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence from side-control to the locked-out choke position. Focus on correct body angles and pressure. (20 reps each side)
    3. Light resistance drill (25% resistance): Partner offers minimal resistance to your arm movement and body positioning. Focus on maintaining control and proper pressure. (15 reps each side)
    4. Controlled resistance drill (50% resistance): Partner actively defends by trying to create frames or turn their head. Focus on maintaining the choke setup and adjusting pressure. (10 reps each side)
    5. Semi-live drill (75% resistance): Partner attempts to escape the submission and regain guard. Focus on finishing the choke or transitioning if the defense is successful. (5 reps each side)
    6. Live rolling (90-100% resistance): Apply the Arm Triangle Choke in sparring, focusing on timing and completion. Be prepared to defend counters. (Round robin)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When in side-control and the opponent turtles their head away, exposing their neck.
    • When applying a pressure pass from side-control and the opponent tries to bridge or shrimp, creating an opening.
    • When in mount and the opponent turns their head to defend a potential armbar or americana.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Stacking" the opponent: If the opponent attempts to stack you, widen your base and drive your hips into their hips, preventing them from collapsing your chest.
    • "Head and Arm Escape" (Turtle defense): The opponent turns away and tucks their chin, bringing their far arm over their head to defend the choke, creating a space to escape.
    • "Shrimping and Recovering Guard": The opponent uses their hips to create space, allowing them to shrimp their hips back and bring their legs between you to re-establish guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my shoulder hurt when I try the Arm Triangle Choke from side control?

    Your shoulder likely hurts because you are trying to "squeeze" with arm strength, which puts excessive strain on the rotator cuff. Instead, focus on driving your chest into the opponent's carotid artery and using your core to create the pressure. Ensure your far arm is securely locked around their neck and your near hand is gripping your own bicep to form the figure-four lock.

    Q: How do I finish the Arm Triangle Choke against a much larger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on achieving a very tight "figure-four" lock and driving your chest into their neck and shoulder. Do not rely on brute strength. Instead, use your hips and core to apply pressure, and subtly adjust your body angle. Ensure your shoulder is deep in their neck and your bicep is firmly locked. You may need to "walk" your hips closer to their head to increase the choke's effectiveness.

    Q: Transitioning to an Arm Triangle from closed guard is less common and more advanced. Typically, it's initiated when the opponent attempts to "stack" you from closed guard. As they drive forward, you can use that momentum to thread your arm around their neck and secure the choke, or to create an opportunity to transition to side-control and apply it there. Focus on maintaining control of their posture.
