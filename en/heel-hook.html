Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The heel hook is a submission targeting the knee joint, typically initiated from leg entanglement positions like the 50-50 guard or inside sankaku. It aims to hyperextend or twist the knee, forcing a tap.

White belts often struggle with heel hooks due to a lack of control, poor understanding of joint mechanics, and an inclination to muscle the submission rather than use leverage.

The critical insight is isolating the opponent's heel while creating rotational pressure on the tibia, forcing the knee to bend in an unnatural direction.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard position, secure your opponent's foot by placing your forearm across their ankle and your other forearm behind their heel, creating a "cup" with your hands.
    2. Your hips should be perpendicular to your opponent's leg, creating a stable base and preventing them from easily turning out.
    3. Drive your hips forward and slightly up, extending your legs to tighten the grip on the heel.
    4. Your chest should be pressed against your opponent's shin, preventing them from bridging or escaping.
    5. Maintain a strong grip on the heel, using your forearms to pull their foot towards your body.
    6. Apply pressure by straightening your legs and slightly rotating your hips away from the opponent's knee.
    7. The knee joint will experience a twisting or hyperextension force; maintain this pressure until the tap.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to "crank" the heel hook with shoulder or arm strength: This can result in ligament tears (ACL, MCL) in the knee as the joint is forced to rotate or hyperextend beyond its natural range of motion. The correct action is to drive hips forward and extend legs to create leverage.
    • Letting the opponent's foot slip from your grip due to insufficient forearm pressure: This allows the opponent to escape and can lead to their foot snapping back, potentially causing a knee hyperextension or sprain for the attacker. Always maintain a firm grip by cupping the heel and pressing forearms into the ankle and Achilles.
    • Bridging your hips excessively without hip control: This can cause your own knee to buckle or twist unnaturally, risking meniscus tears or ligament damage. Keep your hips anchored and drive them forward into the opponent's shin.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip on a training dummy's foot (or your own leg). Focus on isolating the heel and establishing the forearm pressure. (50 reps per side)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice entering the 50-50 guard and securing the heel hook grip. Focus on hip and body positioning. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Partner attempts to resist the grip by slightly pulling their leg away. You focus on maintaining control and creating a stable base.
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance - 50%): Partner actively tries to escape the position. You focus on finishing the submission mechanics without applying full pressure.
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Partner defends the submission with active movements. You practice applying the submission with increasing control, stopping just before causing discomfort.
    6. Live rolling (90%): Incorporate the heel hook setup into sparring rounds, focusing on clean entries and finishes. Tap immediately if caught. (5-minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is in your closed guard and you manage to break their posture and isolate one of their legs.
    • When you achieve a 50-50 guard position, offering a direct path to attacking the heel.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and their leg becomes accessible for entanglements.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape the entanglement: If the heel hook is being applied, immediately try to rotate your body towards the attacker's hips to relieve pressure. This involves turning your hips inward.
    • Straighten your leg: If possible, attempt to straighten your leg to remove the bend from the knee joint, reducing the rotational stress. This requires significant hip mobility.
    • "Stacking" the opponent: If the attacker's hips are not anchored, try to "stack" them by driving your weight onto their chest and head. This can break their base and loosen their grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a heel hook on my partner, even though they haven't tapped?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are applying rotational force to your own knee joint, not the opponent's. This happens when your hips are not properly aligned or your base is unstable. Ensure your hips are perpendicular to the opponent's leg and drive them forward to create leverage on their heel, rather than twisting your own body.

    Q: How can I safely learn heel hooks as a white belt without injuring my training partners?

    Start with extensive solo drilling and partner drills with zero resistance. Focus on understanding the grip mechanics and body positioning. Only progress to light resistance when you can consistently secure the grip and position without causing discomfort. Always communicate with your partner and tap early if you feel any undue pressure.

    Q: The optimal time to transition is after you have broken your opponent's posture and are able to isolate one of their legs. This typically involves controlling their upper body to prevent them from posturing up, then using your legs to "climb" up their body and entangle their leg for the heel hook. Avoid attempting it while they are still postured high.
