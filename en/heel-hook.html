BJJ Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Heel Hook is a powerful leg lock targeting the knee joint, primarily initiated from positions like the 50-50 guard or when an opponent's leg is isolated.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of control over their own body mechanics and an eagerness to 'finish' the submission prematurely, leading to injury.

The key insight is creating a stable base while simultaneously isolating the opponent's heel and applying a rotational force through hip extension, not just leg strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a stable 50-50 guard position, ensuring your hips are square to your opponent's hips.
    2. Secure the opponent's foot by placing your forearm across their instep, gripping your own bicep with your opposite hand.
    3. Your other arm reaches under their knee, gripping your own bicep, creating a figure-four lock around their calf.
    4. Drive your hips forward, creating a strong posterior tilt of your pelvis.
    5. Extend your legs, pushing your hips away from the opponent's heel.
    6. Rotate your hips counter-clockwise (if attacking the opponent's right leg) while maintaining the figure-four grip.
    7. Apply pressure by continuing hip extension and the rotational force, focusing on the knee joint's external rotation.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive upward knee pressure when your hips are not properly tilted: This can lead to a torn ACL or MCL as the tibia is forced into unnatural rotation relative to the femur.
    • Gripping the opponent's foot too loosely and allowing their heel to slip: This results in loss of control and can cause the opponent to escape, potentially leading to you falling awkwardly and injuring your own knee.
    • Attempting to 'crank' the submission with your arms alone: This ineffective method puts immense strain on your own shoulder and elbow joints, risking dislocation or ligament tears, instead of using your hips for leverage.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip tilt and figure-four grip on your own leg 20 times.
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Practice the entire sequence from 50-50 guard with a compliant partner, holding the submission for 5 seconds. 10 repetitions.
    3. Partner drilling (light resistance): Partner actively tries to defend by moving their hips. Focus on maintaining the grip and hip pressure. 10 repetitions.
    4. Positional sparring (controlled): Start in 50-50 guard. The defender attempts to escape. The attacker focuses on maintaining the correct mechanics. 3 rounds, 3 minutes each.
    5. Controlled rolling (focus on leg locks): Both partners are allowed to attempt leg locks, but with explicit communication and tapping on any submission attempt. 3 rounds, 5 minutes each.
    6. Live rolling (full resistance): Execute the Heel Hook within the context of a full roll, prioritizing safety and tapping early.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard with your opponent's leg trapped.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you from a compromised position, exposing their heel.
    • When you can isolate an opponent's leg during guard passing exchanges, creating an opportunity.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Escape: If the attacker's hips are not squared, drive your hips away and attempt to spin your heel out, rotating your knee to face the attacker.
    • Hip Pressure and Stacking: If the attacker has a loose grip, apply heavy hip pressure and stack their upper body to create space and break their control.
    • Foot Switch and Re-Guard: If the attacker's grip is solid, try to switch your trapped foot to the outside and use your free leg to shrimp away and re-establish guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my heel hook not feel tight and my opponent just taps their leg?

    This often occurs because you are not creating sufficient hip extension and posterior pelvic tilt. The rotational force needs to originate from your hips, not just your legs. Ensure your forearm is deep across their instep, gripping your bicep securely, and that your hips are actively driving forward and away from their heel.

    Q: How can I apply a heel hook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on precise control and leverage rather than raw strength. Secure your figure-four grip tightly around their calf, ensuring their heel is firmly trapped. Drive your hips forward with maximum extension and use your entire body weight to create the rotational torque on their knee.

    Q: The optimal time is when you have established a dominant leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard, or when an opponent's leg is exposed during a guard pass attempt. Avoid forcing the submission if the position is not secure, as this can lead to you being swept or put in a worse position yourself.
