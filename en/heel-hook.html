Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Guide

The heel hook is a submission targeting the knee joint by applying rotational pressure to the heel. It's typically initiated from positions where the opponent's leg is isolated, such as the 50-50 guard or inside sankaku.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of joint mechanics, leading to uncontrolled movements and potential injury. They might 'pull hard' without proper body alignment, missing the precise leverage points.

The key mechanical insight is creating a stable base and using your hips to drive into the opponent's heel, isolating the ankle and knee for maximum torque with minimal force.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Entry Grip: From a position like the 50-50 guard, secure your opponent's heel with your armpit. Your forearm should be pressed against the sole of their foot, and your bicep against their heel pad.
    2. Body Alignment: Position your hips perpendicular to your opponent's leg line, creating a straight line from your hips to their heel. Your chest should be tight against their thigh.
    3. Locking the Leg: Use your opposite leg to hook behind your opponent's thigh, trapping their knee. Ensure your shin is across their hamstring.
    4. Hip Drive: Initiate a forward hip drive, pushing your hips towards your opponent's heel. This creates tension and isolates the ankle.
    5. Achieving Apex: As you drive your hips, simultaneously pull your opponent's heel towards your chest with your arm. Your torso should be upright, not slumped over.
    6. Finishing Pressure: Maintain hip pressure and a tight grip. The rotation comes from your hips driving forward and slightly down, creating torque on the heel.
    7. Release: To release, simply reduce hip pressure and loosen your grip on the heel. Never 'hang on' once the submission is achieved.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Hip Angle: Turning hips parallel to the opponent's leg instead of perpendicular. This places excessive shear force on the ACL and MCL, risking ligament tears.
    • Swaying Upper Body: Leaning back or sideways with your upper body instead of driving your hips forward. This strains the lower back and fails to isolate the ankle, potentially hyperextending the knee.
    • Gripping Too Loosely: Not securing the heel pad firmly with your forearm and bicep. This allows the opponent's heel to slip, leading to a loss of control and potential knee hyperextension if the leg is still trapped.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Drill (0% Resistance): Practice the grip and body positioning on a dummy or a willing partner who remains completely still. Focus on hip angle and chest tightness. (20 reps)
    2. Passive Partner Drill (25% Resistance): Partner allows you to secure the heel and position your hips, but offers no active defense. Focus on creating the hip drive and apex. (15 reps)
    3. Controlled Resistance Drill (50% Resistance): Partner offers mild resistance to your entry and hip drive, but does not actively try to escape. Focus on maintaining control and pressure. (10 reps)
    4. Simulated Escape Drill (75% Resistance): Partner attempts to escape the hold by turning their hips and pulling their leg out. You focus on maintaining the lock and hip pressure. (5 reps)
    5. Flow Rolling with Focus (90% Resistance): During rolling, specifically look for opportunities to enter the heel hook position. Partner defends actively but can tap early. (3 rounds)
    6. Live Rolling (100% Resistance): Attempt heel hooks within the context of a live roll, respecting your partner's tap. Focus on safe application and quick submission if achieved. (5 rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is turtled and you can isolate one leg from behind.
    • When you achieve the 50-50 guard and can control their heel.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and their leg is extended and vulnerable.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Heel Hook, even if I don't finish the submission?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not creating the correct biomechanical alignment. Instead of driving your hips into the opponent's heel to create rotational torque, you might be pulling with your upper body or twisting your own knee. Ensure your hips are perpendicular to their leg and your hips are driving forward, isolating the ankle joint for rotation.

    Q: How can I apply a Heel Hook against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on isolating their heel securely with a tight grip and ensuring your hips are directly behind their heel. Your hip drive needs to be strong and consistent, using your entire body weight to create pressure. Do not try to match their strength; instead, use their size against them by controlling their limb's movement.

    Q: When is the right time to attempt a Heel Hook as a white belt?

    As a white belt, the safest and most effective times to attempt a heel hook are when you have achieved a dominant leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard, or when your opponent's leg is extended and vulnerable during a failed Guard Pass attempt. Avoid attempting heel hooks from unstable or compromised positions where you could easily be swept or end up in a dangerous situation yourself.
