Beginner's Guide to the BJJ Heel Hook

The Heel Hook is a leg lock targeting the knee joint by applying rotational force to the heel. It is typically initiated from control positions like the 50-50 guard or a specific leg entanglement.

White belts often rush this technique, neglecting fundamental body positioning and grip integrity. This leads to missed submissions and, more importantly, increased injury risk for both practitioners.

The key mechanical insight is creating a stable base and using your hips to drive forward, forcing the opponent's heel into the lock without relying solely on upper body strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the opponent's leg by placing your forearm across the front of their thigh, just above the knee. Your bicep presses into their hamstring.
    2. Hook your armpit deep around their ankle, ensuring your shoulder is snug against their heel.
    3. Position your hips perpendicular to the opponent's leg, creating a strong base. Your hips should be slightly elevated, not flat on the mat.
    4. Grip your own bicep with your opposite hand, creating a "figure-four" lock around their ankle. Ensure your knuckles are facing upwards.
    5. Drive your hips forward and slightly down, simultaneously pulling your bicep towards your chest. This action isolates the heel.
    6. Maintain pressure on the opponent's knee by keeping your chest connected to their thigh. Do not allow them to straighten their leg.
    7. Apply a controlled, steady pressure by extending your hips and squeezing your arms. Listen for verbal submission or tap.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure before establishing a secure grip: This can lead to the opponent escaping and creates excessive strain on the knee ligaments (ACL, MCL) as the joint is forced into unnatural rotation without proper control.
    • Not keeping hips perpendicular to the opponent's leg: This allows the opponent to rotate their hips and relieve pressure, potentially leading to a hyperextension or twisting injury of the knee if force is applied incorrectly.
    • Rushing the submission by "pulling hard" with the arms: This puts immense stress on the ankle and Achilles tendon, risking a tear, and fails to utilize the powerful hip drive needed for a safe submission.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip movement and arm positioning without a partner, focusing on the feeling of creating the "figure-four" grip and driving hips forward (10 reps).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice securing the leg and applying the "figure-four" grip. Focus on hip placement and body alignment (5 reps per side).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice controlling their leg and initiating the hip drive. Focus on maintaining control throughout the movement (5 reps per side).
    4. Controlled submission drilling: Apply the heel hook with approximately 25% of your maximum force, allowing your partner to tap early and often. Focus on smooth transitions and proper body mechanics (5 reps per side).
    5. Controlled submission drilling (increased intensity): Increase the submission force to 50%, emphasizing maintaining control and listening for the tap. Your partner should communicate their discomfort clearly (5 reps per side).
    6. Live rolling (specific situation): In a controlled rolling scenario, attempt the heel hook from a pre-defined position (e.g., after securing a 50-50 guard). Focus on timing and execution, not necessarily finishing (2 minutes of rolling time, 1 attempt per roll).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established control of both of the opponent's legs in a "50-50" guard position.
    • When you have successfully transitioned to a "4-11" or "honeyhole" leg entanglement.
    • When your opponent attempts a leg drag pass and you can invert to secure their leg.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "The Wall": If the opponent is attempting to apply the heel hook, immediately drive your hips into their body and attempt to "wall off" their hips, preventing them from extending their own hips to create leverage.
    • "The Escape Turn": While the opponent is applying pressure, turn your hips away from the heel hook direction. This often relieves pressure and allows for a potential escape or transition to a better position.
    • "The Counter Leg Grab": As the opponent secures your heel, attempt to grab their opposite leg or arm to create a distraction and disrupt their base, opening a window for escape.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my heel hook attempt feel weak and easy for my opponent to escape?

    This often happens when your hips are not properly aligned perpendicular to your opponent's leg. Your hips should be driven forward, creating a stable base and leverage. Ensure your grip is a tight "figure-four" around their ankle and that you are using your hips to drive into their leg, not just pulling with your arms.

    Q: How can I safely apply a heel hook against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Focus on controlling their leg's rotation rather than brute force. Ensure your hips are driving forward to create a fulcrum, and maintain a strong "figure-four" grip. Prioritize isolating their heel and applying steady pressure rather than explosive movements.

    Q: Is it safe to practice heel hooks with other white belts who don't know the proper technique?

    Practicing heel hooks with other white belts requires extreme caution. Always start with 0% resistance and focus on perfect mechanics. Communicate clearly with your partner about tapping early and often. Your instructor should closely supervise these drills to prevent injuries due to improper application.
