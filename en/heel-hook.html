Mastering the Heel Hook in BJJ: A White Belt's Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The heel hook is a powerful leg lock that attacks the knee and ankle joint by applying rotational torque.

It's typically initiated from positions where you have control of your opponent's leg, like the 50-50 guard or inside sankaku.

White belts often fail by applying excessive, uncontrolled force, leading to potential injury for both parties.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure opponent's leg: Isolate the opponent's leg by controlling their ankle with your armpit and their knee with your opposite hand (e.g., right armpit on their left ankle, left hand on their left knee).
    2. Establish your base: Sit up and swing your hips to your side, creating a perpendicular angle to your opponent's leg line.
    3. Control the foot: Grip the opponent's foot with your hands, knuckles facing upwards, thumb wrapped around the instep.
    4. Create the "figure four": Bring your opposite arm (e.g., left arm) across the opponent's shin, securing it with your bicep against their calf.
    5. Lock the grip: Interlock your hands, ensuring your elbows are tight against your own body.
    6. Hip extension and rotation: Extend your hips upwards and slightly rotate your torso away from the opponent's leg.
    7. Apply pressure: Maintain a stable base and slowly drive your hips forward and slightly up, applying a twisting pressure to the heel and ankle.
    8. Control the knee: Ensure your chest is pressed into the opponent's thigh to prevent them from turning their knee out, which can dissipate pressure.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly "pulling" the leg towards you: This can hyperextend the opponent's knee joint, causing ACL/MCL tears.

    The correct action is to extend your hips away from the opponent and rotate your torso.

    • Applying pressure before securing the "figure four" grip: This allows the opponent's leg to escape and can lead to a compromised position for you.

    Always ensure your grip is solid before initiating hip extension.

    • Bending your own knee excessively during the lock: This puts your own knee at risk of injury if the opponent counters or escapes aggressively.

    Keep your legs relatively straight and use your hips for power generation.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo grip and body positioning drill: Practice securing the leg, establishing the figure four, and rotating your hips without a partner. 50 reps per side.
    2. Partnered static control drill: With a compliant partner, practice isolating their leg and setting up the heel hook position. 10 reps per side, 25% resistance.
    3. Partnered controlled movement drill: Partner resists slightly as you transition into the heel hook. Focus on hip movement and grip security. 10 reps per side, 50% resistance.
    4. Live rolling with "tap early, tap often" rule: Focus on attempting the heel hook from established positions. 5 minutes, 75% resistance.
    5. Controlled sparring with submission focus: Both partners actively attempt submissions, including heel hooks. 5 minutes, 90% resistance.
    6. Full sparring: Apply the heel hook technique as part of a regular rolling session. 5 minutes, 100% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established strong control of your opponent's leg from the 50-50 guard.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and their leg becomes accessible to you.
    • When you are in a position like the inverted 50-50 guard and can isolate the opponent's heel.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "Stacking" the opponent: The opponent can drive their hips and upper body weight directly onto your hips and torso, collapsing the space and relieving pressure.
    • "Turning the corner": The opponent can attempt to rotate their body and leg in the opposite direction of the heel hook, essentially "unscrewing" their own leg.
    • "Knee shield" or "frame": The opponent can use their free leg to create a frame against your hips or chest, preventing you from extending your hips effectively.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my heel hook feel weak and my opponent can easily escape?

    This often happens when your hip angle is not perpendicular to your opponent's leg. Ensure your hips are rotated so your body is sideways to their leg line. Also, check that your "figure four" grip is tight and your elbows are tucked in, preventing any slack in the mechanics.

    Q: How can I apply a heel hook against a much larger opponent in No-Gi BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precise mechanics are crucial. Focus on securing their leg tightly and using your hip extension to create the rotational torque. Do not rely on pure strength. Prioritize isolating the heel and ankle, ensuring a solid "figure four" grip before initiating the hip drive.

    Q: Is it okay to "pull" my opponent's heel when doing a heel hook?

    No, you should not "pull" their heel in the traditional sense. The primary force comes from extending your hips away from your opponent and rotating your torso. This hip extension drives your own body into their heel, creating the necessary torque, rather than a pulling motion.
