Beginner's Guide to the BJJ Heel Hook

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Heel Hook is a powerful leg lock targeting the opponent's ankle and knee, aiming to hyperextend the knee or twist the ankle. It is typically attacked from positions where you have control of the opponent's leg, such as the 50-50 guard or inside sankaku.

White belts often struggle because they lack the precise body control and hip mechanics required, leading to uncontrolled movements and potential injury. Fear of applying it incorrectly or facing retaliatory attacks also hinders their progress.

The core principle is isolating the opponent's heel and using your body's leverage, specifically hip rotation and leg extension, to apply torque to the knee and ankle joint without compromising your own structure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Enter the 50-50 guard by securing both of your opponent's legs between your own, ensuring your hips are square to their hips.
    2. Grip the opponent's heel with your arm closest to it, placing your forearm across the arch of their foot and your hand cupping their heel.
    3. Secure your own ankle with your opposite hand, creating a "figure-four" grip around the opponent's foot.
    4. Extend your hips away from the opponent's hips while simultaneously driving your knees together, creating tension.
    5. Rotate your hips counter-clockwise if attacking the opponent's right leg (or clockwise for their left), while keeping your back flat on the mat.
    6. Apply upward pressure with your grip on the heel and downward pressure with your chest against their shin, forcing knee hyperextension.
    7. Maintain a stable base with your non-attacking leg, ensuring it is not exposed to counter-attacks.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the heel hook with uncontrolled "pulling" motion: This can cause the opponent's knee ligaments (ACL/MCL) to tear due to shearing forces if the angle is incorrect.
    • Incorrect grip leading to slippage: If the heel slips from the grip, your arm can become vulnerable to an armbar or the opponent can escape the position.
    • Over-extending your own knee while applying pressure: This can lead to hyperextension injuries in your own knee joint, especially if the opponent is significantly heavier.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the grip and figure-four setup on your own leg, focusing on the hip rotation and extension mechanics. (50 reps per side)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a partner lying on their back, practice entering the 50-50 and establishing the heel hook grip and body position. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner resists by lightly tucking their leg. Focus on maintaining control and completing the setup. (10 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Partner actively tries to defend by posturing or turning. Focus on maintaining the lock and finishing. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the heel hook from established 50-50 positions, with your partner tapping immediately upon feeling pressure. (5 attempts per round, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full): Attempt the heel hook during rolling, prioritizing safety and tapping if you feel any discomfort or if your partner taps. (100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have successfully entered the 50-50 guard and secured both of your opponent's legs.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg becomes isolated within your guard.
    • When you are in a position like the Ashi Garami and can transition to the 50-50.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Escape (Turn-in): If the opponent attempts to heel hook you, turn your hips *into* the direction of the heel hook, relieving pressure and potentially escaping the grip.
    • Straighten Leg / Posture Up: If the opponent has established the grip but not yet applied significant pressure, attempt to straighten your leg and create space by driving your hips away from them.
    • Sweep / Re-Guard: If the opponent is setting up the heel hook from a less dominant position, attempt a sweep or transition to a guard to reset.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Heel Hook?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are applying the pressure incorrectly. Instead of a controlled rotation and extension using your hips, you might be trying to force the lock with raw strength or an improper angle. Ensure your hips are square to your opponent's and that you are driving your knees together while extending your hips away. This biomechanically sound pressure targets the ankle and heel, not the knee joint itself.

    Q: How can I safely learn the Heel Hook as a white belt without injuring my training partners?

    Safety is paraMount. Start by drilling the mechanics solo, focusing on the grip and body positioning. Then, progress to controlled drills with compliant partners who understand the importance of tapping early. Always communicate with your partner and emphasize tapping as soon as any pressure is felt. Never apply full force until you and your partner are highly experienced and comfortable.

    Q: What is the difference between an inside and outside Heel Hook?

    The terms 'inside' and 'outside' heel hook refer to the direction of the ankle's rotation relative to the opponent's body. An outside heel hook attacks the opponent's heel from the outside, causing internal rotation of the ankle and knee. An Inside Heel Hook attacks from the inside, causing external rotation. Both target the same ligaments but are applied from slightly different angles and grip configurations.
