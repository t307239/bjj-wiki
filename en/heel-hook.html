Mastering the BJJ Heel Hook: A White Belt Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Heel Hook is a submission targeting the knee joint by applying rotational pressure to the heel. It's typically initiated from positions where you can isolate an opponent's leg, like the 50-50 guard or outside Single Leg. White belts often fail because they lack control and apply pressure incorrectly, leading to injury for both themselves and their partner.

The key to a successful and safe Heel Hook is understanding that you are not "pulling" the leg, but rather "crowding" the heel with your body and using precise hip movement to create torque. This isolates the rotational force on the knee's ligaments.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the opponent's foot by placing your forearm across their ankle, thumb pointing upwards, and your other hand gripping their heel with your palm. Maintain a closed fist.
    2. Position your hips perpendicular to the opponent's leg, ensuring your hips are lower than their knee. Your torso should be angled towards their hip.
    3. Place your opposite leg's knee against the opponent's thigh, just above the knee joint, to prevent them from turning their leg out.
    4. Drive your hips forward and towards the opponent's knee while simultaneously arching your back slightly.
    5. Rotate your hips clockwise (if attacking the opponent's right leg) or counter-clockwise (if attacking the opponent's left leg), creating a twisting motion on their heel.
    6. Apply pressure by driving your hips into their knee and slightly upwards, forcing a rotational hyperextension of the knee.
    7. Maintain your grip on the heel and ankle throughout the movement, ensuring your elbow remains tucked to your body to prevent limb escape.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Knee Placement: A white belt might place their knee on the opponent's shin instead of their thigh. This allows the opponent to easily turn their leg out, compromising the lock and risking a knee-on-knee collision causing a MCL or LCL tear.
    • "Pulling" the Leg: Attempting to "pull" the opponent's leg towards you with arm strength. This fails to generate proper torque and can lead to the opponent escaping or you losing positional control, potentially resulting in a hyperextended knee for yourself if you pull too hard.
    • Excessive Hip Rotation: Over-rotating the hips without proper control or a stable base. This can cause a dangerous shearing force on the opponent's ACL or PCL, as well as strain your own hip flexors and lower back if not executed with precision.
    • Lack of Hip Control: Not using the hips to "crowd" the heel and create a solid fulcrum. This results in the pressure being distributed inefficiently, leading to a weak submission attempt and potential damage to your own elbow or shoulder if you try to compensate with arm strength.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation Drill: Practice the grip and hip positioning on a training dummy or a rolled-up gi. Focus on achieving the correct hip angle and grip security. (10 reps each side)
    2. Controlled Partner Transition (0% resistance): From a common entry position (e.g., 50-50 guard), your partner allows you to secure the heel hook grip and hip position without resistance. Focus on the mechanics. (5 reps each side)
    3. Slow Motion Execution (25% resistance): In the same position, your partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to slowly move through the mechanics of the submission. Focus on the hip drive and torque. (5 reps each side)
    4. Adding Hip Drive (50% resistance): Your partner now offers slight resistance to your hip drive, allowing you to feel how to overcome it with your own body weight and hip movement. (5 reps each side)
    5. Adding Leg Defense (75% resistance): Your partner actively tries to defend by turning their leg or pulling it out, forcing you to adjust your hip and body angles to maintain control and complete the submission. (5 reps each side)
    6. Controlled Live Rolling (90% resistance): In a live roll, focus on executing the Heel Hook from the entry position. Your partner will defend realistically, but the focus is on successful application and tap-out. (3 minutes, focus on Heel Hook attempts)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From the 50-50 guard, when you have successfully isolated one of your opponent's legs and achieved a strong heel grip.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg becomes accessible, allowing you to transition into a leg entanglement like the 50-50 or inside sankaku.
    • During scrambles or transitions where an opponent's leg is left exposed, presenting an opportunity for a quick leg lock attempt.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • The Turn-Out Escape: The opponent immediately turns their hip away from the attacker, creating space and disengaging the knee joint from the fulcrum. This requires the attacker to release the grip or risk injury themselves.
    • The "Stack" Defense: The opponent "stacks" their weight onto the attacker's hips, driving their hips down and forward. This compresses the attacker's body, making it difficult to maintain hip drive and torque, and allows for a potential escape or counter-grapple.
    • The "Heel Peel": The opponent uses their free leg to hook the attacker's attacking leg and "peels" it off their heel. This requires precise timing and coordination to break the attacker's grip and control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Heel Hook?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are applying the rotational force incorrectly. Instead of driving your hips forward to "crowd" the heel and create torque, you might be trying to "pull" the leg or twist your own knee. To fix this, focus on keeping your attacking leg straight and your hips low and perpendicular to your opponent's leg, then drive your hips forward to create the rotation on their heel, not your own knee.

    Q: How do I Heel Hook a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. You cannot rely on strength. Focus on securing a deep, clean grip on their heel and ankle. Then, use your hips and core to generate the rotational force, ensuring your knee is positioned correctly on their thigh to prevent them from turning out. Your body weight needs to be used to "crowd" their heel, not to out-muscle them.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt a Heel Hook against a beginner?

    The best time to attempt a Heel Hook against a beginner is when they are relatively stationary and their leg is accessible, such as when they are trying to pass your guard and their leg is extended. Avoid attempting it when they are actively moving or in a position where they can easily Sprawl or turn out, as this increases the risk of injury for both parties. Always ensure your partner is aware and comfortable with leg lock attempts.
