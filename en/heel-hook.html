The heel hook is a powerful leg lock targeting the knee and ankle, typically initiated from a 50-50 guard or when an opponent's leg is isolated. It achieves a submission by hyperextending the knee or rotating the ankle.

White belts often fail due to a lack of control, over-reliance on brute strength, and neglecting proper body positioning. This leads to missed opportunities and increased injury risk for both parties.

The single most critical mechanical insight is isolating the opponent's heel and creating torque through precise hip extension and ankle flexion, not just pulling.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the opponent's leg by placing your outside arm between their thighs, gripping their pant leg just above the knee with your hand. Your forearm should be flush against their inner thigh.
    2. Place your other arm on the outside of their leg, gripping their heel with your palm facing upwards and fingers curled around the heel bone. This is the 'heel grip'.
    3. Position your hips so they are perpendicular to the opponent's body, creating a strong base. Your hips should be slightly elevated off the mat.
    4. Bring your outside leg (the one not controlling the opponent's hip) across their thigh, pinning it with your knee against their hamstring. This prevents them from turning out.
    5. Drive your hips forward, away from the opponent's body, while simultaneously pulling their heel towards your body with your heel grip. This creates internal rotation at the knee.
    6. Flex your foot, pulling your toes towards your shin, to further isolate the ankle joint and increase torque on the heel bone.
    7. Maintain chest-to-chest pressure and keep your hips tight to your opponent's torso. Your body should form a figure-four around their leg.
    8. To finish, continue the hip drive and heel pull. The submission occurs when the opponent feels a sharp, twisting pressure in their knee or ankle and taps.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive force by 'pulling hard' with arm strength alone: This can lead to the opponent's knee ligament (ACL/MCL) tearing due to uncontrolled rotation or hyperextension.
    • Incorrectly gripping the opponent's foot by grabbing the toes or arch: This fails to isolate the heel and can result in a hyperextended ankle or a knee-bar attempt, potentially damaging the talocrural joint or causing a knee hyperextension.
    • Allowing the opponent to turn their hips out: This releases the torque on the knee and can lead to a knee-bar for the opponent, risking a torn ACL or hamstring injury.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation Drill: Practice securing the heel grip and establishing the 'figure-four' body position on a training dummy or a willing partner's stationary leg. (5 minutes, 0% resistance)
    2. Controlled Leg Placement Drill: Partner lies on their back, you are in a neutral position. Practice entering the 50-50 guard and securing the heel grip. Partner offers minimal resistance to leg placement. (5 minutes, 25% resistance)
    3. Static Heel Hook Drill: From 50-50 guard, secure the heel hook. Partner remains completely still. Focus on hip drive and heel pull mechanics for 5 seconds, then release. (10 reps, 50% resistance)
    4. Dynamic Entry and Control Drill: Start from a neutral position. You attempt to enter the 50-50 and secure the heel hook. Partner actively defends but does not counter-attack forcefully. (5 minutes, 75% resistance)
    5. Controlled Submission Drill: From 50-50, secure the heel hook. Apply pressure slowly and controlled, waiting for a tap. Partner communicates pressure and taps early. (10 reps, 90% resistance)
    6. Live Rolling with Leg Lock Focus: Spar with a designated partner, focusing *only* on leg lock entries and finishes. Both partners agree to tap early and communicate any discomfort. (3 minutes rounds, 100% simulated intensity)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard and have isolated the opponent's heel.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg becomes exposed and accessible.
    • When you are in a compromised position (e.g., bottom of side control) and can quickly transition to isolate an opponent's leg.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Turn-in Defense: When the heel hook is applied, immediately turn your hips towards the opponent's hips, creating a perpendicular angle. This relieves pressure and allows you to potentially escape or attack.
    • Leg Escape (Straightening): If possible, attempt to straighten your leg by pushing off the opponent's hip with your free foot. This can create space to pull your heel out and regain a neutral position.
    • Counter-Attack (Sweep/Submission): While the opponent is focused on the heel hook, look for opportunities to sweep them or apply a counter-submission like a triangle choke or armbar if they overcommit.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a heel hook?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are not correctly isolating the opponent's heel. Instead of creating torque on the heel bone and ankle, you're likely forcing a rotational pressure directly onto the knee joint's ligaments (ACL, MCL). Ensure your grip is firmly around the heel bone, and your hip drive is creating external rotation of the femur against a stable tibia.

    Q: How can I safely learn heel hooks as a white belt without getting injured?

    The safest way to learn heel hooks as a white belt is through controlled drills with experienced partners and instructors. Focus on understanding the biomechanics: grip isolation, hip positioning, and controlled pressure. Always tap early to any discomfort, and never apply full, sudden force until you have achieved mastery in drills. Your instructor will guide you on when and how to introduce resistance.

    Q: I can't get a good heel hook because my opponent keeps their leg too straight. What do I do?

    When an opponent keeps their leg straight, they are often trying to prevent the rotational torque. You need to create that torque by anchoring their heel firmly with your grip and driving your hips forward and slightly to the side, perpendicular to their body. This forces internal rotation at the knee. You can also use your free leg to pin their other leg or their hip, preventing them from straightening out entirely.
