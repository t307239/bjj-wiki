Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The heel hook is a powerful leg lock targeting the knee joint's internal rotation. It is typically initiated from positions where the opponent's leg is isolated, such as the 50-50 guard or Ashi Garami variations.

White belts often rush this technique, neglecting foundational control and creating openings for escapes or counter-attacks. They also tend to 'muscle' the submission, increasing injury risk for both parties.

The key mechanical insight is to create a stable platform with your body to isolate the opponent's foot and apply a controlled, rotational torque to their knee, focusing on hip extension rather than brute force.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from a secured Ashi Garami position (e.g., opponent's leg between your legs), establish a 'figure-four' grip on the opponent's ankle: your right hand grips their heel, and your left hand grips their foot just above the toes, fingers pointing towards their shin.
    2. Position your hips directly behind the opponent's knee, ensuring your pelvis is perpendicular to their thigh bone.
    3. Drive your left knee into the opponent's hip crease, creating a strong base and preventing them from easily turning into you.
    4. Extend your right leg straight, pushing your hips forward. This action creates a fulcrum with your pelvis.
    5. Simultaneously, pull your opponent's foot towards your chest with your figure-four grip, applying a slight inward rotation to their foot.
    6. Arch your back, driving your hips forward and upward. This hip extension is the primary source of power for the submission.
    7. Maintain pressure with your left knee on their hip. Your goal is to create a rotational shear force on the knee joint, not a crushing force.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Turning your hips away from the opponent while attempting to pull their foot: This allows the opponent's knee to hyperextend laterally, risking an ACL (Anterior Cruciate Ligament) tear.
    • Grabbing the opponent's foot with an open palm and applying upward pressure: This can lead to a calf slicer injury by compressing the gastrocnemius muscle against the tibia.
    • Applying the submission with straight legs and no hip extension: This relies on shoulder and arm strength, failing to utilize the biomechanical advantage of hip drive and increasing strain on your own joints.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling the grip and hip positioning: Practice the figure-four grip on a dummy or pillow, focusing on hip placement and pelvis angle. 100 reps.
    2. Partner drill - static position control: With a compliant partner, assume the Ashi Garami and practice establishing the figure-four grip and hip connection. 50 reps per side.
    3. Partner drill - slow submission application (25% resistance): Apply the heel hook motion slowly, focusing on the hip extension and rotational pull, with the partner providing minimal resistance. 25 reps per side.
    4. Partner drill - controlled submission (50% resistance): Apply the heel hook with moderate pressure, allowing the partner to tap early. Focus on the feeling of the torque. 50 reps per side.
    5. Partner drill - live submission attempts (75% resistance): In rolling, actively seek the heel hook position. The partner will defend realistically, but the submission is not applied with full force. 5 minutes.
    6. Live rolling (100% resistance): Execute the heel hook with full intention, respecting the tap. Focus on clean mechanics and situational awareness. 10 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have successfully isolated one of the opponent's legs in a position like 50-50 guard or Ashi Garami.
    • When the opponent attempts to stack or pass your guard, leaving their leg exposed.
    • When you have established a strong hip connection and control of the opponent's foot and ankle.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Escape by turning your hips towards the attacker: This changes the angle of attack and relieves pressure on the knee joint. Simultaneously, try to bring your free leg back to defend.
    • Straighten your leg and 'leg drag' your foot out: By extending your leg and pulling it towards your hips, you can often create enough space to disengage from the grip.
    • Stack and face the attacker: If the attacker is applying pressure, try to 'stack' them by driving your hips forward and facing them. This can disrupt their base and control.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Heel Hook?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are trying to 'muscle' the submission instead of using biomechanical leverage. When you pull with your arms and shoulders without proper hip extension and pelvic isolation, you create uneven pressure on the knee joint. Focus on driving your hips forward and arching your back to create a rotational force through your pelvis, which is the correct mechanism for a heel hook.

    Q: How can I do a Heel Hook against a bigger opponent who is strong?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Do not try to overpower them. Focus on securing a tight figure-four grip on their ankle and controlling their foot. Ensure your hips are firmly planted behind their knee and drive them forward. The key is to use your body weight and hip extension to create torque, rather than relying on raw strength. A strong knee-on-hip connection will also help prevent them from using their size to stack you.

    Q: When is the right time to go for a Heel Hook in a roll?

    The optimal time to attempt a heel hook is when you have successfully isolated one of your opponent's legs and established control, typically from a guard position like the 50-50 or an Ashi Garami. This often occurs when an opponent attempts a Guard Pass or when you manage to sweep them and land in a leg entanglement. Avoid attempting it when your own body is not in a stable, controlled position, as this can expose you to counter-attacks.
