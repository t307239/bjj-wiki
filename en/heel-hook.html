The Heel Hook is a powerful leg lock targeting the knee joint, primarily initiated from positions like the 50/50 guard or when an opponent's leg is isolated.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of rotational forces and overreliance on brute strength, leading to injury.

The key mechanical insight is to control the opponent's hip and create a fulcrum with your own hip to isolate and hyperextend the knee.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure the opponent's foot by placing your armpit over their ankle, with your forearm on the outside of their shin.
    2. Grip their heel with your hand, fingers pointing towards their toes, creating a "cupped" hand grip.
    3. Hook your arm around their knee, ensuring your bicep presses against the back of their knee joint.
    4. Position your hips perpendicular to their leg, creating a 90-degree angle between your torso and their shin.
    5. Drive your hips towards their heel, contracting your glutes to create pressure.
    6. Use your free arm to control their hip, preventing them from turning or escaping by placing your hand on their hip bone.
    7. Apply upward pressure with your hips while simultaneously pulling their heel towards your own hip, creating rotational stress on their knee.
    8. Maintain control of their foot with your grip and knee with your arm throughout the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Grasping the shin instead of the heel: This allows the opponent to rotate their foot and escape, while potentially causing hyperextension of your own elbow if they resist.
    • Not controlling the opponent's hip: This allows them to turn their body and relieve pressure, often resulting in your own knee being put in danger.
    • Pulling with the arms only: This fails to create the necessary rotational torque, leading to ineffective attempts and potential shoulder strain for the attacker.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip and hip positioning without a partner. (50 reps per side)
    2. Static partner drill: With a compliant partner, practice securing the grip and establishing hip position. (25 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    3. Controlled entry drill: Partner allows you to enter the position, and you practice maintaining control without applying pressure. (20 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    4. Slow submission drill: Apply the submission slowly, allowing the partner to tap early. Focus on the hip and heel mechanics. (15 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    5. Guard retention and transition drill: Practice entering the heel hook from a specific guard, while partner attempts to defend. (10 reps per side, 90% resistance)
    6. Live rolling with submission focus: Attempt the heel hook during live rolling, but tap immediately if the situation feels compromised. (5 minutes, 100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • From 50/50 guard when your opponent's heel is accessible and their hips are not fully squared.
    • When you have successfully isolated an opponent's leg during a guard pass attempt and they are on their back.
    • When your opponent attempts a leg entanglement and you can reverse the position to attack their heel.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Spinning to face the attacker: If the attacker's hip is not perpendicular to your leg, attempt to spin your hips to face them, relieving pressure.
    • "Stacking" the attacker: If the attacker is not controlling your hip, drive your hips forward and stack their upper body onto your legs, breaking their base.
    • "Uncle Rick" defense: If the attacker has a loose grip, attempt to pull your heel out by rotating your foot outwards and lifting your knee away from their body.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my heel hook attempt feel weak and my opponent can easily escape?

    This often occurs because you are not establishing a strong hip-to-hip connection. Ensure your hips are perpendicular to your opponent's shin, creating a tight frame. Additionally, your grip on the heel must be firm, cupping the heel with your fingers pointing towards their toes, to prevent them from rotating their foot and breaking the lock.

    Q: How can I safely practice the heel hook against a larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on technique and leverage over strength. Prioritize securing a deep, controlling grip on their heel and isolating their leg effectively. Use your hips to create the rotational force, rather than trying to muscle it. Always drill with a compliant partner and communicate clearly about the pressure being applied.

    Q: Is it safe to attempt a heel hook if I don't have perfect hip control?

    Attempting a heel hook without proper hip control significantly increases the risk of injury for both you and your opponent. Your hips act as the fulcrum, and without this control, the force can be misapplied, leading to hyperextension or awkward twisting of the knee joint. Focus on mastering the hip mechanics in drills before applying in live situations.
