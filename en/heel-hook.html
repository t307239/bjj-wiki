Heel Hook Technique for BJJ White Belts

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Heel Hook is a leg lock targeting the opponent's heel to hyperextend the knee or twist the ankle. It's typically initiated from positions like the 50-50 guard or when controlling an opponent's leg.

White belts often rush this submission, neglecting fundamental control and creating dangerous openings for themselves and their partners. They may also apply pressure incorrectly, leading to injury.

The key is isolating the opponent's foot and ankle with your armpit and using your hips to create a fulcrum, driving your shin into their heel to apply rotational pressure.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard or similar leg entanglement, secure your opponent's foot by placing your armpit directly over their heel.
    2. Grip your own shin with your opposite hand, creating a "figure-four" lock around their ankle, ensuring your forearm is flush against their Achilles tendon.
    3. Position your hips so they are perpendicular to your opponent's torso, creating a strong base and allowing for hip drive.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly upward, bringing your chest closer to your opponent's knee.
    5. Simultaneously, drive your shin (the one on the same side as your controlling armpit) into the opponent's heel.
    6. Use your gripping arm to pull their foot towards your chest, increasing the pressure on their heel.
    7. Maintain a tight grip on your own shin and keep your hips engaged; avoid letting your hips drift away or your back round out.
    8. Apply pressure by driving your heel hook shin into their heel and simultaneously pulling their foot towards your chest, creating a twisting motion.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying pressure by "squeezing" your legs together: This can overextend the opponent's knee or ankle joint beyond its natural range of motion, causing ACL, MCL, or meniscus tears.
    • Incorrectly gripping the opponent's foot, allowing their heel to slip: This can lead to hyperextension of the opponent's ankle, damaging ligaments like the ATFL or CFL.
    • Trying to "crank" the submission without proper hip and shin engagement: This often results in uncontrolled torque, potentially causing a fracture or severe ligament damage to the opponent's ankle or knee.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the hip-to-shin and armpit-to-heel isolation without a partner. Focus on the feeling of the "figure-four" grip. (50 reps per side)
    2. Partnered drilling (0% resistance): With a willing partner, practice securing the heel hook position from a controlled 50-50. Partner offers no resistance to grip. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partnered drilling (25% resistance): Partner attempts to lightly resist by preventing leg isolation but does not actively defend the heel hook. Focus on maintaining control and hip position. (15 reps per side)
    4. Partnered drilling (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to escape the 50-50 and prevent the heel hook setup, but does not apply counter-pressure to the lock. Focus on maintaining your grip and hip angle. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partnered drilling (75% resistance): Partner defends the heel hook as they would in a rolling scenario, but taps immediately upon feeling significant pressure. Focus on finishing the technique with control. (5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling (90% intensity): Practice applying the heel hook in a controlled rolling environment, with immediate tap upon submission. Emphasis on situational awareness and safety. (3 rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When your opponent is in your 50-50 guard and their leg is accessible and isolated.
    • When you have successfully transitioned to a leg entanglement where their heel is exposed and your hips can drive forward.
    • During scrambles where you can quickly secure a leg lock position before your opponent stabilizes.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Defense 1 (Leg Escape): The opponent attempts to "spin out" by rotating their hips away from the heel hook, creating space to untangle their leg. This requires precise hip and shoulder movement.
    • Defense 2 (Injury Prevention Tap): The opponent immediately taps the mat with their free hand or foot to signal submission and avoid injury.
    • Defense 3 (Counter-Attack): The opponent attempts to "stack" or drive their weight onto the attacker, or conversely, to attack the attacker's free leg to create a stalemate or transition.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my heel hook feel weak and not tap my opponent?

    A weak heel hook often stems from poor hip positioning and insufficient shin-to-heel pressure. Ensure your hips are perpendicular to your opponent's torso, creating a solid base for driving forward. Drive your shin directly into their heel, not just the calf, and pull their foot towards your chest simultaneously to create the necessary rotational torque.

    Q: How can I safely practice Heel Hooks as a white belt without injuring my training partners?

    Safety is paraMount. Always drill with a clear understanding of the mechanics and use a "tap early, tap often" mentality. Focus on slow, controlled movements in the drill progressions, ensuring your partner can easily communicate any discomfort. Never 'crank' the submission; apply steady, increasing pressure until your partner taps.

    Q: What's the difference between an inside and outside heel hook, and which one should I learn first?

    An Inside Heel Hook attacks the opponent's heel from the same side as their body, while an outside heel hook attacks from the opposite side. The biomechanics are very similar, but the entry points and body positioning differ slightly. For white belts, focus on mastering the fundamental mechanics of isolating the heel and applying pressure from a stable position like the 50-50, regardless of whether it's an inside or outside attack.
