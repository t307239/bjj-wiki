BJJ Heel Hook Technique: A White Belt's Precise Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The heel hook is a powerful leg lock targeting the knee and ankle, typically initiated from a 50-50 guard or Inside Heel Hook position. Its goal is to hyperextend the knee or twist the ankle, forcing a submission.

White belts often rush this technique, focusing on 'pressure' without understanding joint mechanics, leading to ineffective attacks and high injury risk. Proper body alignment and controlled tension are paraMount.

The key mechanical insight is creating a stable fulcrum with your body to isolate the opponent's foot and ankle, allowing precise torque application on the heel bone to stress the knee joint's ligaments.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Assume a 50-50 guard position, ensuring your hips are perpendicular to your opponent's hips. Your lead leg's knee should be pointed towards your opponent's head.
    2. Secure your opponent's foot with your armpit, ensuring their heel is trapped against your hip bone. Your grip should be "S"-shaped, with your fingers pointing towards their toes.
    3. Place your secondary leg (the one not attached to the heel) over your opponent's thigh, creating a "figure four" grip to prevent them from turning their leg out.
    4. Your torso should be angled towards your opponent's hips, creating a stable base. Your weight should be distributed forward, loading your hips into their knee.
    5. To initiate the lock, draw your hips back slightly while simultaneously pushing your opponent's heel away from you with your armpit grip. This creates a rotational force.
    6. Maintain a tight "S" grip on the heel, ensuring your thumb is on the outside of their foot and your fingers curl around the heel bone. Your elbow should be tucked in.
    7. As you draw your hips back and push the heel away, your opponent's knee will naturally begin to hyperextend and rotate, stressing the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL).

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying excessive, uncontrolled rotational force directly to the opponent's ankle. This can cause a severe ankle sprain by tearing the lateral or medial collateral ligaments of the ankle joint.
    • Attempting to 'crank' the lock with arm strength alone without proper hip extension. This can lead to a hyperextension injury of the opponent's knee, damaging the ACL or PCL.
    • Grabbing the opponent's foot too loosely, allowing their heel to slip. If the foot slips, you can inadvertently twist your own knee or ankle, risking ligament tears.
    • Not maintaining a stable base, leading to you falling over while applying the lock. This can result in a knee injury for you due to awkward weight distribution and impact.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice securing the "S" grip on your own leg, focusing on the hip drive and armpit placement. 10 repetitions per side.
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a willing partner, assume the 50-50 position and practice the full sequence of the heel hook, focusing on body mechanics. 5 repetitions per side.
    3. Partner drill (light resistance - 25%): Partner gently resists hip movement. Focus on maintaining your grip and driving your hips. 5 repetitions per side.
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance - 50%): Partner actively tries to escape the grip. Focus on maintaining your "S" grip and figure four leg. 5 repetitions per side.
    5. Partner drill (high resistance - 75%): Partner attempts to escape and defend. Focus on finishing the lock with controlled hip extension. 3 repetitions per side.
    6. Live rolling (90%): Integrate the heel hook into sparring, looking for opportunities but prioritizing safety and tapping early if caught. 2-minute rounds, 50% of rolling time dedicated to attempts.

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you achieve a strong 50-50 guard position with control over your opponent's leg.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and you can invert to secure their leg.
    • When you are on your back and can isolate one of your opponent's legs, creating an opportunity to transition to a heel hook.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Turn your hip away: If your opponent is attacking your heel, immediately turn your hip away from the direction of the "S" grip. This relieves pressure on the knee joint.
    • Straighten your leg: Extend your leg as much as possible, making it difficult for your opponent to get a clean "S" grip on your heel. This also helps to create space.
    • Bridge and roll: If your opponent has a strong grip, bridge your hips and attempt to roll through to relieve pressure and create an escape. This requires good timing.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my heel hook feel weak and my opponent can easily escape?

    Your heel hook likely lacks the biomechanical foundation for effective torque. Ensure your "S" grip is tight, specifically targeting the heel bone, not just the Achilles tendon. Crucially, your hip action must be a controlled backward drive, not a simple push, to isolate and stress the knee joint ligaments.

    Q: How can I safely practice the heel hook against a bigger opponent without injuring them?

    Focus heavily on the drill progressions with extremely light resistance (0-25%). Prioritize control over speed or power. Always communicate with your partner about pressure and stop immediately if they signal discomfort. Ensure you have a solid 50-50 or inside heel hook position before even thinking about applying pressure.

    Q: Is it okay to 'crank' the heel hook if my opponent isn't tapping?

    Absolutely not. 'Cranking' implies uncontrolled force, which is a direct path to serious injury for your opponent and a violation of BJJ safety principles. A proper heel hook submission is achieved through precise biomechanical leverage, not brute force. If you're not getting a tap, re-evaluate your grip, body positioning, and hip drive.
