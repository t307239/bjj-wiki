Heel Hook Technique: A White Belt's Guide to BJJ Leg Locks

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The heel hook is a powerful leg lock targeting the knee and ankle joints, typically initiated from a 50-50 guard or a similar leg entanglement. It achieves a submission by applying rotational torque to the opponent's heel.

White belts often fail due to a lack of understanding of the precise mechanics, leading to ineffective application or dangerous self-injury. Fear of injuring their training partner can also cause hesitation.

The key mechanical insight is creating a stable base with your hips and using your body weight to isolate the opponent's heel, forcing a painful flexion or extension at the knee and ankle.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Achieve a 50-50 guard position where both you and your opponent have control of each other's lead leg, with both knees facing inward.
    2. Secure a "double-grip" on the opponent's heel: place your primary hand's thumb inside the opponent's heel cup and wrap your fingers around the outside. Your secondary hand grips the opponent's heel from the top, with your fingers pointing towards their toes.
    3. Position your hips so they are perpendicular to your opponent's hips, creating a stable base. Your chest should be tight against their thigh.
    4. Drive your hips back and slightly to the side of the opponent's trapped leg, creating tension and isolating the heel.
    5. Place your non-trapping leg's foot flat on the mat for base, or hook it around the opponent's hip to prevent them from turning out.
    6. Apply pressure by extending your hips away from your body and simultaneously pulling the heel towards your chest, creating a rotational force.
    7. Maintain head control by placing your head on the opponent's hip or thigh, preventing them from posturing up or escaping.
    8. Ensure your own knees are protected by keeping them tucked in and not allowing them to be twisted or hyperextended by the opponent's defense.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Applying the heel hook with excessive speed and uncontrolled hip extension: This can cause a sudden, forceful hyperextension of the opponent's knee, leading to ACL or MCL tears. The correct alternative is a slow, controlled application of pressure, listening for the tap.
    • Gripping the opponent's foot incorrectly, such as only grabbing the toes or the shin: This results in poor torque transfer and can lead to the opponent easily escaping. It also increases the risk of injuring your own hand or wrist. The correct alternative is the "double-grip" on the heel cup as described.
    • Allowing your own hips to collapse or become parallel to the opponent's: This creates an unstable base and allows the opponent to easily relieve pressure or escape. It also puts your own knee at risk of injury if the opponent counters. The correct alternative is maintaining a perpendicular hip angle with a strong base.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip acquisition and hip movement without a partner. Focus on the feel of the "double-grip" and the hip rotation. (10 reps per side)
    2. Static partner drill: With a compliant partner in the 50-50 guard, practice securing the grip and making minor hip adjustments. No pressure applied. (5 reps per side)
    3. Controlled pressure drill: Apply light, controlled pressure while your partner remains still. Focus on feeling the engagement of the heel. (25% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    4. Dynamic partner drill: Work on transitioning into the heel hook from the 50-50 guard with a partner who offers minimal resistance to the setup. (50% resistance, 3 reps per side)
    5. Flow rolling with leg lock focus: Engage in controlled rolling, specifically looking for opportunities to set up and attack the heel hook, with immediate verbal tap if the submission is secured or if there's discomfort. (75% resistance, 5 minutes)
    6. Live rolling with Heel Hook awareness: Attempt the heel hook with full intent, but be prepared to immediately defend or release if the opponent counters or if you feel any undue stress on your own joints. (90% resistance, 5 minutes)

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established a stable 50-50 guard, controlling both of your opponent's legs.
    • When your opponent attempts to stack you from the 50-50 guard, creating an opportunity to isolate their heel.
    • When your opponent tries to turn into you while you are in a leg entanglement, exposing their heel.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • "The Spin": When the heel hook is being applied, immediately spin your body away from the direction of the hook, keeping your hips mobile. This disperses the torque. Maintain a strong grip on your own leg to prevent further extension.
    • "The Knee Shield": If your opponent has a weak grip, attempt to place your free foot's shin across their hip and push away, creating space to relieve pressure. This is most effective if they haven't fully isolated your heel.
    • "The Straighten and Turn": While maintaining a defensive posture, attempt to straighten your leg and turn your hips away from the attacker. This can sometimes break the grip or alleviate pressure, but requires good timing and awareness to avoid further injury.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do a Heel Hook as a white belt?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are either applying the pressure incorrectly or your opponent is not tapping quickly enough, leading to excessive torque. Ensure you are using a proper "double-grip" on the heel and creating a stable hip base. Focus on controlled extension and rotation, and always prioritize your partner's safety by stopping immediately when they tap. If you feel pain, release the submission.

    Q: How can I safely learn the Heel Hook BJJ submission against a bigger opponent?

    When facing a bigger opponent, focus on superior body mechanics and grip fighting to achieve the 50-50 guard. Isolate their heel with precision and use your hips to generate torque, rather than relying on brute strength. Ensure your base is solid and your hips are driving back effectively to create leverage. Communicate with your partner about the pressure and ensure they understand how to tap early.

    Q: Is it okay for white belts to practice Heel Hooks in BJJ, or is it too dangerous?

    White belts can and should learn the fundamental mechanics of the heel hook, but it must be done under strict supervision and with controlled drilling. The danger lies in uncontrolled application and lack of awareness. Focus on the setup, the grip, and the initial engagement of pressure. Always drill with a compliant partner and prioritize safety over submission. Your instructor should guide your progression.
