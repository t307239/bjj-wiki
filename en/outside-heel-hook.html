Precise Outside Heel Hook Technique for BJJ White Belts

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Outside Heel Hook is a leg lock targeting the opponent's heel, aiming to hyperextend the knee and ankle to force a submission.

White belts often fail due to a lack of control over their own body and an over-reliance on brute strength rather than leverage.

The key mechanical insight is creating a fulcrum with your hips and using your arms to isolate and rotate the opponent's heel, not their leg.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard or similar position, establish a "figure-four" grip on the opponent's leg: your right arm's bicep presses against their hamstring, and your right hand grips your left bicep.
    2. Your left arm reaches under their knee and grabs your own right forearm, creating a tight "S" shape with your arms.
    3. Your hips must be angled towards the opponent's hips, creating a stable base and preventing them from turning into you.
    4. Your right knee should be positioned to block the opponent's hip, preventing them from creating space or bridging out.
    5. Your left leg hooks behind the opponent's ankle, preventing them from "kicking" their heel out.
    6. Initiate the submission by driving your hips forward and slightly upward, while simultaneously pulling your left forearm towards your chest.
    7. The rotation comes from your hips turning towards your left shoulder, creating torque on the opponent's heel and ankle joint.
    8. Maintain constant pressure on the hamstring with your right bicep; this is your primary control point.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying pressure by "kicking" your leg out: This can hyperextend your own knee joint, leading to ACL or MCL tears.

    The correct movement is to drive your hips forward and rotate your torso.

    • Gripping the opponent's shin or foot instead of the heel: This shifts the pressure away from the heel and onto the ankle, potentially causing ankle sprains and failing to secure the submission.

    Always aim to cup the heel with your hand and grip your bicep, isolating the heel.

    • Allowing the opponent to bridge or turn into the submission: This can lead to a "sprung" knee or ankle, where the joint is overextended and potentially torn.

    Maintain hip pressure and use your right knee as a block to prevent their bridge.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the "figure-four" grip and arm "S" shape with your own leg, focusing on isolating the heel and hip angle. (50 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a partner lying down, practice establishing the grip and hip angle, focusing on the precise arm and leg placement. (20 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light control): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to practice the hip drive and rotation without significant force. (15 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (controlled submission): Partner taps immediately upon feeling pressure, allowing you to practice the full movement with controlled force. (10 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Outside Heel Hook from a stable position in rolling, with explicit agreement to tap early and avoid dangerous movements. (5 attempts per round, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (full): Incorporate the Outside Heel Hook attempt into regular rolling, understanding the risks and tapping to defensive maneuvers. (Full rolling, 90-100% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard with good control of the opponent's leg.
    • When the opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg is exposed.
    • During transitions where you can isolate one of their legs.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Heel-out" escape: If the opponent has the figure-four grip, quickly turn your hips away from them and "kick" your heel out of their grasp, aiming to face them.
    • "Stacking": If the opponent is attempting the heel hook, drive your weight forward onto their hips and chest, collapsing their base and relieving pressure on the heel.
    • "The Flip" (advanced): If the opponent has your leg, attempt to invert your body and roll *towards* their attacking leg, using their momentum to escape the grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try the Outside Heel Hook?

    This usually happens when you are gripping the opponent's foot or ankle instead of their heel. The Outside Heel Hook targets the heel bone and the rotation of the ankle. If you are not isolating the heel properly with your grip and hip rotation, you will be putting pressure on the wrong joints, leading to ankle pain and an ineffective submission. Ensure your figure-four grip is tight and your hip drive is focused on rotating the heel.

    Q: How can I safely attempt an Outside Heel Hook against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage is paraMount. Focus on establishing a very tight "figure-four" grip to prevent them from using their size to escape. Your hip angle is critical; ensure your hips are angled towards their hips to prevent them from bridging effectively. Drive your hips forward and rotate your torso with controlled power, rather than trying to match their strength. A solid "S" grip with your arms will prevent them from posturing up and relieving pressure.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to an Outside Heel Hook from other leg entanglements?

    The optimal time to transition is when you have achieved a stable "50-50" position, meaning both you and your opponent have a similar grip on each other's leg. This provides the necessary control to isolate one leg for the heel hook. Also, if the opponent is actively trying to pass your guard and exposes their leg, this is a prime opportunity to secure the Outside Heel Hook before they can fully establish their pass.
