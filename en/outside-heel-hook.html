Outside Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Outside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the opponent's ankle and knee by applying rotational pressure. It's typically initiated from positions where you can isolate a single leg, like the 50-50 guard or when an opponent is on their back.

White belts often struggle because they lack the precise body control and understanding of joint mechanics required. This leads to ineffective attempts or dangerous over-application, risking injury to themselves or their partner.

The key mechanical insight is to create a stable base while simultaneously controlling the opponent's hip and applying a specific rotational force through the ankle joint, isolating the heel.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Secure a strong grip on the opponent's ankle with your arm closest to their head, thumb-up grip around the Achilles tendon area. Your forearm should be flush against the shin.
    2. Place your other arm's hand on the opponent's instep, fingers pointing towards their toes, creating a "figure-four" grip with your first arm. This secures the foot.
    3. Sit up and bring your knees towards your chest, creating a strong base. Your hips should be angled slightly towards the opponent's hips.
    4. Use your legs to create a "trap" around the opponent's leg. Your thigh on the same side as the trapped foot should be pressed against their hamstring.
    5. Drive your hips back and down, extending your legs to create tension on the opponent's leg. Simultaneously, arch your back slightly.
    6. Rotate your hips counter-clockwise (if attacking their left leg) to apply inward pressure on the heel. This is the primary rotational force.
    7. Maintain a strong, stable base with your non-attacked leg extended to prevent them from turning into you. Keep your weight centered.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect grip and hip drive: Applying pressure with a loose grip or by simply "pulling" with your arms without proper hip extension can lead to the opponent escaping or the submission failing.
    • Cruciate Ligament (ACL) tear risk: If the opponent's knee is not properly controlled and they twist their body away violently while you are applying pressure, it can cause a severe ACL tear in their knee.
    • Knee joint hyperextension: Attempting to "stomp" your foot down or excessively flexing your own ankle can put dangerous hyperextension pressure on your own knee or the opponent's knee if the grip is lost.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo grip practice: Practice the figure-four grip on a training dummy or your own leg. (50 reps per side)
    2. Solo positional drilling: Practice the sit-up and hip drive motion with perfect form, focusing on creating tension without a partner. (50 reps per side)
    3. Partnered positional drilling (0% resistance): Partner lies on their back, you secure the Outside Heel Hook position. Focus on precise grip and body alignment. Partner offers no resistance. (5 reps per side)
    4. Partnered positional drilling (25% resistance): Partner attempts to lightly shrimp or bridge. You focus on maintaining your structure and hip drive. (10 reps per side)
    5. Partnered drilling with escape attempts (50% resistance): Partner actively tries to escape by turning or bridging. You focus on adjusting your hips and maintaining the lock. (15 reps per side)
    6. Controlled rolling (75% resistance): Roll with a partner specifically focusing on setting up and finishing the Outside Heel Hook. Communicate to tap early. (3 x 2-minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When an opponent is on their back and you have secured a leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard.
    • When an opponent attempts a guard pass where their leg is extended and accessible, such as a failed toreando or leg drag.
    • When you are in a position to isolate an opponent's leg, like from half guard when they are posturing up and their leg is extended.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Straightening" Defense: The opponent immediately extends their leg and pushes their hips away, creating space and breaking the angle of attack. This requires the attacker to immediately shift their weight to prevent being swept.
    • The "Turn-In" Defense: The opponent turns their body into the attacker, relieving pressure on the heel hook and potentially creating an opportunity to escape or counter-attack.
    • The "Grip Break" Defense: The opponent uses their free hand to pry at the attacker's gripping hands, aiming to break the figure-four grip and free their leg.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do an Outside Heel Hook?

    This usually occurs because you are not properly isolating the heel and are instead applying pressure directly to the ankle joint itself. Ensure your figure-four grip is secure around the instep and Achilles, and your hip drive is creating rotational force on the heel bone, not just compressing the ankle.

    Q: How can I safely practice the Outside Heel Hook against a bigger opponent without injuring them?

    Always prioritize communication and tapping early. When drilling, start with 0% resistance, focusing solely on perfect body mechanics and grip placement. Gradually increase resistance, but never to the point where the opponent cannot safely tap. Focus on controlled hip extension rather than explosive power.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an Outside Heel Hook in a rolling situation?

    The optimal time is when the opponent's leg is isolated and their hips are somewhat anchored, preventing them from easily turning into you. This often happens when you've successfully transitioned to a 50-50 guard or when an opponent overcommits to a Guard Pass and leaves a leg vulnerable.
