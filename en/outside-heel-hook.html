Outside Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The outside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the knee and ankle, typically initiated from positions like the 50-50 guard or when isolating an opponent's leg.

White belts often struggle due to improper hip control, leading to inefficient mechanics and increased risk of injury to themselves or their partner.

The key is achieving a stable base and controlling the opponent's hip to prevent them from rotating out, allowing for a clean torque application on the heel.

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Entry Grip: From a control position (e.g., 50-50 guard), secure the opponent's foot with your armpit and the crook of your elbow. Your forearm should press against the Achilles tendon.
    2. Hip Alignment: Position your hips perpendicular to the opponent's hips, creating a stable base. Your chest should be against their thigh.
    3. Body Control: Use your free arm to grip the opponent's ankle or shin firmly, preventing them from pulling their leg free.
    4. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight forward, driving your hips towards the opponent's leg, compressing it.
    5. Knee Placement: Ensure your knee is positioned on the outside of the opponent's thigh, preventing their hip from turning.
    6. Heel Isolation: Drive your hips forward and slightly down, creating pressure that isolates the opponent's heel bone.
    7. Finishing Pressure: With a stable base and control, extend your hips away from the opponent's knee while maintaining your grip on the foot, applying torque to the heel.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Hip Rotation: Attempting to finish by twisting your own hips can strain your sacroiliac joint or lower back, as the torque is not directed at the opponent's heel.
    • Premature Hip Extension: Releasing hip pressure too early allows the opponent to escape, potentially exposing your own knee to a counter-attack or causing a hyperextension of your knee.
    • Slipping Grip: Losing the grip on the opponent's foot while applying pressure can result in your own leg being left vulnerable to a counter heel hook or knee bar.
    • Knee Collision: Allowing your knees to bang together can cause meniscus tears or ligament damage in your own knees.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Isolation Drill: Practice securing the opponent's foot and establishing the correct hip angle and body position without an opponent (50 reps each side).
    2. Partnered Entry & Control: With a compliant partner, practice entering the position and securing the grips without applying pressure (20 reps each side).
    3. Controlled Compression Drill: With a compliant partner, practice the hip compression and body control elements, stopping just before applying finishing pressure (30 reps each side).
    4. Slow Motion Submission Drill: Execute the entire submission at 25% speed and pressure, focusing on precise mechanics and communication (20 reps each side).
    5. Light Resistance Submission Drill: With a partner offering minimal resistance (50%), execute the submission, focusing on maintaining control and applying pressure gradually (15 reps each side).
    6. Live Rolling Application (Controlled): In a live roll, if the opportunity arises, attempt the outside heel hook with 75% commitment, prioritizing safety and tapping early if needed.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is on their back with their legs exposed, particularly in the 50-50 guard.
    • When you have successfully isolated one of your opponent's legs during transitions or scrambles.
    • When your opponent attempts to pass your guard and their leg is within reach and control.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Heel Hook Escape (Rotation): The opponent can attempt to rotate their hips away from the pressure by turning their body towards the attacking leg, creating space.
    • Leg Pull Out: The opponent can attempt to pull their heel out of the grip by flexing their ankle and driving their foot towards their own hips.
    • Switching to Inside Heel Hook: A skilled opponent might attempt to counter by transitioning to an inside heel hook, forcing you to react to their new angle.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do an outside heel hook?

    Your knee is likely hurting due to improper hip alignment and insufficient control of your opponent's hips. If you're not perpendicular to your opponent and driving your hips into their leg, you're not isolating the ankle and heel correctly. This can cause your own knee to twist or hyperextend as you try to force the submission, damaging your ACL or meniscus. Focus on creating a solid base and controlling their hip rotation before applying pressure.

    Q: How do I set up an outside heel hook against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on speed and precision in your entry and grip. Don't try to overpower them; instead, use their size against them by quickly isolating a leg and securing a tight grip on their foot, preventing them from posturing up or rotating. Your hip control is paraMount. Ensure your hips are completely perpendicular to theirs and drive your weight into their leg to disrupt their base before they can use their mass to defend.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt an outside heel hook in a BJJ match?

    The ideal time to attempt an outside heel hook is when you have established control over your opponent's leg, most commonly when you are in the 50-50 guard or have isolated a leg during a scramble. It's also a strong option when your opponent is on their back and their legs are accessible, or when they are attempting a Guard Pass and expose their leg. Avoid attempting it when you are in a compromised position, as this leaves you vulnerable to counters.
