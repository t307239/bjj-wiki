The Outside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the knee and ankle from leg entanglement positions. It aims to hyperextend the opponent's knee joint. White belts often rush this technique, leading to sloppy mechanics and injury. The key is isolating the opponent's heel and creating a perpendicular lever arm at the knee.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard or similar entanglement, secure an "Uncle Rico" grip: your forearm behind their heel cord, thumb pointing up, and your other hand gripping your own bicep.
    2. Create a perpendicular hip angle: your hips must be perpendicular to your opponent's leg line, not parallel.
    3. Drive your hips forward: Simultaneously, drive your hips towards their heel, creating inward pressure.
    4. Control their knee: Use your opposite leg's shin across their thigh to prevent them from rotating out.
    5. Isolate the heel: Ensure your forearm is firmly pressing into the opponent's heel cord, not the Achilles tendon.
    6. Apply outward pressure: With your "Uncle Rico" grip, create outward pressure on their heel, pulling it away from their body.
    7. Extend your hips and back: A slight extension of your hips and upper back will complete the hyperextension of the knee.
    8. Maintain hip perpendicularity: Continue to keep your hips perpendicular to their leg throughout the entire submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Rushing the finish by "pulling hard" on the heel: This can tear the lateral collateral ligament (LCL) or anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) of the opponent's knee by forcing a valgus collapse. Instead, focus on the hip-drive and perpendicular angle.
    • Gripping the Achilles tendon instead of the heel cord: This leads to a less effective submission and can cause ankle sprains or Achilles tendonitis. Grip directly on the bony prominence of the heel.
    • Allowing the opponent's knee to track over their toes: This creates a dangerous situation where the knee can buckle inwards, risking ACL or meniscus tears. Maintain control of their knee with your shin.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo visualization: Mentally rehearse the steps, focusing on hip angles and grip placement (10 reps).
    2. Partnered positional drilling (0% resistance): Practice the setup and initial grip from a static 50-50 position, focusing on body mechanics (10 reps per side).
    3. Partnered submission drilling (25% resistance): Apply the "Uncle Rico" grip and hip drive, with the partner offering minimal resistance to the finish (10 reps per side).
    4. Partnered submission drilling (50% resistance): Execute the full Outside Heel Hook with the partner offering light pressure to escape or defend the grip (10 reps per side).
    5. Partnered submission drilling (75% resistance): Apply the Outside Heel Hook with the partner actively attempting to defend or escape, focusing on maintaining control and hip angle (5 reps per side).
    6. Live rolling (90% resistance): Attempt the Outside Heel Hook in controlled rolling scenarios, prioritizing technique over speed and focusing on safe finishes (3 minutes).
    7. Live rolling (100% resistance): Integrate the Outside Heel Hook into regular rolling, always tapping early if caught and respecting your partner's safety (5 minutes).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard and can control your opponent's leg.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and exposes their heel to your control.
    • When you have successfully swept or reversed your opponent and can isolate a leg.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Tuck and Roll" defense: If the opponent has your heel, immediately tuck your chin and roll your body in the direction of your free leg to relieve pressure and potentially escape the grip. This requires quick reaction and body awareness.
    • "Stacking" defense: If the opponent has your heel, drive your hips into them and attempt to "stack" their upper body onto their legs, collapsing the structure and relieving pressure. This is best applied early in the submission attempt.
    • "Bridging and Rotating" defense: If the opponent attempts to extend their hips, bridge your hips upwards and simultaneously rotate your body away from the heel hook. This can break their structure and allow you to untangle your leg.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do the Outside Heel Hook?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not maintaining a perpendicular hip angle to your opponent's leg. This causes your knee to twist internally or externally instead of being hyperextended. Ensure your hips are directly beside your opponent's hip, creating a 90-degree angle to their attacking leg, and drive your hips forward to create the correct lever arm.

    Q: How can I use the Outside Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, the key is precision and leverage, not strength. Focus on securing a very tight "Uncle Rico" grip on the heel cord and driving your hips forward with maximum perpendicularity. Use your free leg to control their knee joint, preventing them from rotating out. Your smaller frame will be an advantage if you can create a tight, efficient lever arm.

    Q: When is the best time to go for an Outside Heel Hook as a white belt in BJJ?

    As a white belt, the best time to attempt an Outside Heel Hook is when you have already achieved a dominant leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard, and your opponent is not actively defending the position. Avoid attempting it from compromised positions where you might expose your own leg or be easily countered. Prioritize securing control of the leg first.
