Your First Outside Heel Hook: A White Belt's Guide to BJJ

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Outside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the opponent's ankle and knee, aiming to hyperextend the knee or twist the ankle. It's most commonly attacked from a 50-50 guard or a similar leg entanglement position.

White belts often rush this technique, leading to injury or failed attempts because they lack the foundational control and understanding of joint mechanics. The focus is often on 'pulling' rather than precise leverage.

The key mechanical insight is to create a strong fulcrum with your hips and use your legs to isolate and then torque the opponent's heel bone, forcing the heel to rotate against the ankle and knee joints.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard, secure your opponent's left leg with your legs. Your right leg goes over their thigh, and your left leg goes under their thigh, creating a 'figure-four' lock around their leg.
    2. Grip their left heel with your right hand, thumb pointing upwards. Your palm should cup the back of their heel bone.
    3. With your left hand, grip your own right shin. This stabilizes your leg structure and prevents your opponent from escaping.
    4. Rotate your hips to your right, away from your opponent's hips. This creates a slight separation and angles your body.
    5. Drive your left knee towards your opponent's buttock. This prevents them from 'stacking' you and adds pressure.
    6. Arch your back and lift your hips upwards. This elevates your opponent's leg, making it more accessible for the lock.
    7. Simultaneously, pull your right heel towards your chest while pushing your hips forward. This action isolates the heel and applies rotational pressure.
    8. Maintain a strong grip on the heel and continue the hip drive and chest pull to complete the submission.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly gripping the opponent's foot: Grabbing the toes or midfoot instead of the heel bone. This can lead to a loss of control and ineffective torque, potentially causing the opponent to escape or apply a counter.
    • Failing to control the opponent's hip: Allowing the opponent to 'stack' their weight by driving their hips into yours. This pressure can force your spine into flexion, risking a disc injury, and also allows them to relieve pressure on their leg.
    • Applying torque without proper leg isolation: Trying to twist the leg without first establishing a firm 'figure-four' leg lock and controlling their hip. This lack of control can lead to the opponent's knee buckling in an uncontrolled way, risking ACL or MCL tears for both parties.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Leg Isolation Drill: Practice establishing the 'figure-four' leg lock around a training dummy's leg for 1 minute. 0% resistance.
    2. Grip and Hip Angle Drill: From the 'figure-four', practice securing the heel grip and rotating your hips away for 30 seconds. 0% resistance.
    3. Controlled Submission Entry (No Resistance): From 50-50 guard, have a partner lie passively. Practice the full sequence of entering the Outside Heel Hook for 5 repetitions. 0% resistance.
    4. Light Resistance Entry: Practice entering the Outside Heel Hook from 50-50 guard with a partner offering minimal resistance (they don't actively defend the leg). Focus on hip movement and grip. 25% resistance.
    5. Controlled Submission Practice: Enter the Outside Heel Hook from 50-50 guard. Your partner offers light defense but allows you to complete the mechanics. Tap when you feel the submission applied. 50% resistance.
    6. Live Rolling (Controlled): In rolling, look for opportunities to enter the Outside Heel Hook. If successful, apply with controlled pressure and tap immediately if your partner taps. If your partner defends effectively, disengage and reset. 75% resistance.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you achieve a strong 50-50 guard with clear control of your opponent's leg.
    • When your opponent attempts to 'stack' you, creating an opportunity to trap their leg.
    • When your opponent tries to 'turn in' or 'spin out', exposing their heel.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The 'Stack' Defense: If the opponent attempts the heel hook, drive your weight forward and 'stack' them by pressing your hips into their torso. This relieves pressure on your knee and ankle.
    • The 'Heel Pop' Defense: As the opponent grips your heel, flex your foot aggressively and try to 'pop' your heel out of their grip by rotating your ankle and knee inwards.
    • The 'Spin Out' Defense: If you feel the heel hook being applied, immediately attempt to spin your body towards the direction of the torque. This helps to unwind the joint and can break the opponent's grip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do an Outside Heel Hook?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are not properly isolating your opponent's leg with your own legs before applying torque. Ensure your 'figure-four' leg lock is tight around their thigh and that your hips are driving forward to create leverage. Avoid any twisting motion of your knee while entering the submission; the torque should come from rotating your opponent's heel.

    Q: How can I apply the Outside Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on achieving a superior positional advantage before attacking the leg. Use your hips and core to create space and prevent them from using their weight to crush you. Once in the 50-50 guard, prioritize controlling their hip to prevent stacking, then use your entire body's leverage by driving your hips forward and arching your back to isolate and torque their heel.

    Q: When is the best time to transition to an Outside Heel Hook from closed guard?

    Direct transitions from Closed Guard to an Outside Heel Hook are rare and often risky for white belts. It's usually more effective to first transition to a more advantageous position like open guard or half guard, and then work towards a leg entanglement like the 50-50 guard. Attempting it directly from closed guard often involves opening your guard prematurely and exposing yourself to sweeps or passes.
