Outside Heel Hook BJJ: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Outside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the knee and ankle by applying rotational force. It is typically initiated from positions where the opponent's leg is isolated, such as the 50-50 guard or a leg entanglement.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of proprioception and a tendency to 'muscle' the submission, leading to injury or ineffective attempts. Understanding the precise mechanics is crucial for safety and success.

The key insight is to use your body's levers to create torque on the opponent's foot and ankle, rather than brute strength, stressing the knee joint through rotation.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard or similar leg entanglement, establish a 'figure-four' grip on the opponent's heel with your arm: your forearm hooks behind their Achilles tendon, and your bicep presses against their instep.
    2. Secure a secondary grip on their foot with your other hand, gripping the top of their foot near the toes, ensuring your thumb is pointed upwards.
    3. Position your hips so they are perpendicular to the opponent's leg, creating a strong base and preventing them from easily rotating away.
    4. Drive your hips forward into their heel, creating a fulcrum point against your own body.
    5. Extend your legs away from your body while simultaneously pulling your secondary grip (on the toes) towards your chest.
    6. Your gripping arm (forearm on Achilles) acts as a pivot, while your pulling arm (on the foot) drives the rotational torque.
    7. Apply pressure by extending your hips and pulling the foot, focusing on rotating the opponent's heel outwards relative to their knee.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly attempting to 'crank' the heel hook by pulling with your arms alone without hip extension. This can cause a hyperextension or sprain of the opponent's knee ligaments (ACL, LCL) due to insufficient rotational control.
    • Gripping too low on the foot, near the ankle bones, instead of the instep. This allows the opponent's heel to slip and can lead to a dangerous twisting of your own ankle if they counter.
    • Allowing your hips to become parallel with the opponent's leg, losing the perpendicular angle. This reduces your leverage and can result in your own knee being vulnerable to a counter-heel hook.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip sequence and hip positioning on a training dummy or a stable object for 10 reps per side, 0% resistance.
    2. Partnered drilling (no resistance): With a willing partner, isolate a leg and practice the exact grip and body mechanics without any pressure for 10 reps per side, 25% resistance.
    3. Static drilling: From a controlled 50-50 position, have your partner offer no resistance to the heel hook attempt. Focus on the hip drive and pulling motion for 10 reps per side, 50% resistance.
    4. Controlled submission drilling: From the 50-50, your partner allows you to apply the heel hook until they tap. Focus on a controlled, smooth application, not a sudden crank, for 5 reps per side, 75% resistance.
    5. Flow drilling: Integrate the Outside Heel Hook into a sequence of movements from the 50-50, where your partner can defend and you can attempt. Focus on smooth transitions and recognizing opportunities for 5 reps per side, 90% resistance.
    6. Live rolling (situational): During rolling, if you find yourself in a 50-50 or similar entanglement, focus on transitioning to the Outside Heel Hook. Tap early and often if you are caught. 100% resistance (with absolute control and communication).

    When to Use & Counters

    WHEN TO ATTEMPT:

    • When you have established a dominant 50-50 guard with control of the opponent's heel.
    • When your opponent attempts to escape by turning their hips away, creating an opportunity for the outside heel hook.
    • During transitions where you can isolate and secure an opponent's leg after a sweep or failed guard pass.

    PRIMARY COUNTERS:

    • Turtle: If the opponent is turtling, the outside heel hook is generally not a primary option. Focus on other submissions or positional advancement.
    • Straightening the leg: If the opponent significantly straightens their leg and creates distance, the torque is lost. You may need to transition to a different submission or re-establish position.
    • Sweeping the leg: If the opponent attempts to sweep your leg out from under you, be prepared to disengage or transition to a defensive position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my Outside Heel Hook feel weak and my opponent can easily escape?

    Your base and hip angle are likely incorrect. Ensure your hips are perpendicular to your opponent's leg, creating a strong fulcrum. Instead of pulling with your arms, drive your hips forward into their heel and extend your legs away. This utilizes your body's leverage system, not just arm strength, to apply the necessary torque.

    Q: How can I effectively use the Outside Heel Hook against a much larger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, precision is paraMount. Focus on securing the 'figure-four' grip on their heel and a strong secondary grip on their foot. Use your body weight to drive your hips into their heel, creating a solid anchor. Then, extend your legs to create distance and pull their foot towards you to generate the torque, rather than trying to overpower them.

    Q: Is it okay to 'crank' the Outside Heel Hook to make it faster?

    Absolutely not. 'Cranking' is a dangerous and imprecise method that bypasses the biomechanical principles of the submission. It significantly increases the risk of severe knee ligament damage for your opponent and can lead to injury for yourself if they counter suddenly. Always focus on a controlled application of rotational force.
