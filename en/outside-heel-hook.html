Outside Heel Hook: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide to BJJ

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Outside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the opponent's ankle and knee joints. It is typically attacked from positions where you can isolate one of their legs, such as the 50-50 guard or when they attempt a Guard Pass.

White belts often get injured attempting Heel Hooks due to uncontrolled movements and a lack of understanding of joint stress. They might try to 'muscle' the submission or apply pressure without proper structure.

The key mechanical insight is using your body weight and hip rotation to create leverage, forcing the opponent's heel to rotate inwards, stressing the knee and ankle ligaments without relying on brute strength.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a dominant leg entanglement (e.g., 50-50 guard), secure your opponent's heel with your armpit and grasp their ankle bone (malleolus) with your hand, fingers pointing towards their toes.
    2. Place your other hand on the opponent's knee, fingers pointing towards their toes, to control their leg extension.
    3. Position your hips perpendicular to your opponent's hips, creating a 'T' shape with your bodies.
    4. Drive your hips forward and slightly to the side of your opponent's attacked leg, maintaining a tight connection.
    5. Lift your hips off the mat, creating a fulcrum with your own hips and the opponent's knee.
    6. Rotate your hips inwards (towards your opponent's head), simultaneously pulling their heel towards your body.
    7. Apply a controlled inward rotation of the opponent's heel by squeezing your armpit and driving your hips. This stresses the fibular collateral ligament (LCL) and anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL).

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly applying pressure by 'stomping' your hips forward: This can cause a hyperextension injury in the opponent's knee, potentially tearing the ACL or PCL.
    • Gripping the opponent's foot too loosely and allowing their heel to escape: This can lead to a twisting injury of the ankle, damaging the ATFL or deltoid ligament.
    • Allowing the opponent to straighten their leg while you are applying pressure: This can result in a severe knee hyperextension injury, damaging the ACL and PCL.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice the hip rotation and body positioning without a partner. Focus on achieving the perpendicular hip alignment and the 'T' shape. (20 reps)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice securing the heel and performing the hip drive and rotation. Partner offers no resistance. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner offers slight resistance to leg extension. Focus on maintaining your frame and driving through the movement. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance)
    4. Partner drill (moderate resistance): Partner attempts to defend by posturing up or pulling their leg. Focus on maintaining control and completing the rotation. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance)
    5. Partner drill (heavy resistance): Partner actively defends. Focus on the precise mechanics of the hip drive and heel rotation. (3 reps per side, 75% resistance)
    6. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Outside Heel Hook in a controlled sparring session, prioritizing safety and tapping early if caught. (5 minutes, 90% resistance)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard and have isolated one of your opponent's legs.
    • When your opponent attempts a guard pass and their leg is extended within your reach, allowing for a quick entanglement.
    • During scrambles where you can secure a leg entanglement and transition to attacking the heel.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Hip Escape and Leg Unwinding: If your opponent attempts an Outside Heel Hook, immediately hip escape away from the direction of the heel rotation and simultaneously try to unwind your leg, creating space.
    • Posture Up and Pull Leg Free: If you feel pressure, immediately posture up, creating slack in your leg, and pull your leg towards your hips to break the entanglement.
    • Tap Early: The most crucial counter is to recognize the submission is locked in and tap immediately to avoid serious injury.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do the Outside Heel Hook?

    Ankle pain during the Outside Heel Hook often stems from you applying the submission incorrectly. You might be trying to twist your opponent's ankle directly with your hands, which is biomechanically inefficient and strains your own ankle. Instead, focus on using your hips to drive their heel into your armpit and then rotating your hips to create the leverage, making their ankle do the work.

    Q: How do I perform the Outside Heel Hook against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, brute strength is your enemy. Focus on superior body positioning and timing. Secure their leg with a tight entanglement, ensuring your hips are perpendicular to theirs. Your goal is to use your hip rotation to create the torque, not to overpower them. Prioritize isolating their heel and using your entire body weight to drive the submission, rather than just your arms.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Outside Heel Hook in BJJ, and how do I avoid injuring my training partners?

    The Outside Heel Hook is best attempted when you have secured a dominant leg entanglement, like the 50-50 guard, or when your opponent exposes a leg during a pass. To avoid injury, always drill with compliant partners, communicate clearly, and tap early. Never 'muscle' the submission; focus on precise biomechanical execution. Ensure your partner understands the importance of tapping immediately to prevent joint damage.
