Precise Outside Heel Hook Technique for BJJ White Belts

BROWN★★★★★Expert
Brown Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → Brown Belt Requirements

The Outside Heel Hook is a submission targeting the opponent's heel, initiated from positions like the 50-50 guard or a specific leg entanglement. It achieves a submission by hyperextending the knee and ankle joints.

White belts often struggle due to a lack of control over their own body's leverage and a tendency to use brute strength rather than precise mechanics. This can lead to ineffective attempts or injury.

The ONE key mechanical insight is isolating the opponent's heel and applying rotational pressure to their ankle, while maintaining a stable base to prevent counter-attacks.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a 50-50 guard position, ensure your opponent's heel is trapped between your thighs. Your primary control grip is the 'Heel Hook Grip': cup their heel with your palm, fingers pointing towards their toes.
    2. Your secondary grip is the 'Ankle Grip': secure their ankle with your forearm on the outside of their ankle bone, hand cupping the front of their shin.
    3. Align your hips parallel to the opponent's hips, creating a stable base. Your chest should be tight against their thigh.
    4. Drive your hips forward into the opponent's hip line. This creates a fulcrum for rotational pressure.
    5. Begin to extend your hips and simultaneously rotate your torso away from the opponent's trapped leg. This action pulls the heel out of alignment.
    6. Apply upward pressure with your 'Heel Hook Grip' and a squeezing pressure with your thighs around their calf. This isolates the ankle.
    7. Continue the hip drive and torso rotation, focusing on creating a twisting motion on the opponent's foot and ankle, while keeping your own knees protected by staying tight to their leg.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to 'pull hard' with your arms to finish the submission: This can lead to a hyperextension of your own elbow or shoulder, damaging the ligaments in those joints. The correct movement is to drive your hips and rotate your torso, using your arms for control, not brute force.
    • Allowing the opponent to bridge or roll effectively while you are attempting the heel hook: This can cause a rotational force on your own knee joint, leading to an ACL or MCL tear. Maintain a wide, stable base and keep your hips connected to their hip line.
    • Not isolating the heel properly, causing your own shin to press into the opponent's knee: This can result in a knee-on-knee impact, potentially damaging both your knee and theirs. Ensure your thigh is tight against their calf and your hip is driving into their hip.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice the grip acquisition and hip alignment with no partner. Focus on the feel of the grip and the base. (10 reps each side, 0% resistance).
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): With a compliant partner, practice the entire sequence of grip, hip drive, and rotational movement. Partner offers no resistance. (5 reps each side, 25% resistance).
    3. Controlled entry: Practice entering the 50-50 guard and securing the heel hook grips. Partner offers light resistance to entry. (5 reps each side, 50% resistance).
    4. Submission focus: Practice the full submission sequence, with the partner tapping immediately upon feeling the pressure. Focus on the correct hip drive and rotation. (10 reps each side, 75% resistance).
    5. Defensive awareness: Practice the submission while the partner attempts to defend by bridging or turning. Focus on maintaining your base and control. (5 reps each side, 90% resistance).
    6. Live rolling (controlled): Attempt the Outside Heel Hook in a controlled sparring session. Focus on timing and positional awareness, tapping early if you feel yourself in a bad position. (3-5 minute rounds, 100% resistance, with explicit agreement on leg lock safety).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When you have successfully swept an opponent and landed in a position where their legs are exposed, such as a broken open guard.
    • When you have established a strong 50-50 guard, controlling both of their legs.
    • During transitions from a failed guard pass where the opponent attempts to scramble and exposes their heel.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The 'Escape the Hip': If the opponent attempts the heel hook, immediately drive your hips away from their rotational force and attempt to shrimp your hips out, breaking the alignment. This releases the pressure on the ankle.
    • The 'Body Lock Defense': If the opponent secures the heel hook grip, aggressively establish a body lock on their torso and drive your weight forward, collapsing their structure and preventing the hip extension. This disrupts their base.
    • The 'Counter Sweep/Roll': If the opponent is slow to secure the heel hook or has a weak grip, use their rotational momentum against them by performing a quick sweep or rolling motion to escape the entanglement and regain a neutral or advantageous position.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try the Outside Heel Hook?

    Your ankle is likely hurting because you are not isolating the opponent's heel correctly, or you are allowing their foot to twist in a way that puts undue stress on your own ankle. Ensure your grip is firmly on the heel and your thighs are tightly squeezing their calf to prevent any unwanted rotation. Focus on driving your hips to create the submission pressure, not twisting your own ankle.

    Q: How do I Outside Heel Hook against a bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes paraMount. Do not try to muscle them. Focus on achieving a very tight 'Heel Hook Grip' and 'Ankle Grip' to prevent them from creating space. Drive your hips powerfully and use your entire body weight to create the rotational pressure. Ensure your base is wide and stable to avoid being easily postured up or swept.

    Q: When is the best time to go for an Outside Heel Hook in a BJJ match?

    The Outside Heel Hook is best attempted when you have established a dominant leg entanglement, most commonly the 50-50 guard. It's also effective during transitions, such as when an opponent is trying to recover guard or scramble. Avoid attempting it when you are in a disadvantageous position, as it can leave you vulnerable to sweeps or strikes if executed poorly.
