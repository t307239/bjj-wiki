Double Under Pass: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Double Under Pass is initiated from a standing or kneeling position when the opponent has established a Closed Guard. Its goal is to bypass the opponent's legs and establish side control or mount.

White belts often struggle because they rely on brute force rather than proper weight distribution and hip pressure, leading to inefficient movement and potential injury.

The key mechanical insight is creating a "frame" with your arms to control the opponent's hips and then driving your shoulders forward, forcing your legs under their hips to break the guard.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grip: Establish "collar and sleeve" grips (or two collar grips) on the opponent's gi, pulling them slightly forward. Your knees should be shoulder-width apart, maintaining a stable base.
    2. Frame Creation: Lower your hips towards the mat, creating a strong base. Drive your forearms into the opponent's hips, pushing them away slightly to create space.
    3. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight onto your lead foot, angling your hips to your opponent's left (if passing to your right). Your chest should be angled towards the mat.
    4. Leg Insertion (Right Leg): Drive your right knee forward and under the opponent's left hip. Your right shin should be perpendicular to the mat, creating a wedge.
    5. Leg Insertion (Left Leg): Simultaneously, drive your left knee forward and under the opponent's right hip. Your left shin should also be perpendicular to the mat.
    6. Shoulder Drive: With both knees inserted, drive your shoulders forward, pressing your chest into the opponent's hips and pushing them down.
    7. Pass and Secure: Continue driving forward, allowing the opponent's legs to fall to the side. Maintain chest-to-hip connection and secure the opponent's hips with your arms as you move to side control.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Extension Injury: Incorrectly driving knees forward without hip angling can cause hyperextension of the knee joint, leading to ACL or PCL tears. Always maintain a slight bend in the knee and drive with the hips.
    • Ankle Sprain: Attempting to "step over" the opponent's legs instead of driving the shins underneath can lead to the ankle rolling outwards, causing ligament damage. Focus on insertion directly beneath the hips.
    • Lower Back Strain: Slouching or rounding the back during the drive, instead of maintaining a neutral spine and driving with the hips and chest, puts excessive shear force on the lumbar spine, risking disc injury. Keep your core engaged and back flat.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Mobility Drill: Practice driving your hips forward and backward with knees bent, simulating the hip movement for the pass. 50 repetitions.
    2. Solo Frame and Drive: From a kneeling position, practice creating the frame with your arms and driving your shoulders forward, aiming to push imaginary hips. 50 repetitions.
    3. Partnered Static Pass: With a compliant partner, from their closed guard, practice the grip, frame, and leg insertion without any resistance. 20 repetitions each side.
    4. Partnered Light Resistance Pass: Partner offers minimal resistance by lightly holding your arms. Focus on driving through their hips. 20 repetitions each side (50% resistance).
    5. Controlled Pass: Partner actively tries to maintain guard but allows the pass to be completed. Focus on maintaining pressure and connection. 20 repetitions each side (75% resistance).
    6. Live Rolling Application: Attempt the Double Under Pass during rolling, focusing on the mechanics learned. Do not force the pass if it's not working. 5 minutes (100% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When the opponent has a tight closed guard and is posturing up.
    • When the opponent's hips are slightly elevated, creating a small gap.
    • When you have established good initial grips and are controlling the opponent's posture.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Retention: The opponent can actively pull your hips back in by tightening their legs and bridging, preventing leg insertion. Maintain a strong base and drive forward with your hips.
    • Shrimping/Hip Escape: If the passer's weight is too far forward, the opponent can shrimp their hips out, creating space to re-guard. The passer must maintain constant forward pressure and keep their chest on the opponent's hips.
    • Leg Lock Defense: If the passer exposes their legs during the pass, the opponent can attempt leg locks. The passer must keep their legs tight and connected to the opponent's hips, avoiding overextension.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Double Under Pass?

    Knee pain often occurs when you force your knee directly into the opponent's hip without proper hip angling. This can cause hyperextension or direct pressure on the joint. Ensure you are driving your hips forward and under their hips, keeping a slight bend in your knee, and using your shins as wedges, not directly impacting the knee joint.

    Q: How do I perform the Double Under Pass against a much bigger and stronger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on using their weight against them by creating a strong frame and driving forward with your shoulders, not just your arms. Angle your hips significantly to the side, making it harder for them to use their mass directly. Prioritize breaking their posture first to disrupt their base before attempting the leg insertion.

    Q: When should I switch from a collar-sleeve grip to a double collar grip for the Double Under Pass?

    The collar-sleeve grip is generally preferred initially as it offers better control over posture and allows for easier frame creation. Switch to a double collar grip if the opponent consistently prevents the sleeve grip or if you are finding it difficult to break their posture with the sleeve grip alone. The double collar grip can provide more leverage to pull them forward.
