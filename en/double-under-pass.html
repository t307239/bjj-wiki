This pass starts from a standing or kneeling position against an opponent's Closed Guard, aiming to break their leg control and establish side control.

Many white belts struggle because they focus on brute force, neglecting the crucial hip pressure and head-body connection.

The key insight is using your opponent's legs as a fulcrum for your hips, allowing you to drive through them with leverage.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Opponent in closed guard: Secure a "Collar and Sleeve" grip on one side, and a "Pants Grip" on the same side's ankle.
    2. Lower your base: Sink your hips by bending your knees to approximately 90 degrees, keeping your back straight.
    3. Drive your head forward: Wedge your forehead or the side of your head into your opponent's hip on the side of the pants grip.
    4. Hip engagement: Drive your hips forward and slightly down, creating a wedge between your opponent's legs.
    5. Underhook the far leg: Simultaneously, reach your free arm (the one not gripping collar/sleeve) under your opponent's far leg.
    6. Weight transfer: Shift your weight onto your knees and hips, driving your body forward and through their legs.
    7. Establish side control: Once their legs are bypassed, secure a "Seatbelt Grip" or "High Side Control" grip and drive your chest into their torso.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly driving forward with your upper body only: This strains your cervical spine and can lead to neck injury (e.g., whiplash) if the opponent shrimps or counters. The correct movement involves driving with your hips and a stable base.
    • Grabbing the opponent's shin instead of their ankle or pant leg: This provides less leverage and can cause wrist hyperextension or finger sprains as you try to secure a weak grip. Always aim for the pants or the ankle bone.
    • Letting your hips rise too high: This creates a gap for the opponent to re-guard or attack your legs, and can lead to knee hyperextension if you fall backward. Maintain a low, stable base throughout the pass.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling (0%): Practice the hip drive and head placement motion without an opponent, focusing on feeling the wedge. (20 reps)
    2. Partner drill - no resistance (25%): With a compliant partner, practice the full sequence of grips, hip drive, and underhook, focusing on the feel of their legs being bypassed. (10 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill - light resistance (50%): Partner offers minimal resistance, allowing you to feel the pressure needed to break their guard. Focus on maintaining your base. (10 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill - moderate resistance (75%): Partner actively tries to maintain guard, requiring you to adjust your hip pressure and head placement. (5 reps per side)
    5. Live rolling - controlled (90%): Attempt the pass during live rolling, but with the understanding that you will reset if the pass is not completed cleanly. Focus on technique over outcome. (3 attempts)
    6. Live rolling - full intensity (100%): Execute the Double Under Pass as you would in a match, aiming for completion. (Continue until end of round or pass completed)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent's knees are tightly locked, preventing an easy knee-slice or torreando.
    • Opponent is attempting to shrimp away, creating a slight gap.
    • You have established a dominant posture, forcing them to defend.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • "Shrimping": The opponent explosively pushes their hips away, creating space to re-establish guard or escape. The counter is to maintain hip pressure and follow their movement, preventing them from creating a significant gap.
    • "Technical Stand-up": The opponent attempts to stand up by bringing their knees to their chest and pushing off the mat. The counter is to immediately drop your weight and secure a dominant position like side control or knee-on-belly.
    • "Leg Re-guard": The opponent uses their free leg to "hook" around your hip or arm, attempting to re-establish their guard. The counter is to keep your head pressure applied and your chest tight to their body, preventing the leg from passing.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my neck hurt when I try the Double Under Pass?

    Your neck likely hurts because you are leading the pass with your head and upper body, rather than driving with your hips. This puts excessive strain on your cervical spine. To fix this, focus on sinking your hips low and using them to drive forward. Your head should act as a wedge, not the primary force, ensuring a stable base and protecting your neck.

    Q: How can I do the Double Under Pass against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage and precision are paraMount. Instead of trying to power through, focus on their hip structure. Secure a strong pants grip on one leg and use your head as a precise wedge into their hip joint. Drive your hips forward and under their center of mass, using their own weight against them. Your underhook on the far leg becomes crucial for control.

    Q: When is the best time to transition from a failed Double Under Pass attempt?

    If you feel your opponent successfully re-guarding or escaping, the best time to transition is immediately. Do not commit to a stalled pass. Instead, use the momentum of your failed attempt to either secure a dominant position like Side Control if you've bypassed one leg, or to transition to a different pass like a leg drag if they are pulling their leg back. Always maintain connection to their body.
