The Double Under Pass in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

This pass is initiated from your opponent's Closed Guard or open guard, aiming to secure side control. White belts often struggle by trying to force it without understanding hip pressure.

The key insight is using your hips to drive *through* their legs, not over them, creating a lever against their base.

It's a powerful pass when executed with proper weight distribution and hip angle, securing a dominant control position.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Grips: Establish collar grips (either both lapels or one lapel and the opposite sleeve) with your hands. Your thumbs should be inside the collar.
    2. Initial Framing: Place your hands on your opponent's hips or biceps, creating a frame to prevent them from closing their guard further or shrimping away.
    3. Hip Angle: Lower your hips and angle them towards your opponent's left side (if passing to your right). Your chest should be close to their chest.
    4. Leg Drive: Drive your left knee (if passing to your right) forward and across their body, aiming to get it past their hip bone.
    5. Weight Transfer: Shift your weight forward, driving your chest into their hips while simultaneously pulling their upper body slightly towards you with your collar grips.
    6. The "Under": As your left knee clears their hip, begin to swing your right leg *under* their body, aiming to get your right knee to the mat on the other side of their hip.
    7. Securing the Pass: Once your right knee is on the mat and your left leg is across their body, drive your hips down and forward to establish side control. Your chest should be heavy on their torso, and your arms should secure a cross-face or underhook grip.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Knee Injury: Attempting to drive your knee *over* an opponent's hip instead of swinging it under can cause your knee to buckle outwards. This can sprain or tear the MCL or LCL. The correct movement is a low, driving motion with the knee swinging *under* their hip line.
    • Lower Back Strain: Trying to lift your opponent's hips with your arms to create space will put excessive strain on your lower back. This can lead to muscle pulls or disc issues. The correct movement is to use your hips and chest pressure to drive *through* their guard.
    • Shoulder Impingement: Reaching too far over your opponent's head to grab their collar without proper body positioning can lead to shoulder impingement. Your elbow should be tucked, and your body should be angled in. The correct movement is to maintain a tight connection with your chest and drive your hips forward.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Hip Swivel & Knee Drive (0% Resistance): From a kneeling position, practice swiveling your hips and driving one knee forward and across an imaginary opponent's hip, then swinging the other knee under. 10 reps per side.
    2. Solo Hip Swivel & Knee Drive with Frame (0% Resistance): Add hand placement on an imaginary opponent's hips. Practice the hip swivel, knee drive, and leg swing, maintaining the frame. 10 reps per side.
    3. Partner Drill - Static Pass (25% Resistance): Your partner lies on their back with legs extended. You practice the grip acquisition, hip angle, knee drive, and leg swing to get to side control. Partner offers minimal resistance. 5 reps per side.
    4. Partner Drill - Controlled Guard Open (50% Resistance): Your partner opens their guard slightly. You practice the double under pass, focusing on maintaining connection and hip pressure as they try to re-guard. 5 reps per side.
    5. Partner Drill - Dynamic Guard (75% Resistance): Your partner actively tries to defend and re-guard. You focus on the timing of your drive and leg swing, reacting to their movements. 5 reps per side.
    6. Live Rolling (90-100% Resistance): Attempt the double under pass during sparring, focusing on correct mechanics and weight distribution against a resisting opponent. 5 attempts per round.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is attempting to establish or maintain a tight closed guard.
    • When your opponent is attempting to create space for an open guard sweep or submission.
    • When your opponent is attempting to shrimp away or create a frame to escape your pressure.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • The "Stall" Counter: If the passer drives their knee too high and gets stuck, the defender can trap the passing leg with their own leg and hip, creating a base to shrimp out or sweep.
    • The "Re-Guard" Counter: As the passer swings their second leg under, the defender can use their hips and legs to create a frame and re-establish guard, often by bringing their knees to their chest.
    • The "Inside Leg Hook" Counter: If the passer's hips are too far away, the defender can hook the passer's nearest leg with their own inner thigh and ankle, creating leverage to sweep or transition to a different guard.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try the Double Under Pass in BJJ?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are attempting to drive your knee *over* your opponent's hip instead of swinging it *under*. This puts shear force on your knee joint, potentially damaging the ligaments. Focus on keeping your hips low and driving your knee forward and across, aiming for it to pass *beneath* their hip bone, then swinging your second leg under.

    Q: How do I perform the Double Under Pass against a much bigger and heavier BJJ opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, prioritize hip pressure and leverage over brute strength. Ensure your grips are tight on their collar and your chest is glued to their hips. Focus on driving your hips *through* their body, using your own body weight as a lever. Do not try to lift them; instead, aim to get your hips lower than theirs and drive forward to collapse their base.

    Q: When is the best time to attempt the Double Under Pass in BJJ, and what are common timing mistakes?

    The ideal time is when your opponent's guard is slightly open or when they are actively trying to create space to shrimp. A common timing mistake is attempting the pass when their guard is fully closed and tight, as you will lack the necessary space to drive your legs through. Another mistake is starting the pass too early before securing your grips, allowing them to react and defend easily.
