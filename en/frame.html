The Frame is a defensive posture primarily used from guard positions to create space and prevent an opponent's forward pressure. It stops the opponent from achieving dominant positions like Side Control or mount.

White belts often fail by using their arms passively or by collapsing their structure, allowing the opponent to bypass their defense. They lack the understanding of how to use their entire body as a lever.

The key mechanical insight is to use your bones (forearm, shin) as rigid levers against the opponent's body, specifically their hips and shoulders, to push them away.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From Closed Guard: Internally rotate your shoulders, place your forearms across the opponent's hips. Your elbows should be bent approximately 90 degrees, creating a rigid angle.
    2. Maintain a slight posterior pelvic tilt, keeping your hips close to the mat to prevent stacking.
    3. Apply pressure by extending your elbows outwards, pushing their hips away from your body. This utilizes your shoulder and elbow joints as hinges.
    4. When an opponent attempts to pass your guard by driving forward, use your shins and knees to create a barrier against their hips and chest.
    5. For an arm frame against a collar grip, place your forearm between their neck and your collarbone, with your elbow bent and locked. Push their head away.
    6. In Open Guard, use your shins across their hips or biceps to create a wedge. Keep your knees slightly bent and your feet flexed.
    7. The goal is to create a structural integrity where your bones act as support beams, preventing their body weight from collapsing your guard.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Attempting to frame with straight, hyperextended elbows: This can lead to ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) tears in the elbow, as the joint is forced beyond its natural range of motion. The correct alternative is to keep a slight bend in the elbow, creating a stable, but not locked, joint.
    • Framing with the wrist instead of the forearm: This puts excessive torque on the wrist joint, potentially causing sprains or even fractures, especially under pressure. The correct alternative is to use the broader, stronger surface of your forearm to contact the opponent.
    • Letting your hips collapse to the mat when framing from guard: This allows the opponent to stack you, putting immense pressure on your spine and potentially leading to disc injuries. The correct alternative is to maintain a slight posterior pelvic tilt and keep your hips elevated.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drilling: Practice extending and retracting your arms and legs to create space, focusing on the feeling of rigidity. 10 reps per limb.
    2. Partner drilling (no resistance): Your partner stands or kneels in front of you. Practice applying the frame to create space, and have them gently push into it. 5 sets of 10 reps.
    3. Partner drilling (light pressure): Partner attempts to close the distance or enter into a pass. You use the frame to maintain space. 25% resistance. 3 rounds of 30 seconds.
    4. Guard retention drills: Partner attempts a specific guard pass (e.g., knee slice). You use the frame to prevent them from completing the pass. 50% resistance. 3 rounds of 45 seconds.
    5. Controlled sparring: Implement the frame as a primary defensive tool during live rolling. Focus on maintaining space and preventing passes. 75% resistance. 5 minutes.
    6. Full live rolling: Use the frame instinctively whenever an opponent applies forward pressure or attempts to pass your guard. 100% resistance. 5 minutes.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is in your guard (closed, open, half) and applying forward pressure.
    • When an opponent attempts to pass your guard by driving their hips into yours.
    • When an opponent tries to establish side control or mount from a dominant position above your guard.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Guard Pass Prevention: When an opponent drives forward, extend your arms (forearms) and legs (shins) outwards to create a rigid barrier, pushing their hips and chest away. Maintain a strong base with your hips slightly elevated.
    • Hip Escape to Create Space: If an opponent is too close, use your frame to create just enough space to hip escape and re-establish a safer guard distance. Push their hips away and quickly shrimp your hips to the side.
    • Transition to a Safer Guard: Use the momentary space created by the frame to transition to a guard that offers better control or is more suited to the opponent's pressure, like butterfly guard if they are close-lining.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my elbow hurt when I try to Frame in BJJ?

    Your elbow is likely hurting because you are hyperextending it, locking it out completely. This puts excessive stress on the ligaments. The correct biomechanical approach is to maintain a slight bend, about 10-15 degrees, in your elbow. This creates a rigid lever using your bones without locking the joint, protecting it from injury while still effectively pushing away.

    Q: How do I Frame against a much bigger opponent in BJJ?

    Against a larger opponent, leverage becomes even more critical. Focus on using your entire body's structure, not just arm strength. Ensure your hips are active and slightly elevated, creating a base. Use your shins and forearms as rigid beams against their hips and chest, and think about pushing them away from your center of gravity, rather than directly against their mass.

    Q: When should I start Framing, before they get too close or when they are already pushing?

    The ideal time to initiate a Frame is preemptively, as soon as you recognize the opponent's intent to close distance or apply forward pressure. Think of it as establishing your defensive perimeter before they enter your personal space. If they are already pushing, your Frame becomes a reactive tool to create space and reset your guard, but prevention is always more effective.
