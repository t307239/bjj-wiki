Reverse De La Riva Guard: A White Belt's Biomechanical Guide

The Reverse De La Riva Guard is an open guard position where you use your leg to control your opponent's leg from the outside, facing away from them.

White belts often struggle by overextending or relying on brute strength, leading to loss of balance and potential injury.

The key is to use your controlling leg as a lever and maintain a tight, connected base with your hips to generate off-balancing forces.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated, open guard position, establish a strong collar grip with your right hand and a sleeve grip (same side) with your left hand. Your opponent is standing in front of you.
    2. Swing your left leg *under* your opponent's right leg, placing your shin across their right ankle joint.
    3. Immediately establish a grip on your opponent's right ankle with your left hand, thumb-up grip, ensuring your forearm is tight against their shin.
    4. With your right leg, hook *outside* your opponent's right thigh, creating a "C" shape with your leg around their limb. This is the "Reverse De La Riva hook".
    5. Drive your hips back and to your left, creating a strong frame with your hooked leg and your controlling ankle grip.
    6. Use your collar and sleeve grips to pull your opponent forward and slightly to your left, initiating their weight transfer.
    7. As they step to maintain balance, drive your right knee forward to create space and prepare to sweep or transition.
    8. Maintain constant tension on the ankle grip and the hook to prevent them from easily extracting their leg.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Gripping the opponent's ankle too loosely: This allows them to easily step out and pass. The ankle joint can be hyperextended or sprained if you lose control of their foot and they apply sudden rotational force.
    • Swinging your hooking leg too wide: This exposes your own leg to attacks like leg drags and can cause your knee or hip to hyperextend if your opponent pushes into it.
    • Trying to pull the opponent over with arm strength alone: This strains your shoulders and elbows and fails to utilize the biomechanical advantage of the guard. Focus on hip movement and weight transfer.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice establishing the collar and sleeve grips, then the ankle grip and Reverse De La Riva hook motion without a partner. (10 reps per side)
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Partner stands, you establish grips and hook. Partner holds static weight. (5 reps per side)
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Partner makes minimal adjustments to maintain base. You practice pulling and hip movement. (25% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    4. Partner drill (medium resistance): Partner actively tries to step out or pressure forward. You focus on maintaining grips and hip connection. (50% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    5. Partner drill (high resistance): Partner attempts to pass. You focus on controlling their leg and off-balancing. (75% resistance, 5 reps per side)
    6. Live rolling: Integrate Reverse De La Riva into sparring, focusing on maintaining the position and attempting sweeps. (100% resistance, 2-minute rounds)

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • Opponent is in a standing or kneeling position directly in front of you.
    • You have established a strong collar and sleeve grip, allowing you to control their upper body posture.
    • Opponent is attempting to pass your guard from a distance, giving you space to set up the leg entanglement.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Leg Extraction: If the opponent tries to step their trapped leg out, immediately shift your hips to your right and pull their ankle towards you, preventing them from completing the step.
    • Knee Slide Pass Defense: If they attempt to slide their knee across your body, use your left sleeve grip to pull their arm and your right leg to push their thigh away, creating space to recompose guard.
    • Posture Control: If they try to posture up, use your collar grip to pull them down and your left leg to keep their trapped leg elevated, preventing them from establishing a strong base.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my ankle hurt when I try to do the Reverse De La Riva guard?

    Your ankle might hurt due to improper grip on the opponent's ankle. Instead of a loose grip, ensure your left hand has a firm, thumb-up grip on their ankle, with your forearm tight against their shin. This prevents hyperextension. Also, ensure your hips are driving back, not pulling forward with your ankle grip, which reduces strain.

    Q: How can I use Reverse De La Riva against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a larger opponent, focus on using your frames and hip movement to create angles. Your collar and sleeve grips are crucial for controlling their posture and pulling them off-balance. Use your Reverse De La Riva hook and ankle grip to disrupt their base, and be ready to transition to a sweep by driving your hips and using their forward momentum against them.

    Q: When should I switch from De La Riva to Reverse De La Riva?

    You generally switch from De La Riva to Reverse De La Riva when your opponent's weight shifts significantly to their *other* leg, or when they attempt to step *around* your De La Riva hook. This shift allows you to redirect your leg over their ankle and establish the Reverse De La Riva entanglement, creating a new angle of attack and control.
