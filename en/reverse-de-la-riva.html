The Reverse De La Riva is an open guard position where you hook your opponent's leg from the inside, facing away from them. It allows for sweeps and transitions to other guards or submissions by attacking the opponent's base and mobility.

White belts often struggle because they lack the proprioception and control to maintain the frame and hip angle. They tend to rely on brute force or flailing limbs, leading to loss of position or injury.

The key mechanical insight is creating a strong, mobile base with your hips and legs, using your non-hooking leg to control distance and your hook to disrupt their balance and mobility.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. Starting from a seated open guard position, with knees bent and feet on the mat.
    2. Choose one leg to be your 'hook' leg. Place the sole of your foot on the inside of your opponent's ankle bone (lateral malleolus).
    3. Your other leg remains free and bent, with the foot positioned to establish a frame against your opponent's hip or thigh.
    4. To initiate the Reverse De La Riva, sit back slightly, shifting your weight onto your hips, not your heels.
    5. Angle your hips approximately 45 degrees away from the opponent's free leg (the one not being hooked).
    6. Grip the opponent's pants near the knee or ankle of the hooked leg with your hand on the same side as the hook.
    7. Use your free arm to establish a frame on the opponent's hip or thigh, pushing away to create space.
    8. Your hooking foot should be actively flexed, with the heel driving towards your opponent's groin, not just passively draped.
    9. Maintain a constant slight tension on the pant grip to prevent them from easily removing your hook.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrect Hooking Leg Placement: Placing the hooking foot too high on the thigh or too low on the shin can allow the opponent to easily step over. This can lead to knee hyperextension or ligament tears (ACL/MCL) if they force weight onto your leg.
    • Overextending the Free Leg: Pushing too hard with your free leg without maintaining hip connection can cause your knee joint to buckle or your hip to become exposed to a sweep or pass.
    • Slouching with Weight on Heels: Sitting back onto your heels with a rounded back removes your base and makes it impossible to generate hip movement or leg power, leading to easy passes or submission attempts.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo Static Hook Drill: Practice placing the hook on an imaginary opponent's ankle, focusing on the angle and foot flexion. 10 reps per leg.
    2. Solo Frame and Hook: With a training partner lightly resisting, practice establishing the hook and the frame with no weight transfer. 10 reps per leg.
    3. Partnered Hook and Frame (0% Resistance): Your partner stands, you sit in Reverse De La Riva. Practice establishing grips and frames without any movement from your partner. 10 reps.
    4. Partnered Hip Angle and Push (25% Resistance): Partner takes a step. You practice shifting your hips and pushing with the frame. 10 reps per leg.
    5. Controlled Sweep Attempt (50% Resistance): Partner provides light resistance. Attempt a basic sweep (e.g., a simple leg sweep by rotating hips). 5 reps per leg.
    6. Live Rolling (75%-100% Resistance): Integrate Reverse De La Riva into sparring, focusing on maintaining position and attempting sweeps or transitions. Aim for 5-10 minutes of rolling time, focusing on the position.

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is postured up and you can establish a quick hook before they can stabilize.
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your guard and you can catch their leg as they step in.
    • When you have an opportunity to transition from a failed sweep or another open guard position.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Knee-Slide Pass: When the white belt's hook is loose, the opponent drives their knee towards the white belt's hip, sliding it past the hooked leg and maintaining pressure.
    • Stand-Up and Drive: The opponent stands up completely, using their weight to break the hook and drive forward, often leading to a pass or a scramble.
    • Leg Peel: The opponent uses their hands to grip the white belt's hooked leg and peels it off, often by stepping their own foot on the outside of the white belt's hip.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do Reverse De La Riva?

    Your knee likely hurts because you are allowing your hooking leg to be passively draped or you are overextending it. When your opponent puts weight on it, your knee joint can hyperextend or twist. To fix this, ensure your hooking foot is actively flexed with the heel driving towards your opponent's groin, and maintain a strong frame with your other leg to control distance and prevent your knee from collapsing.

    Q: How do I use Reverse De La Riva against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, your hip angle and frame become even more critical. Focus on using your frame arm to create significant distance and prevent them from collapsing onto you. Your hooking leg should be used to actively disrupt their base and prevent them from establishing a strong connection. Prioritize sweeps that utilize your hips to generate momentum, rather than trying to lift their weight directly.

    Q: When should I switch from De La Riva to Reverse De La Riva?

    You typically switch from a standard De La Riva to a Reverse De La Riva when your opponent tries to step their inside leg over your De La Riva hook. Instead of letting them pass, you can quickly adjust your hooking leg to the inside of their ankle, creating the Reverse De La Riva position. It's also a good transition if your opponent is too far away for a standard De La Riva sweep and you want to attack their opposite leg.
