Reverse De La Riva Guard: A White Belt's Biomechanical Blueprint

WHITE★★☆☆☆Intermediate
White Belt Technique
📖 See Full Guide → White Belt Guide

Reverse De La Riva is an open guard position where you hook your opponent's leg from the outside, facing away from them. It allows for sweeps and transitions to other guards or submissions.

White belts often struggle because they lack the foundational body awareness and control needed for dynamic leg entanglements. They also tend to overcommit and become static.

The key is maintaining a strong base and using your hips to create angles, allowing you to control distance and pivot effectively while keeping your hooked leg active.

Contents

    Grips & Mechanics

    1. From a seated or kneeling open guard, establish a standard De La Riva hook with your right leg over their left thigh, foot pointed towards their hip.
    2. Simultaneously, your left leg extends to hook *behind* their left ankle or calf from the outside, creating the 'reverse' hook.
    3. Maintain a strong frame with your left forearm on their right hip, and your right hand gripping their left ankle or pant cuff.
    4. Your torso should be angled 45 degrees away from your opponent, creating a stable base with your hips low.
    5. Initiate movement by pushing off your right foot and pivoting your hips towards your left side, drawing their hooked leg towards you.
    6. As they resist, use your right foot (the original De La Riva hook) to block their knee from advancing, and your left foot to keep their ankle controlled.
    7. Shift your weight onto your left hip, using it as a fulcrum to elevate their hooked leg and initiate a sweep or transition.

    ⚠️ White Belt Warnings

    • Incorrectly extending your hooking leg to 'push' their leg away: This hyper-extends the knee and ankle, risking ACL/MCL tears and ankle sprains. The correct movement is to use your hip to pull their leg towards you while your foot maintains a light, controlling hook.
    • Letting your base collapse by bringing your hips too close to your opponent: This compromises your frame and weight distribution, making you vulnerable to passes and risking knee joint compression. Maintain a consistent hip distance and keep your base wide by engaging your glutes and core.
    • Gripping too tightly with your hooking foot and losing control of your base: This can lead to ankle lock vulnerability and a loss of positional control. Focus on a flexible, active hook that allows for hip movement, rather than a rigid grip.

    Drill Progressions

    1. Solo drill: Practice transitioning from a seated position to establishing the Reverse De La Riva hook with both legs, focusing on hip movement and frame placement. (5 reps per side).
    2. Partner drill (no resistance): Opponent stands, you establish Reverse De La Riva. Practice pulling their leg, creating angles, and maintaining your frame. (5 reps per side, 25% resistance).
    3. Partner drill (light resistance): Opponent attempts to step out or pass. You practice holding the hook, creating angles, and blocking their knee with your other foot. (5 reps per side, 50% resistance).
    4. Sweep attempt drill: From Reverse De La Riva, initiate a sweep (e.g., a sweep that uses hip elevation). Opponent offers minimal resistance to the sweep. (5 reps per side, 75% resistance).
    5. Transition drill: From Reverse De La Riva, practice transitioning to a different guard (e.g., Spider Guard) or attempting a sweep and then immediately resetting. (3 reps per side, 80% resistance).
    6. Live rolling: Focus on establishing and maintaining Reverse De La Riva for 30-second intervals, attempting sweeps or passes. (3 rounds x 30 seconds, 90% resistance).

    When to Use & Counters

    • WHEN TO ATTEMPT:
    • When your opponent is attempting to pass your open guard by stepping around your legs.
    • When you are in a seated or kneeling open guard position and have the opportunity to isolate one of their legs.
    • When you want to create dynamic angles and off-balance your opponent for a sweep.
    • PRIMARY COUNTERS:
    • Standing Base: If your opponent attempts to pressure forward, maintain a strong frame with your forearms on their hips and drive your hips away to create space.
    • Leg Entanglement Reversal: If they try to break your hook by stepping over, use your free leg to block their knee and pivot your hips to re-establish control or transition.
    • Hip Escape and Reset: If your base is compromised, immediately perform a hip escape to create distance and reset your guard, avoiding getting stacked or passed.

    Common BJJ Problems & FAQ

    Q: Why does my knee hurt when I try to do Reverse De La Riva?

    Your knee is likely hurting because you are hyperextending it when you try to 'push' their leg away. The Reverse De La Riva hook should be an active, controlling grip, not a lever to push them. Instead, focus on using your hips to pull their leg towards you and keep your hooking leg slightly bent, with your foot flexed to protect the ankle and knee joint.

    Q: How can I effectively use Reverse De La Riva against a much bigger opponent?

    Against a bigger opponent, leverage and angles are crucial. Focus on using your hips to create a strong frame and maintain distance, preventing them from collapsing onto you. Your goal is to use their size against them by off-balancing them with sweeping motions powered by your hip rotation, rather than trying to muscle them.

    Q: When should I switch from De La Riva to Reverse De La Riva?

    You typically switch to Reverse De La Riva when your opponent moves to your outside or attempts to step around your standard De La Riva hook. If they are directly in front of you, a standard De La Riva is usually more effective. The transition happens when their leg is accessible from the outside, allowing you to hook behind it.
